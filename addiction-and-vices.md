# Addiction and Vices

Every person carries appetites that would happily run the whole operation. The drink that becomes the evening. The phone you pick up before you've decided to. The bet, the scroll, the vape, the tab you close and reopen. None of this makes you weak. It makes you a human animal living in an environment that very smart people have engineered to capture exactly those appetites.

This file is about keeping appetites in service rather than in command. It is not puritanism. Pleasure belongs in a good life. The question is never "is this enjoyable?" The question is "who is choosing: me, or the loop?"

This is general education, not medical advice. If you are physically dependent on anything, read the next section first and act on it.

---

## Safety Lines (Read First)

**Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can kill.** If you have been drinking heavily and daily for a long period, or taking benzodiazepines (such as Xanax, Valium, Klonopin or Ativan) long-term, stopping abruptly can cause seizures, delirium tremens and death. Do not white-knuckle it alone. Talk to a doctor before you stop, and taper or detox under medical supervision.

**Opioid tolerance drops fast.** After days or weeks without using, a dose you used to handle can be fatal. Street drugs may contain fentanyl. Never use alone, and keep naloxone (Narcan) on hand; it is sold over the counter in the US.

**Who to call (US):**
- **SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357** (1-800-662-HELP). Free, confidential, 24/7, 365 days a year. Treatment referrals and information in English and Spanish.
- **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline:** call or text 988 if you are in crisis or thinking about suicide.
- **Emergency:** 911.
- Smoking: 1-800-QUIT-NOW. Gambling: 1-800-GAMBLER.

Outside the US, find your national equivalents now, before you need them.

## Habit, Compulsion, Addiction

Three different things that get blurred together.

**Habit:** an automatic behavior triggered by a cue. Neutral. Add a little friction and it stops without much distress.

**Compulsion:** you feel driven to do it, often to relieve tension, and you do it even when part of you would rather not. The pleasure fades; the relief remains.

**Addiction:** compulsion that persists despite clear, mounting harm, usually with loss of control and often with tolerance and withdrawal. Clinically, the DSM-5 diagnoses substance use disorder on eleven criteria, graded mild, moderate or severe by how many apply. Gambling disorder sits in the same chapter. The WHO's ICD-11 adds gaming disorder, and classifies compulsive sexual behaviour disorder as an impulse-control disorder.

The line is not the activity. It is your relationship to it. The same glass of wine can be a ritual for one person and a symptom for another.

**The core test:** can you stop when you decide to, and does stopping cost you more than mild annoyance?

## What Drives Compulsion

**1. Wanting, not liking.** Dopamine is routinely called the pleasure chemical. That is misleading. Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson's incentive-salience research separates *liking* (the pleasure itself) from *wanting* (the pull toward it). Dopamine systems are central to wanting, motivation and learning what predicts reward (see Wolfram Schultz's reward-prediction-error work). With repetition, cues for a reward can trigger intense wanting even while the liking stays flat or fades. That is why the third hour of scrolling isn't fun and you keep going anyway. The craving outruns the enjoyment.

**2. Relief.** Many vices are less about pleasure than about switching something off: anxiety, boredom, loneliness, shame, physical pain. Gabor Maté's reframe, to ask what pain the addiction is solving rather than why the addiction, is useful even if you don't accept the strong claim that all addiction is trauma. Childhood adversity does raise risk; the ACE research by Vincent Felitti and Robert Anda found a dose-response link with later substance problems.

**3. Availability and cues.** Access predicts use. Lee Robins studied US soldiers returning from Vietnam: many had used heroin there, yet most did not resume addiction once home. The drug was the same. The environment changed. Places, people, times of day and moods fire the wanting before you think.

**4. Engineered products.** Slot machines, betting apps, infinite feeds, loot boxes and flavored vapes are built by teams whose metrics are your time and money. Their tools: variable rewards (the variable-ratio schedules B.F. Skinner showed are the hardest to extinguish), near-misses, losses disguised as wins, autoplay, streaks, push notifications. Natasha Dow Schüll's *Addiction by Design* documents how machine gambling is tuned to keep players in what she calls the machine zone. You are not just fighting your own weakness. You are fighting an industry. Plan accordingly.

**5. Vulnerability.** NIDA estimates genetics account for roughly 40 to 60 percent of addiction risk. Early onset, depression, anxiety, ADHD, PTSD, chronic stress and isolation all add to it. Knowing your loading tells you how much margin you have.

## The Common Vices: Costs and Honest Debates

**Alcohol.** The cost: alcohol is classified by the WHO's cancer agency (IARC) as a Group 1 carcinogen, linked to cancers of the mouth, throat, esophagus, liver, bowel and breast. It wrecks sleep quality ([health/sleep.md](health/sleep.md)), raises blood pressure, and fuels accidents, violence and decisions made at 1 a.m. The debate: older observational studies suggested moderate drinkers outlived abstainers. Later analyses argue much of that came from a flawed comparison group, since "non-drinkers" often included people who had quit because they were already sick. In January 2023 the WHO stated in *The Lancet Public Health* that no level of alcohol consumption is safe for health. Experts still argue about how large the risk is at low intakes, but the direction is clear: less is safer. Treat a drink as a small risk you choose for the pleasure, not as a health habit.

**Nicotine and vaping.** Nicotine is highly addictive. The WHO estimates tobacco kills up to half of long-term users who don't quit. UK health authorities describe vaping as substantially less harmful than smoking for adults who switch completely, and Cochrane reviews have found nicotine e-cigarettes help some smokers quit better than nicotine replacement. But vaping is not harmless, long-term effects are unknown, and high-nicotine products can drive dependence hard. The honest position: if you don't use nicotine, don't start. If you smoke, switching completely beats smoking. Quitting everything beats switching.

**Porn.** The costs people report: time, attention, secrecy in relationships, distorted expectations, escalation. The debate: "porn addiction" is not an official diagnosis, though ICD-11's compulsive sexual behaviour disorder covers loss of control. Joshua Grubbs and colleagues found that feeling addicted is strongly predicted by moral disapproval of porn, not just by how much someone uses ("moral incongruence"). Claims that porn commonly causes erectile problems in young men are contested. Use the same test as for anything: control, harm, secrecy. See [sexuality.md](sexuality.md).

**Gambling and sports betting.** The cost: debt, lying, broken relationships, and elevated suicide risk in gambling disorder. Since the US Supreme Court struck down the federal ban in 2018 (*Murphy v. NCAA*), legal sports betting has spread across many states and now lives in your pocket around the clock, with in-game micro-bets, parlays, "boosts" and deposit bonuses. The house edge is built in; over time the math wins. Rules if you bet at all: only money already written off as entertainment, hard deposit limits, no in-game betting, never chase losses. If you have ever chased, lied about it or borrowed to gamble, that is the signal. Most legal markets offer self-exclusion.

**Phones, social media, gaming.** The cost: fragmented attention, lost sleep, comparison, hours that vanish. The debate: Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (2024) argues smartphones and social media drove a rise in teen anxiety and depression; researchers including Candice Odgers and Amy Orben argue the evidence is largely correlational and average effects are small. For you as an adult, your own data matters more than the debate: what does your screen-time report say, and how do you feel after? Gaming disorder is real, and most gamers don't have it. See [attention.md](attention.md).

**Cannabis.** The cost: cannabis use disorder is real, with withdrawal (irritability, poor sleep, low appetite, cravings). Today's products are far more potent than those of past decades. Frequent use of high-potency cannabis, especially starting in adolescence, is associated with higher psychosis risk. Heavy users can develop cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (cycles of severe vomiting). It suppresses REM sleep. The debate: it has medical uses, fatal overdose from cannabis alone is very rare, and it is legal in many places. Legal does not mean harmless, and "it's natural, it's not addictive" is simply wrong.

## Warning Signs

- **Loss of control:** more or longer than you intended; failed attempts to cut back.
- **Continued use despite harm:** to health, work, money or relationships.
- **Tolerance:** you need more for the same effect.
- **Withdrawal:** irritability, anxiety, poor sleep or physical symptoms when you stop.
- **Hiding it:** lying about amounts, stashes, deleted histories, defending it too hard.
- **Narrowing:** other interests fade; you plan your time around it.
- **Needing it to function:** to relax, sleep, socialize or feel normal.
- **Other people are worried.**

One sign is a flag. Several is a pattern. The two that matter most are loss of control and continued use despite harm.

## The Honest Self-Audit

Do this privately, on paper, once a year during your [annual review](routines/annual-review.md), and any time you catch yourself getting defensive.

For each candidate (alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, porn, gambling, phone and social media, gaming, food, shopping, even work):

1. **How much, how often?** Use data, not memory: screen-time reports, bank statements, a two-week log.
2. **When did I last go 30 days without it?** Could I start tomorrow without dread?
3. **What does it do for me?** Relax, numb, connect, escape, reward, sleep.
4. **What does it cost me?** Money, sleep, health, attention, relationships, self-respect.
5. **Do I hide or minimize it?**
6. **Is it trending up compared with a year ago?**
7. **If my closest friend described this exact pattern, what would I tell them?**

For alcohol, also take the WHO's AUDIT (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test), a free ten-question screen.

**The 30-day test:** abstain for 30 days. If it's easy, you've learned something. If it's hard, you've learned something more important. (Not for anyone physically dependent on alcohol or benzodiazepines. See Safety Lines.)

## Strategies That Work

1. **Environment and friction.** Make the vice harder and the alternative easier. Don't keep it in the house. Delete the apps, log out, install blockers, charge the phone outside the bedroom, remove saved cards, self-exclude. Willpower is your weakest tool; design is your strongest. Full method: [reality-engineering.md](reality-engineering.md).
2. **Map your cues.** Log every urge for two weeks: time, place, people, mood. Watch for HALT (hungry, angry, lonely, tired). Then pre-decide: "If it's 9 p.m. and I'm restless, I walk, shower or call someone."
3. **Urge surfing.** From Alan Marlatt's relapse-prevention work: when an urge hits, neither fight it nor feed it. Locate it in your body, rate it 1 to 10, breathe, and watch it rise, crest and fall like a wave. Every urge you ride out without acting weakens the link.
4. **Replace the need.** Every vice is a bad solution to a real need. Name the need and meet it better: stress with training or breathwork, boredom with a project, loneliness with people, the end-of-day reward with a new ritual (sauna, walk, a good non-alcoholic drink). Remove without replacing and the vacuum refills.
5. **Accountability.** Tell someone: a friend, partner, sponsor, coach or group. Secrecy is the vice's oxygen.
6. **Tracking.** Log every instance. Counting changes behavior on its own. Watch the trend, not just the streak.
7. **Delay.** "Not now. In twenty minutes." Many urges don't survive the wait.
8. **Bright lines over fuzzy goals.** "Weekends only, two maximum, never alone" beats "drink less." Pre-commit in writing.
9. **Shore up the base.** Sleep, training, food, sunlight, people. A depleted person is a vulnerable one.

## Abstinence vs. Moderation

There is no universal answer. It depends on you, the substance and your history.

**Choose abstinence when:** you have lost control before, you have tried to moderate and failed repeatedly, dependence is moderate or severe, the substance is high-risk (opioids, stimulants, nicotine), there is heavy family history, or the vice is already wrecking something important. For many people zero is easier than one, because there is nothing to negotiate every day.

**Moderation can work when:** the problem is mild and early, and you will follow hard rules and track them. Gretchen Rubin's popular split between "abstainers" and "moderators" is a framing, not science, but it helps you know yourself.

**The test:** set specific moderation rules and track them for 90 days. If you break them more than once or twice, the answer is abstinence.

**Harm reduction counts.** Never using alone, carrying naloxone, switching from cigarettes to a less harmful product: these are legitimate steps, not failures.

## Professional Help and Evidence-Based Treatment

Addiction is treatable, and recovery is common. Asking for help is a strategic move, not a surrender.

- **Start with a doctor:** primary care or an addiction medicine specialist. Be honest. They have heard worse.
- **Medications for alcohol use disorder:** naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram are FDA-approved and rarely prescribed. Ask about them.
- **Medications for opioid use disorder:** methadone, buprenorphine and extended-release naltrexone. Methadone and buprenorphine substantially cut the risk of overdose death. They are the standard of care, not "swapping one drug for another."
- **Nicotine:** combination nicotine replacement, varenicline or bupropion, ideally with quitline support.
- **Therapies with good evidence:** cognitive behavioral therapy, motivational interviewing (William Miller and Stephen Rollnick), contingency management (tangible rewards for verified abstinence, especially strong for stimulants), and treating co-occurring depression, anxiety, ADHD or PTSD.
- **Mutual-help groups:** Alcoholics Anonymous and other twelve-step groups (NA, GA). A 2020 Cochrane review by John Kelly, Keith Humphreys and Marica Ferri found AA and twelve-step facilitation did as well as other established treatments, and better for continuous abstinence. SMART Recovery is a secular, CBT-based alternative. Try several meetings before you judge.
- **Levels of care:** outpatient, intensive outpatient, residential, and medically supervised detox where withdrawal is dangerous.
- **Finding care in the US:** the SAMHSA Helpline (1-800-662-4357) and FindTreatment.gov.

## Relapse Is Part of Recovery, Not the End

NIDA puts relapse rates for substance use disorders at roughly 40 to 60 percent, comparable to other chronic conditions such as hypertension and asthma. Relapse doesn't mean treatment failed. It means the plan needs adjusting.

- **A lapse is not a relapse.** One slip is data. What turns a slip into a slide is what Marlatt called the abstinence violation effect: "I've blown it, so I might as well keep going." Kill that thought on sight.
- **After a lapse:** stop, tell someone within 24 hours, and debrief ([failure.md](failure.md)). What cue? What state? What was missing from the plan? Fix one thing and resume.
- **The danger window:** after time away from opioids, tolerance is lower. A return to an old dose is how many overdoses happen.
- **Measure the trend.** Ten good months and one bad week is a winning record.

## Helping Someone Else

1. **Skip the TV-style ambush.** The evidence favors CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training, developed by Robert Meyers and colleagues): reward sober behavior, let natural consequences land, communicate without contempt, and invite treatment at moments of openness.
2. **Talk when they're sober and calm, in private.** Specifics, not verdicts: "I've noticed X. I'm worried. I'll help you find help."
3. **Don't cover, lie for them, fund the habit or clean up every consequence.**
4. **If opioids are involved,** keep naloxone, learn the signs of overdose and call 911.
5. **Hold your boundaries.** You cannot control their choices; you can control what you tolerate. See [boundaries.md](boundaries.md).
6. **Get your own support:** Al-Anon, Nar-Anon, SMART Recovery Family & Friends, or a therapist.
7. **Keep hope realistic.** People get better, often after several attempts.
