# Stoic Practice

Not philosophy as theory. Philosophy as daily operating system. Marcus Aurelius ran the Roman Empire using these tools. They work.

---

## The Core Architecture

**The Dichotomy of Control** — Epictetus's foundation, everything else builds on it.

Divide the universe into two columns:

| Up to me | Not up to me |
|----------|--------------|
| My judgments | My reputation |
| My desires | My body's health |
| My actions | Outcomes |
| My response to events | What others think |
| My effort | Luck, timing, fate |

Work the left column with full force. Release the right column completely. Most suffering comes from attempting to control the right column. Most power comes from mastering the left.

---

## Morning Practice — Premeditatio Malorum

*Pre-meditate the worst.* Before the day begins, briefly visualize what could go wrong. Not to catastrophize — to inoculate.

1. What difficulty might I face today?
2. How will I respond if it happens?
3. What is within my control in that scenario?

This practice does two things: it removes the shock of adversity (you've already rehearsed it) and it reveals that most feared things are survivable. The Stoics called this *negative visualization*. It is not pessimism — it is preparation.

*"Rehearse death. To say this is to rehearse freedom. A person who has learned how to die has unlearned how to be a slave."* — Seneca

---

## The Daily Operating Filter

Before reacting to any event — good or bad — run it through:

**"Is this up to me?"**

- If yes → engage fully, take responsibility, act
- If no → release it, respond without being consumed

This is not passivity. It is the most radical form of agency: choosing your response to every event rather than being dragged by it.

## Amor Fati — Love Your Fate

Not acceptance. Not tolerance. *Love.*

Whatever happens — the obstacle, the loss, the failed plan, the betrayal — treat it as the exact material needed for what comes next. Nietzsche called it *amor fati*. The Stoics lived it.

The practice: when something goes wrong, ask — *how is this useful?* Not "why me?" Not even "how do I fix this?" — *how is this the right material to work with?*

*"Not merely bear what is necessary, still less conceal it... but love it."* — Nietzsche

---

## Evening Review — Marcus Aurelius's Method

End each day with three questions (write the answers, don't just think them):

1. **What did I do wrong?** — not self-flagellation, honest assessment
2. **What did I do well?** — reinforce what worked
3. **What was left undone?** — carry it forward with intention, not guilt

The Stoics called this *the evening examination*. It closes the day cleanly and prevents the unconscious accumulation of unprocessed experience.

---

## Memento Mori — Remember Death

Death is not the enemy. Forgetting it is.

The practice: once a week, spend 60 seconds genuinely contemplating that this day could be your last. Not morbidly — clearly. Ask:
- Am I living in a way I'd stand behind?
- What would I regret leaving undone?
- Who matters to me that I haven't told?

Used by Roman generals who had a slave stand behind them during triumph parades, whispering *"memento mori"* — remember you will die — to prevent ego inflation and keep perspective.

*"You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do and say and think."* — Marcus Aurelius

---

## The View from Above

When overwhelmed, trapped in ego, or consumed by minor grievance — zoom out.

Visualize yourself from above:
- Your body in the room → your city → your country → the planet → the solar system → the galaxy → the scale of cosmic time

Your problem is real. It is also invisible from 10,000 feet and will be unknown in 200 years. This is not nihilism. It is proportion. Act fully, but hold loosely.

---

## Stoic Rules for Daily Life

- **React slowly.** Between stimulus and response, insert a pause. The pause is where freedom lives.
- **Seek discomfort on purpose.** Voluntary hardship prevents the horror of involuntary hardship. Fast occasionally. Sleep on the floor. Wear less. The practice shows you need less than you think.
- **Judge yourself, not others.** You have no access to their interior. You have full access to yours. Focus there.
- **The present is all you have.** The past is fixed. The future is uncertain. The only place to act is now.
- **Be useful.** Marcus Aurelius asked himself every morning: *"What am I still needed for?"* That question is the antidote to self-pity, ego, and drift.
