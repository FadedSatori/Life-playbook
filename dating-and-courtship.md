# Dating and Courtship

For most men, the most consequential decision of their lives isn't a career or an investment. It's who they build a life with. Yet most approach it with less preparation than buying a car: drifting, swiping, and hoping.

This file is about finding a partner with integrity: becoming someone worth choosing, getting clear on what you want, meeting people, connecting honestly, reading interest accurately, and choosing well. The goal is a real relationship with a real person, not a scoreboard.

The standard throughout is respect and honesty. Not only because it's right, though it is, but because it works. Tactics built on manipulation attract people who respond to manipulation, and they build a relationship on a false self you then have to keep performing.

---

## Become Someone Worth Choosing

Not "fix everything first." Waiting until you're perfect is avoidance with a better excuse. Build these in parallel with dating.

- **Health.** Train, sleep, groom, wear clothes that fit. The point isn't to look like a model. It's to show you take care of yourself. See [health/fitness.md](health/fitness.md).
- **Purpose.** A man going somewhere. Partners want to join a life, not become someone's entire life. See [purpose.md](purpose.md).
- **Social life.** Friends, community, things you do. A man with no friends puts impossible weight on a partner, and friends are also how you meet people. See [friendship.md](friendship.md).
- **Emotional steadiness.** Can you handle frustration, rejection, and conflict without collapsing or lashing out?
- **Stable finances.** Not rich. Stable and honest about where you stand.
- **Done with the last one.** If you're still raw from a breakup, you'll choose someone to anesthetize it. See [grief-and-loss.md](grief-and-loss.md).

## Know What You Want

**Values.** Children (yes, no, when), faith, money, ambition, where to live, how you want to spend ordinary days.

**Non-negotiables.** Keep the list short: three to five. Real dealbreakers are about values and character (wants kids or doesn't, honesty, substance abuse), not surface preferences like height or a particular job.

**The partnership.** What kind of relationship do you want? How you'll divide roles, how much togetherness, monogamy, pace.

**Hold your checklist loosely.** In speed-dating research, Paul Eastwick and Eli Finkel found that people's stated ideal-partner preferences did a poor job of predicting whom they were actually drawn to in person. Know your values cold. Be open on the rest.

**Exercise:** Write one paragraph describing the life you want in ten years, and one describing how a partner fits into it. If you can't write it, you aren't ready to recognize it.

## Where People Meet

Michael Rosenfeld, Reuben Thomas, and Sonia Hausen (*PNAS*, 2019) analyzed a nationally representative US survey and found that meeting online had overtaken meeting through friends as the most common way heterosexual couples met, by the mid-2010s. Among couples who met in 2017, roughly 39% met online, while meeting through friends had fallen to about 20%. "Online" included more than dating apps, and it was a plurality, not a majority.

**What that means in practice:**
- **Online works, but it's a funnel, not a destination.** Recent, clear photos. A short, honest profile that shows your actual life. Move toward a real meeting reasonably soon instead of becoming a pen pal.
- **Friends and social circles still matter.** Roughly one couple in five, and they come pre-vetted. Tell good friends you're looking.
- **Shared-activity settings.** Clubs, classes, leagues, faith communities, volunteering, run clubs. Repeated contact in a shared context lets familiarity and attraction build naturally.
- **Work is possible but risky.** Know the policy, and never date someone you supervise.
- **Everyday life.** Approaching someone in person is fine when it's respectful and the context allows it.

## Approaching and Handling Rejection

Approaching takes courage, and courage is trained by exposure. See [courage.md](courage.md).

- **Read the context.** Headphones in, working, mid-set at the gym, stuck on a train they can't leave: leave them alone. Approach where conversation is normal and the other person can easily exit.
- **Be direct.** "Hi, I noticed you and wanted to say hello. I'm [name]." No lines, no routines.
- **Make the exit easy.** Short answers, body turned away, "I'm seeing someone": say "No problem, have a good day," and go.
- **Ask specifically.** "I'd like to take you for a drink Thursday. Are you free?" beats "We should hang out sometime."
- **Treat rejection as information about fit, not a verdict on your worth.** They don't know you yet. Thank them, leave, don't argue, don't ask again, and never insult or retaliate.
- **Volume is a sign you're taking shots.** Count your attempts, not just your results.

## Conversation That Builds Connection

- **Be curious.** Karen Huang, Alison Wood Brooks, and colleagues (2017) found that people who asked more questions, especially follow-up questions, were better liked by conversation partners. Their speed-dating data pointed the same way, though a later reanalysis disputed that part.
- **Follow the thread.** Notice where their energy rises and ask about that. "What got you into that?" "What was the hardest part?"
- **Disclose gradually and reciprocally.** In Arthur Aron's 1997 "36 questions" study, pairs of strangers who took turns with increasingly personal questions reported feeling closer than pairs making small talk. It built closeness in a lab, not guaranteed love, but the principle holds: match depth, step by step.
- **Be responsive.** Harry Reis's work on responsiveness suggests intimacy grows when people feel understood, validated, and cared for. Show you heard what they said.
- **Be yourself, specifically.** Opinions, stories, humor. Agreeing with everything is boring and dishonest. See [humor-and-play.md](humor-and-play.md).
- **Avoid:** interview mode, monologues, complaining about exes, the phone on the table.

## Consent and Respect: Non-Negotiable

- **Consent is a clear, freely given, ongoing yes,** and it can be withdrawn at any moment. Silence is not yes. Someone who is drunk or high may be unable to consent at all.
- **Ask.** "Can I kiss you?" is not weak. It's clear. Keep checking in as things progress.
- **Pressure is not seduction.** Persistence after a no, guilt, sulking, or getting someone drunk is coercion.
- **Respect their safety.** They may be meeting a stranger. Meet in public, let them get themselves there and home, don't push for an address or a ride. Their precautions aren't personal.

See [sexuality.md](sexuality.md) on consent, power, and ethics.

## Reading Interest Honestly, and Taking No

**Interest is a pattern, not a signal.** They sometimes initiate contact, ask questions back, make plans or suggest alternatives, follow through, and seem at ease with you.

**Disinterest is a pattern too.** Short replies, no initiating, cancelling without rescheduling, vague "maybes."

- **A cancellation without an alternative is often a soft no.** Ask once more at most. Then the ball is in their court.
- **Don't mistake friendliness for attraction.** Research going back to Antonia Abbey's studies in the early 1980s, and later Martie Haselton and David Buss's error management theory, finds men on average tend to overestimate women's sexual interest. Correct for the bias.
- **When unsure, ask directly and warmly.** "I like you and I'd like to see you again. Are you interested?" Accept whatever answer comes.
- **No means no. Maybe usually means no or not yet. Only yes means yes.**

## Red Flags and Green Flags

**Red flags** (see [dark-psychology.md](dark-psychology.md)):
- **Love bombing.** Overwhelming attention, flattery, gifts, "soulmate" talk, and plans for the future within weeks. Intensity is not intimacy.
- **Inconsistency.** Hot then cold; words and actions don't match. Intermittent reinforcement bonds people hard to the wrong person. See [counter-strategies.md](counter-strategies.md).
- **Contempt.** Mockery, eye-rolling, sneering at you or others. John Gottman considers contempt the most damaging pattern he observed in couples. His famous divorce-prediction figures are debated, but contempt is corrosive regardless.
- **How they treat people with no power over them.** Servers, strangers, their family. And if every ex was "crazy," ask what the common factor was.
- **Testing your boundaries early,** pushing past a no to see if it holds.
- **Jealousy framed as love,** or pressure to drop your friends.
- **Rushing** sex, moving in, or commitment.

**Green flags:**
- They do what they say they'll do.
- They respect your no and your time.
- They're curious about you and remember details.
- They handle small disappointments (a cancelled plan, a disagreement) with maturity.
- They have friends, a life of their own, and a reasonably settled relationship with their past.
- They can apologize. See [forgiveness-and-apology.md](forgiveness-and-apology.md).
- They're kind to people who can do nothing for them.
- Around them you feel calmer and more yourself, not anxious and performing.

Run the same lists on yourself.

## Pacing and the Exclusivity Conversation

- **Let it build.** Early infatuation (what Dorothy Tennov called limerence) distorts judgment. Big moves like moving in, meeting your kids, or merging money wait until you've seen each other through stress, conflict, and ordinary weeks. See [relationships/intimate-partnership.md](relationships/intimate-partnership.md).
- **Don't play timing games.** Waiting days to reply to seem aloof is manipulation-lite. Reply when you want to; just don't flood.
- **Sexual pacing is mutual.** Nobody owes anyone a timeline.
- **Have the exclusivity talk out loud.** Don't assume. When you know you want to see only them: "I'm not seeing anyone else, and I don't want to. I'd like us to be exclusive. How do you feel about that?"
- **Accept the answer.** If they aren't there yet, decide honestly whether you can wait without resentment, and for how long.

## Compatibility That Predicts Long-Term Success

- **Shared values and direction.** Kids, money, faith, place. Love does not reconcile a yes and a no on children.
- **How you each handle conflict.** You need to see a real disagreement before deep commitment. Do they repair? Do you?
- **Kindness.** In David Buss's study of mate preferences across 37 cultures, both sexes ranked kindness and intelligence near the top.
- **Friendship and respect.** Do you like them as a person, apart from the attraction?
- **What you build matters more than the checklist.** Samantha Joel and colleagues (*PNAS*, 2020) pooled 43 longitudinal couples studies. Features of the relationship itself, like perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, and conflict, predicted relationship quality far better than either partner's individual traits. Choose well, then show up well.

Chemistry is necessary. It is not sufficient.

## Why Pickup Tactics Fail

Manipulation-based "game" includes negging (backhanded insults meant to lower someone's confidence), scripted routines, fake time limits, push-pull, isolating someone from their friends, and "overcoming resistance."

1. **They treat a person as an obstacle.** That habit doesn't stay in the bar; it changes how you see everyone.
2. **They select for the wrong partner.** Tactics that exploit insecurity work best on insecure people.
3. **They build on a false self.** The relationship is with your persona, and it collapses when you stop performing.
4. **They shade into coercion.** "Overcoming resistance" means pushing past a no. That is wrong, and it can be criminal.
5. **They make you worse.** You learn to see connection as a game, rejection as an enemy, and your worth as a number. See [sexuality.md](sexuality.md) on separating desire from validation.
6. **They can't deliver what you actually want:** someone who chose the real you.

Keep what's honest from that world: approaching, fitness, style, social skill. Drop the rest. See [masculinity.md](masculinity.md).

## Protocol: The First 90 Days

**Days 1–30: Discovery**
1. Date with intention: real plans, clear asks, short first dates (coffee, a drink, a walk).
2. Ask good questions and share honestly. No performance.
3. Watch how they treat others, keep plans, and handle small letdowns.
4. Keep your own life running: training, friends, work. Don't vanish into someone new.
5. After each date, write two lines: How did I feel, calmer or anxious? What did I learn? See [decision-journal.md](decision-journal.md).

**Days 31–60: Depth**
6. Let the big topics come up naturally: kids, money, faith, ambition, past relationships. Talk, don't interrogate.
7. Meet some of each other's friends.
8. See at least one stressful situation or minor disagreement, and how both of you handle it.
9. Have the exclusivity conversation if you want it.

**Days 61–90: Decision**
10. Review against your non-negotiables and the flag lists. Ask a trusted friend what they see.
11. Ask: Do I respect how they handle difficulty? Do I like who I am around them?
12. Decide: commit, keep going with eyes open, or end it kindly and directly. "I've enjoyed getting to know you, and I don't see this going where I want it to. I wanted to tell you directly." After more than a date or two, don't ghost.

Past 90 days, the work moves to [relationships/intimate-partnership.md](relationships/intimate-partnership.md).
