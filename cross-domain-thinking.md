# Cross-Domain Thinking

Universal laws appear in every field simultaneously. Learn them once in one domain and you can read any other domain faster. This is Munger's latticework in practice — the cognitive architecture of the polymath.

---

## Munger's Latticework

Charlie Munger's core insight: a single mental model is a tool. A hundred mental models from a hundred different domains, held simultaneously and connected, is a weapon.

*"You've got to have models in your head. And you've got to array your experience — both vicarious and direct — on this latticework of models. You may have noticed students who just try to remember and pound back what is remembered. Well, they fail in school and in life."*

The goal is not to memorize frameworks. It is to internalize universal principles so deeply that when new information arrives, it automatically hooks into multiple models at once — producing insight that no single-domain thinker can reach.

What follows are the most cross-transferable principles in the latticework. Each one appears in at least five unrelated fields. Learn them in the domain where they're most intuitive; deploy them everywhere.

---

## Power Laws

**The principle:** A small number of inputs produce the majority of outputs. The distribution is not bell-curved — it is heavily skewed. A few items account for most of the effect.

**Where it appears:**
- Economics: 20% of customers → 80% of revenue (Pareto)
- Physics: earthquake magnitudes, solar flare intensities, city sizes
- Biology: a few species dominate most ecosystems
- Social networks: a few nodes have most of the connections
- Your own life: a few relationships, habits, and decisions produce most of your results

**How to use it:**
- Before optimizing anything, find the 20%. Ignore the 80% until the 20% is maxed.
- Don't spread effort evenly. Concentrate it where the return is disproportionate.
- In any system, ask: what is the single input with the highest leverage? Start there.

## Feedback Loops

**The principle:** Systems loop back on themselves. Outputs become inputs. The behavior of a system is determined more by its feedback structure than by its initial conditions.

**Positive feedback loops** (amplifying):
- Rich get richer. Network effects. Compounding interest. Viral spread. Reputation snowballing.
- Left unchecked, positive loops produce runaway growth or collapse.

**Negative feedback loops** (stabilizing):
- Thermostats. Predator-prey cycles. Market price discovery. Homeostasis in the body.
- These maintain equilibrium. Remove them and the system oscillates wildly.

**Where it appears:**
- Biology: the immune system, hormonal regulation, circadian rhythms
- Economics: interest rates, inflation targeting, market corrections
- Psychology: anxiety feedback loops, confidence spirals, addiction cycles
- Relationships: contempt breeds distance breeds more contempt

**How to use it:**
- Before intervening in any system, identify the feedback structure. Adding more input to a negative feedback loop can make things worse.
- Build positive feedback loops into your habits deliberately. Small wins that create energy for the next win.
- Identify and interrupt negative loops early — they're easier to break before they become self-sustaining.

## Emergence

**The principle:** Complex, novel behavior arises from the interaction of simple rules. The whole is qualitatively different from — and irreducible to — the sum of its parts.

**Where it appears:**
- Biology: consciousness from neurons; life from chemistry; behavior from genes + environment
- Physics: superconductivity, phase transitions, turbulence
- Economics: markets from individual transactions; bubbles from rational local behavior
- Sociology: culture from individual choices; trends from independent decisions
- Organizations: corporate culture from daily interactions

**How to use it:**
- Don't try to predict or control emergent phenomena at the level of their components. Manage the conditions that give rise to them.
- In building teams or systems: set the rules and the environment, then let emergent behavior develop. Micromanagement destroys emergence.
- When a system produces unexpected behavior, don't ask "who did this?" Ask "what interaction rules produced this?"

## Network Effects

**The principle:** The value of a network grows with the number of nodes. Each new member makes the network more valuable for all existing members.

**Where it appears:**
- Technology: telephone, internet, social media, language itself
- Biology: neural networks (more connections = more capability), immune system memory
- Social capital: the person with 500 high-trust connections is not 5x as connected as the person with 100 — they're exponentially more connected
- Ideas: a meme spreads faster in a denser network of believers
- Markets: more buyers and sellers = better price discovery = more participants

**How to use it:**
- Enter networks early. The first 1,000 nodes of a valuable network are worth more, proportionally, than the millionth.
- In relationships: depth compounds, but breadth creates optionality. A wide network of weak ties surfaces opportunities that close networks never see.
- Your ideas spread faster in proportion to your network's density and trust level. Build the network before you need it.

## Entropy

**The principle:** Closed systems naturally move toward disorder. Order requires continuous energy input. Maintenance is not optional — it is the price of the current state.

**Where it appears:**
- Physics: the second law of thermodynamics
- Biology: cellular decay, aging, ecological succession
- Organizations: bureaucracy creeps, culture erodes, processes bloat
- Relationships: connection fades without investment; resentment accumulates without clearing
- Skills: unused skills atrophy; unused languages fade; unused muscles weaken
- Information: files accumulate, inboxes fill, codebases rot

**How to use it:**
- Everything you want to maintain requires active investment. There is no steady state — only growth or decay.
- Schedule maintenance before you need to repair. The cost of maintenance is always lower than the cost of restoration.
- The question is not "do I need to tend to this?" but "at what rate is this decaying, and what is the minimum input to reverse it?"

## Game Theory

**The principle:** Rational behavior depends on what other rational actors will do. Strategy is not optimizing in isolation — it is anticipating and responding to other agents.

**Core concepts:**
- **Prisoner's Dilemma:** Rational self-interest produces collectively bad outcomes. Cooperation requires either communication, trust, or repeated games.
- **Repeated games:** In a one-shot game, defection is rational. In repeated games, cooperation becomes the dominant strategy because reputation matters.
- **Nash Equilibrium:** The stable state where no player can improve their outcome by changing strategy alone. Doesn't mean it's optimal — just stable.
- **Schelling Points:** Coordination solutions that emerge without communication, based on salience. People converge on them because they expect others to.

**Where it appears:**
- Economics: pricing strategy, auctions, labor negotiations
- Biology: evolutionary arms races, animal displays, predator-prey behavior
- Politics: nuclear deterrence, international treaties, voting systems
- Relationships: who calls first after a fight; splitting costs; commitment signals
- Business: competitive pricing, platform strategy, hiring

**How to use it:**
- In any competitive situation, model the other player's incentives before deciding your own move.
- Repeated game thinking: behave as if every interaction will be repeated, because reputation travels further than you think.
- Find the Schelling Points in negotiations — the obvious focal points both sides will converge on. Anchor there.

## Inversion

**The principle:** Many problems are solved more easily backward than forward. Instead of asking "how do I achieve X?" ask "what would guarantee I fail at X?" Then avoid those things.

*"Invert, always invert."* — Carl Jacobi (mathematician; Munger's most-cited source)

**Where it appears:**
- Mathematics: proof by contradiction
- Engineering: failure mode analysis, fault trees
- Medicine: epidemiology (what causes disease → avoid the cause)
- Investing: Munger's "all I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there"
- Strategy: pre-mortem analysis (imagine failure, work backward)

**How to use it:**
- Before planning how to succeed at anything important, spend equal time planning how to guarantee failure. Then don't do those things.
- When stuck on a problem, flip it. What would make this problem worse? What are you currently doing that's on that list?
- Pre-mortem: before a project launches, assume it has failed catastrophically. What caused it? Fix those things first.

## The Map Is Not the Territory

**The principle:** Every model, framework, map, or mental representation of reality is a simplification. It captures some features and omits others. Mistaking the model for reality is one of the most common and costly errors in thinking.

*Named by Alfred Korzybski; popularized by Bateson, Borges, and many others.*

**Where it appears:**
- Science: all scientific models are approximations of reality — useful, not true
- Economics: GDP is not wellbeing; market price is not value; the model of rational actors is useful, not accurate
- Psychology: your story about why someone did something is not their actual motivation
- Medicine: the diagnosis is not the patient; the category is not the individual
- Strategy: the plan is not the battle; the org chart is not the organization

**How to use it:**
- Hold your models lightly. Use them; don't worship them.
- When a model stops producing accurate predictions, update the model — not reality.
- Ask of every framework you use: what does this model *not* capture? What does it systematically distort?
- The most dangerous maps are the ones that look most accurate. They're the ones you'll forget to question.

## How to Build the Latticework

The models above are only useful if they're *connected* in your mind and *available* in real time.

**The practice:**
1. Learn each model deeply in its home domain first. A shallow understanding doesn't transfer.
2. Actively hunt for the model in other domains. Every time you spot power laws in a new context, record it.
3. When facing any significant decision or problem, run it through the latticework. Which models apply? What does each one say?
4. Over time, the retrieval becomes automatic. The latticework fires without effort.

**The test:** When you encounter a new problem, do multiple models light up simultaneously — or just one? Multiple simultaneous models = latticework functioning. Single model = you're still in specialist mode.

The polymath's cognitive advantage is not speed or raw intelligence. It is that every new piece of information hits a richer, denser network — and produces more insight per unit of input.
