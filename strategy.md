# Strategy

Long-game thinking. Not what to do next — where to be positioned so the right moves become obvious.

---

## Positioning

The best move is often made weeks or years before it's needed.

- **Be in the room before the opportunity.** Build relationships, skills, and credibility before you need them. Reactive positioning is always weaker.
- **Own the high ground.** In any competitive situation, ask: which position gives me the most options? Occupy that. Options are the currency of strategy.
- **Make yourself hard to replace.** Rare + valuable beats common + competent. Find the overlap of what you're good at, what the world needs, and what almost no one else can do.
- **Avoid single points of failure.** In income, relationships, health, reputation — concentration creates fragility. Diversify the critical systems.

### The Uncontested Space

Porter's framework defines two competitive strategies: low-cost or differentiated. There is a third: create a category where you are the only one. Peter Thiel calls this a monopoly. Blue Ocean Strategy calls it value innovation. The logic is the same — stop competing in crowded markets. Build the market that doesn't exist yet.

- **Category creation is the highest-leverage positioning move.** If you're the fifth-best in a category, you're fighting for scraps. If you define the category, you set the rules.
- **Ask "what is true about the world that almost no one believes yet?"** Thiel's secret: find things that are true but not yet consensus. Act on them before the consensus forms. By the time others agree, you've already moved.
- **Own the name.** Language is territory. If you can get people to use your vocabulary — your framework, your terms — you've colonized their thinking. This is how consultants build empires: the client starts using your language.
- **Be first or be best, not second-best.** In a world of abundant information, second place is often invisible. Either pioneer the space or dominate it so thoroughly that your name is synonymous with it.

### Building Moats

Buffett talks about economic moats — competitive advantages that protect against erosion. The same concept applies to personal and professional positioning.

The four personal moats:
1. **Knowledge moat:** Accumulated expertise that takes years to replicate. Deep technical skill, domain mastery, pattern libraries built from experience. Not easily copied because it takes time.
2. **Relationship moat:** Trust networks. The person who knows everyone who matters in a field has an advantage that credentials alone cannot replicate. Built over years, not months.
3. **Reputation moat:** What people say about you when you're not there. Once solidified, reputation precedes you into every room. It recruits on your behalf. It survives your absence.
4. **Institutional moat:** Access, credentials, affiliations that gate-keep specific opportunities. Being inside the institution vs. outside it is often the entire game.

Identify which moat is most relevant to your current objectives. Invest disproportionately there.

### Positioning Questions to Ask Annually

- What is my current position and how did I get here?
- What position do I want to occupy in 5 years — and is my current trajectory pointing toward it?
- Where am I competing where I shouldn't be? (Wrong arena)
- Where am I not competing where I could dominate? (Underexplored territory)
- What do I know that almost nobody else knows, and am I leveraging it?

## Asymmetric Leverage

Look for moves where effort is small and the effect is large. These are always available — most people aren't looking.

- **The 80/20 rule applied ruthlessly.** 20% of inputs produce 80% of outputs. Identify the 20% and over-invest there. Cut the rest.
- **Compounding is the most powerful force in any domain.** Skills, reputation, relationships, wealth, knowledge — all compound. Start early. Interrupt the compounding as rarely as possible.
- **Trust is asymmetric leverage.** Earned slowly, destroyed instantly. A high-trust reputation opens doors that talent alone cannot. Invest in it.
- **Ask better questions than everyone else.** The person who frames the question often controls the answer. Question quality is a strategic advantage.

### Finding Leverage Points

A lever is any input where a small force produces a disproportionate output. Donella Meadows identified leverage points in systems — the same logic applies to life strategy.

The highest leverage points in a human system:
1. **Rules of the game** — Who makes the rules governs outcomes. Get into positions that let you shape the rules.
2. **Information flows** — Who knows what, when, determines behavior. Control information flow or make sure yours is better than everyone else's.
3. **Goals of the system** — Change the objective and everything reorganizes. Most people tinker with parameters. Change the goal.
4. **Paradigm** — The deepest leverage. What people believe about how the world works governs everything. If you can shift the shared paradigm, you change what's possible.

Practical leverage checklist:
- Am I solving a problem that if solved, makes 10 other problems easier?
- Am I building something durable, or just completing a task?
- Does this action create optionality or close it?
- What's the compounding potential? In 5 years, what does this become?

## Game Theory in Real Life

Strategy is not a solo game. It is always played against other agents with their own interests. Understanding game theory is not academic — it's operational.

### The Prisoner's Dilemma

Two parties can cooperate for mutual gain or defect for individual advantage. The rational individual choice leads to collectively worse outcomes. This dynamic is everywhere:

- Business negotiations where both sides over-leverage and destroy the deal
- Arms races where both sides would prefer no weapons but neither can commit first
- Trust collapses where defection triggers mutual retaliation

The optimal long-run strategy (Robert Axelrod's tournament): **Tit for Tat with generosity.** Cooperate first. Mirror the other party's moves. Forgive occasionally. Never be the first to defect. This strategy outperforms always-defect and always-cooperate over repeated interactions.

Operational implications:
- In repeated games, cooperate by default. The relationship is worth more than the individual transaction.
- Punish defection swiftly and visibly. If defection has no consequence, it continues.
- Signal your strategy clearly. Unpredictability is only an advantage in zero-sum games. In positive-sum games, clarity builds trust.
- Convert zero-sum thinking to positive-sum frames wherever possible. Find the deal structure where both parties win.

### Signaling Theory

You cannot show people your internal reality directly. You signal it. The signal's value depends on its cost — cheap talk is ignored because it costs nothing to fake.

- **Costly signals are credible.** A reputation for keeping promises is credible because breaking promises costs you. A degree from a hard school is a credible signal because fake students wash out.
- **What are you actually signaling?** Everything you do signals something. Your punctuality signals how much you value other people's time. Your follow-through signals whether you can be trusted. Your silence signals either confidence or emptiness — others will interpret based on context.
- **Design your costly signals deliberately.** Do the thing that's hard but visible. Show up when it's inconvenient. Deliver before the deadline. These are credible signals because they are costly to fake consistently.
- **Beware of signal substitutes.** Wearing the uniform without doing the work. Using the vocabulary without having the knowledge. The gap between signal and substance is eventually discovered and the collapse is total.

### Coordination Games

Many strategic situations are not about competition but about coordination — getting others to align on the same equilibrium as you. Traffic flows on the right side. Contracts use standard terms. Agreements happen at focal points.

- **Schelling points:** Thomas Schelling identified that absent communication, people coordinate on "focal" solutions — obvious choices that stand out by prominence or convention. Be the Schelling point in your space. Be the obvious choice people naturally converge on.
- **Standard setting:** Whoever establishes the standard creates the coordination game that everyone else must play on their terms. VHS beat Betamax not on quality but on coordination.
- **Make alignment easy.** When you want people to do something, make your preferred option the natural Schelling point. Make the alternative require coordination.

### Reputation as Enforcement

In repeated games, reputation functions as a commitment device. You cannot credibly promise future behavior — except through reputation.

- **Reputation is a binding contract.** If you're known for never negotiating after a handshake, people know the handshake is binding. Your reputation enforces the agreement you cannot legally enforce.
- **Invest in reputation asymmetrically.** In long-run relationships: trust-building behaviors compound. In one-shot transactions: reputation has less value, so be proportionally more cautious.
- **Punish reputation violations visibly.** When someone violates a social contract, the appropriate response is visible and proportionate. Private punishment doesn't update others' models.
- **Don't trade reputation for short-term gain.** The arbitrage is tempting. The cost is always larger than it appears.

## Intelligence: Knowing Before Others Know

Information asymmetry is a strategic weapon. The person who knows first moves first. The person who moves first defines the landscape everyone else navigates.

### Information Architecture

Construct your information diet as deliberately as you construct your physical diet. Most people consume information reactively — what surfaces, what's viral, what's in their feed. This is nutritionally equivalent to eating whatever is put in front of you.

Build an active intelligence system:
- **Primary sources over secondary.** Read the actual paper, the actual data, the actual filing. Most people read summaries of summaries. The advantage lives in the gap between the original and the interpretation.
- **Track leading indicators, not lagging ones.** The news reports what happened. You want to understand what is happening, and what is about to happen. Leading indicators: job postings, VC investment patterns, regulatory filings, academic preprints, conference agendas, patent applications.
- **Go where serious people go before they go elsewhere.** Early-stage communities — academic conferences, specialized forums, niche newsletters — concentrate insight before it disperses into mainstream awareness. Arrive early. Stay until the interesting people leave.
- **Ask questions in rooms where the answers are.** The fastest intelligence is direct. Know who knows things and maintain those relationships. One conversation with the right person beats three months of research.

### What to Track

Build a signal map — the indicators, sources, and relationships that give you early visibility into what matters to your goals.

For career and professional intelligence:
- Who is getting promoted, and into what roles
- What skills appear repeatedly in job descriptions for the role you want in 3 years
- Which ideas are spreading through your field's thought leaders
- What problems are getting funded that weren't funded before

For market and opportunity intelligence:
- Where are the best people moving?
- What technologies are 10 years out from mainstream adoption?
- Which regulations are being drafted?
- What assumptions are so widely shared they've stopped being questioned?

### Information Asymmetry as Weapon

You don't always need more information. You often need better information — or the same information used more strategically.

- **Silence in negotiation:** The counterparty who speaks first often reveals their position. Ask questions. Let them fill silences. They will tell you what you need to know.
- **Disclose strategically.** What you reveal determines what others know about your position. Share what serves you. Withhold what doesn't.
- **Understand the other party's information state.** Before negotiating, ask: what do they know? What do they believe? What do they want me to believe they know? These are different questions.

## Tempo and Pace

Controlling the speed of events is a strategic variable most people never consider.

### Boyd's OODA Loop

John Boyd — fighter pilot, military strategist — described decision-making as a loop: Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. The pilot who cycles through this loop faster wins. Not because each decision is better, but because the faster looper is acting inside the opponent's decision cycle — the opponent is always responding to a situation that has already changed.

This applies everywhere:
- The company that ships faster than it can be copied wins markets
- The negotiator who moves the agenda faster than the counterparty can process takes control
- The person who iterates on their own thinking faster than peer consensus forms shapes the consensus

**Speed is not always the goal.** It depends on the game. In games where speed wins: increase tempo. In games where deliberation matters: slow the game down.

### Tempo Disruption

Changing pace disorients opponents who have adapted to your rhythm. Sun Tzu: "In battle, use the direct methods to engage, and the indirect methods to win." The indirect method is often a tempo shift.

- **Accelerate when the opponent is organizing.** Hit before they're ready.
- **Decelerate when the opponent wants urgency.** False deadlines are a manipulation tactic. Let them expire. The urgency they manufactured disappears.
- **Change your operating rhythm periodically.** Predictable patterns are exploitable. Vary when you respond, how you respond, what you prioritize. Stay oriented; let others lose orientation.

### Controlling Meeting and Negotiation Tempo

- Set the agenda. The agenda is the tempo.
- Ask for time to think when decisions are being rushed. "I want to give this the thought it deserves" is a tempo-slowing move.
- Accelerate when you have momentum. Press advantages in the moment they exist.
- Use silence. Pauses are tempo tools. Fill them and you lose control of the pace.

## The Alliance Game

No major objective is achieved alone. Coalition-building is a strategic discipline.

### Who to Align With

The right allies share enough interests to cooperate and have enough capability to matter. But compatibility requires more precision than that.

Criteria for alliance:
- **Aligned incentives, not just aligned goals.** Goals can be shared at a surface level while incentives diverge at depth. Ask: what do they actually gain from this? Does it track with what they say they want?
- **Complementary capabilities.** The strongest alliances cover different ground. Two people who do the same thing add linearly. People with different strengths multiply.
- **Track record of reliability.** What you see now is what you get under pressure. Watch how the person handles adversity before committing to them.
- **Capacity to act.** An ally who wants to help but cannot is not an alliance — it's a liability. Assess whether the aligned party has actual agency.

### Who to Avoid

Some alliances are costly regardless of apparent benefit:
- People whose reputation would contaminate yours
- People with a pattern of betrayal in similar situations
- People who need constant managing — taking more than they contribute
- People who are loyal to the situation, not to you — allies of convenience who will calculate against you when conditions change

### When to Create Coalitions

Coalitions are appropriate when:
- No single actor has enough power to achieve the objective
- The objective benefits multiple parties
- The opposing force is too strong to challenge directly

Coalition dynamics: the larger the coalition, the harder to maintain alignment. Minimize size to the minimum necessary for the objective. Build trust before you need to deploy it.

### Strategic Alliances vs. Tactical Alliances

Strategic alliances: long-term, built on shared values and deep mutual interest. These are worth significant investment. Tactical alliances: formed for a specific objective, dissolved when complete. Fine, but don't confuse them for strategic alliances. Treating a tactical ally as a strategic partner creates obligation and exposure that wasn't warranted.

## Patience as a Weapon

Most people are playing a short game. The long game has less competition.

- **Time arbitrage:** Be willing to wait when others can't. The impatient always pay a premium — to the patient.
- **Let problems solve themselves.** A large percentage of urgent problems are not problems in a week. Don't spend capital on what time will resolve.
- **Compound your patience.** The longer you can hold a correct position, the larger the eventual payoff. This applies to investments, relationships, and ideas.
- **Recognize the late-mover disadvantage.** Others will start more things, generate more noise, burn more resources. Stay focused. Let them tire.

### Strategic Patience vs. Strategic Paralysis

These two states feel similar from the inside. They are opposites in effect.

**Strategic patience:**
- You have a clear thesis and are waiting for conditions to align
- You are actively monitoring for signals that your moment has arrived
- The waiting is deliberate — a choice, not a default
- You are building while you wait

**Strategic paralysis:**
- You lack clarity on what you actually want
- The waiting is avoidance of a decision that carries risk
- No signals are being tracked because no thesis exists
- Nothing is being built; there is only waiting

Diagnostic question: "If the perfect conditions appeared tomorrow — would I know? And would I act?" If the answer to either part is no, you are in paralysis.

Breaking paralysis requires not more analysis but more specificity. Define the thesis. Define the signal that triggers action. Write it down. The act of specification often dissolves the paralysis.

## Narrative Control

The story being told about you — by you and others — shapes what's possible.

- **Frame first.** The person who defines the terms of the discussion usually wins it. When entering any negotiation or conflict, name the frame before the other side does.
- **Reputation precedes you.** Invest in what people say about you when you're not there. That version of you opens doors you'll never know about.
- **The story you tell yourself matters most.** Identity narratives (I am someone who...) are the most powerful behavioral governors. Choose them deliberately.
- **Silence is strategic.** You don't have to fill every gap. Comfortable silence is a power move. Over-explaining is a tell.

### Framing Mechanics

A frame is a set of assumptions that determines what questions get asked, what evidence gets weighed, and what conclusions seem obvious. Controlling the frame is often more important than the argument within the frame.

- **Re-frame don't rebut.** Rebutting arguments accepts the frame. Re-framing changes the game. When attacked, don't defend on the attacker's terms. Change the terms.
- **Name things first.** The vocabulary people use constrains their thinking. Label the situation, the problem, the opportunity — with your language. Others will navigate inside your categories.
- **Whose story is this?** In any conflict or negotiation, competing narratives are at war. Know what story the other party is telling. Displace it with a more compelling one — not by arguing that their story is wrong, but by making yours more compelling.

### Narrative Management Over Time

Your narrative evolves. Active people are always in the middle of a story others are watching. You do not get to opt out of having a narrative. You only choose whether to manage it or leave it to others.

- **Consistent behavior is the foundation.** Narrative is built from behavior patterns over time. The story follows the pattern. Change the pattern to change the story.
- **Control the re-entry.** After a setback, failure, or absence, how you return defines how others interpret the chapter. Return with specificity, not apology. Show the lesson, not just the wound.
- **Create record.** What you write, publish, and say publicly is the raw material of your narrative at scale. Silence is not neutral — others fill it.

## The Endgame Principle

Work backward from where you want to end up.

- **Define "enough."** For money, status, achievement. Without a finish line, the race never ends and you never win.
- **Don't optimize for the metric that replaced the goal.** The map is not the territory. Revenue is not the business. Likes are not influence. Keep the actual goal in view.
- **Legacy question:** At the end, what do you want to have built, done, and been? Let that pull present-day decisions toward it.

### Backward Induction

In game theory, backward induction means solving a game by reasoning from the end backward. What needs to be true in the final state? What does the penultimate state need to look like? Work backward to now.

Applied to life:
- State the end clearly. Not vaguely ("be successful") but specifically ("have built X, experienced Y, contributed Z").
- Identify the last mile requirements. What needs to be true 5 years before the end state?
- Work backward in intervals until you reach now.
- Ask: is what I'm doing today on the path this backward induction describes?

Most people drift toward the future. Backward induction locks in direction and lets you feel the pull of the destination.

### Avoiding Goal Displacement

Metrics are means, not ends. The system optimizes for what it measures. When you measure a proxy for the goal, the proxy becomes the goal and the original goal drifts.

- Revenue is a proxy for business health. A business with high revenue and no margin is failing by the original goal.
- Followers are a proxy for influence. An account with millions of followers who take no action has no influence.
- Credentials are a proxy for capability. The credential without the underlying capability is a costume.
- Busyness is a proxy for productivity. A full calendar and no output is theater.

Periodically: strip the metrics and ask if you're actually achieving the underlying objective they were built to measure.

### The Regret Minimization Framework

Jeff Bezos: when deciding whether to take a significant risk, project yourself to age 80 and ask which you would regret more — trying and failing, or not trying. The calculus shifts when viewed from the end.

Most people overweight current comfort and underweight long-run regret. The regret minimization framework corrects this bias. The risks that look large now look different from 80.
