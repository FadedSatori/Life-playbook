# Cognitive Biases: A Field Guide

Your brain runs on shortcuts. Most of the time they work: they let you decide fast on partial information. But they fail in predictable directions, and those predictable failures are cognitive biases. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's 1974 paper in *Science*, "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," launched the modern study of them. Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) is the best single book on the subject.

This file is a field guide: 24 biases, grouped by where they strike, each with what it is, what it looks like, and the counter. [epistemic-self-defense.md](epistemic-self-defense.md) covers biases as targets for manipulation. This file is the catalog and the structural defenses.

The central lesson comes first: knowing the list will not save you. Building the counter into how you decide will.

---

## Why Knowing Isn't Enough

**The bias blind spot.** Emily Pronin, Daniel Lin, and Lee Ross (2002) found that people readily spot biases in others while rating themselves as less biased than average. Later work (West, Meserve, and Stanovich, 2012) found that higher cognitive ability did not shrink this blind spot.

**Biases run below awareness.** Knowing that the lines in an optical illusion are equal doesn't make them look equal. Anchors pull estimates even when people know the anchor is random.

**Training helps, partially.** Some interactive training has produced measurable reductions on specific biases that lasted at least two months (Carey Morewedge and colleagues, 2015). But the gains are partial, and how well they carry into real, high-stakes decisions is still uncertain.

**The conclusion:** Don't count on catching yourself in the moment. Change the structure of the decision so the bias has less room to work. Most of the counters below are structures, not attitudes.

## Judgment and Estimation

**Anchoring.** The first number you see pulls your estimate toward it, even when it's irrelevant. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) spun a wheel rigged to stop at 10 or 65, then asked people what percentage of UN members were African countries; median answers were 25 and 45.
- *Example:* The asking price shapes what you think a house is worth.
- *Counter:* Write your own estimate before seeing anyone else's. List reasons the anchor could be wrong. See [negotiation.md](negotiation.md).

**Availability.** Judging how common or likely something is by how easily examples come to mind (Tversky and Kahneman, 1973).
- *Example:* After wall-to-wall coverage of a plane crash, flying feels more dangerous than the drive to the airport, which per mile is far riskier.
- *Counter:* Ask for the number, not the story. What is the actual rate?

**Base-rate neglect.** Ignoring how common something is in general and judging only by how well the specific case fits a stereotype.
- *Example:* A quiet, orderly man "must be a librarian," though there are far more farmers than librarians.
- *Counter:* Start with the reference class and its base rate, then adjust for specifics. See [mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md](mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md).

**Overconfidence.** Being more sure than your accuracy justifies.
- *Example:* Asked for ranges they are 90% sure contain the true answer, people typically give ranges that miss far more than 10% of the time.
- *Counter:* Widen your ranges. Record your confidence and check it against outcomes in a [decision journal](decision-journal.md).

**Planning fallacy.** Underestimating time, cost, and risk for your own projects while knowing similar projects run over (named by Kahneman and Tversky; documented in students' thesis estimates by Roger Buehler and colleagues in 1994).
- *Example:* The renovation scheduled for six weeks takes four months.
- *Counter:* Ask how long similar projects actually took. Add a buffer based on your own track record of overruns.

**Scope insensitivity.** Feelings don't scale with numbers. In a classic study by William Desvousges and colleagues, groups asked what they'd pay to save 2,000, 20,000, or 200,000 birds from oil ponds gave about the same answer: roughly $80, $78, and $88.
- *Example:* One identifiable victim moves you more than a statistic of thousands.
- *Counter:* Do the arithmetic per unit. Ask: if this were ten times larger, would my answer change? If not, my feelings aren't tracking the size.

**Affect heuristic.** Letting how you feel about something stand in for judging it. Paul Slovic and colleagues found that when people like something, they tend to see its benefits as high and its risks as low, and vice versa.
- *Example:* The investment you're excited about seems both high-return and safe.
- *Counter:* Rate benefits and risks separately, on paper. Sleep on any decision you feel strongly about.

## Evidence and Belief

**Confirmation bias.** Seeking, interpreting, and remembering evidence that supports what you already believe. In Peter Wason's 2-4-6 task (1960), most people tested only examples that fit their hypothesis, never ones that could disprove it.
- *Example:* After buying a car, you read every glowing review and skim past the complaints.
- *Counter:* Write the evidence that would change your mind, then go looking for it. Argue the other side in writing.

**Narrative fallacy.** Nassim Taleb's term (*The Black Swan*, 2007) for forcing a coherent story onto events, which makes us feel we understand more than we do.
- *Example:* A business book explains a company's rise by its culture, ignoring luck and the companies with the same culture that failed.
- *Counter:* Ask what the boring explanation is: luck, base rates, regression to the mean. Prefer numbers over arcs.

**Survivorship bias.** Drawing conclusions only from the cases that made it through a filter. The classic case: in World War II, statistician Abraham Wald pointed out that bullet holes on returning bombers showed where planes could be hit and survive; the armor belonged where the returning planes had no holes.
- *Example:* "Dropouts become billionaires," judged from the few famous ones.
- *Counter:* Ask where the failures are and what the denominator is.

## Memory and Time

**Hindsight bias.** Once you know the outcome, you believe you saw it coming (Baruch Fischhoff's research in the 1970s).
- *Example:* "I always knew that hire wouldn't work out."
- *Counter:* Write predictions down before the outcome. Compare later. See [decision-journal.md](decision-journal.md).

**Outcome bias.** Judging a decision by its result rather than by the quality of the decision when it was made (Jonathan Baron and John Hershey, 1988). Annie Duke calls it resulting.
- *Example:* Praising a reckless bet because it paid off.
- *Counter:* Separate process from result: good process with bad luck is still good process.

**Recency bias.** Overweighting the latest information.
- *Example:* Investors extrapolate last year's returns; annual reviews reflect the last month.
- *Counter:* Look at the long series. Keep running notes across the whole period you'll judge.

## Risk and Choice

**Loss aversion.** Losses loom larger than equal gains (Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, 1979). Losses are often said to weigh about twice as much, but the size and generality of the effect are debated.
- *Example:* Turning down a coin flip that wins $150 or loses $100.
- *Counter:* Judge small bets as part of a portfolio: would you take this bet a hundred times? Set rules in advance.

**Sunk cost fallacy.** Continuing because of what you've already invested rather than what lies ahead (Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer, 1985).
- *Example:* Staying in a failing project because of the two years already spent.
- *Counter:* Ask: if I were starting fresh today, would I choose this? See [mental-models/decision-making.md](mental-models/decision-making.md).

**Status quo bias.** Preferring the current state because it's current (William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser, 1988). Defaults are powerful: countries where organ donation is opt-out have far higher consent rates than opt-in countries (Johnson and Goldstein, 2003).
- *Example:* Keeping the same insurance, investments, or job for years without ever re-choosing.
- *Counter:* Reverse the question: if I didn't have this, would I sign up for it today? Set your defaults deliberately.

**Framing.** The same facts presented differently change choices. In Tversky and Kahneman's 1981 "disease problem," people preferred an option framed as lives saved but rejected the identical option framed as lives lost.
- *Example:* "90% survival" sounds better than "10% mortality."
- *Counter:* Restate every important option in both frames and in absolute numbers.

## Social

**Fundamental attribution error.** Explaining others' behavior by character while underweighting their situation (named by Lee Ross in 1977). Edward Jones and Victor Harris (1967) found people attributed pro-Castro views to essay writers even when told the position had been assigned.
- *Example:* The driver who cut you off is a jerk; when you do it, you're rushing to an emergency.
- *Counter:* Ask what situation would make a decent person act this way.

**Halo effect.** One positive trait colors judgments of unrelated traits (Edward Thorndike, 1920).
- *Example:* A tall, articulate candidate is assumed competent.
- *Counter:* Score each criterion separately, on a pre-set rubric, before discussing. Use work samples.

**In-group bias.** Favoring your own group. Henri Tajfel's minimal group experiments found favoritism even in groups formed on trivial grounds, such as preferring one painter over another.
- *Example:* Accepting a weak argument because someone on your side made it.
- *Counter:* The swap test: would I accept this if the other side said it?

**Conformity.** Going along with the group against your own judgment. In Solomon Asch's line-judgment experiments (1950s), about three-quarters of participants went along with an obviously wrong majority at least once, and about a third of critical responses conformed. A single dissenting ally cut conformity sharply. Rates vary across cultures and eras.
- *Example:* Staying silent in a meeting when everyone endorses a plan you think is flawed.
- *Counter:* Collect written views independently before discussion. Assign a dissenter. Be the ally.

**Obedience to authority.** Stanley Milgram's 1963 study found that 26 of 40 participants (65%) continued to the maximum shock level when instructed. Obedience dropped in variations where the experimenter was absent, the victim was closer, or peers refused. The interpretation is debated: Gina Perry's archival work (*Behind the Shock Machine*, 2012) found departures from the script and many participants doubting the shocks were real, and Alex Haslam and Stephen Reicher argue participants were identifying with the scientific project rather than blindly obeying.
- *Example:* Following a senior person's instruction you suspect is wrong because they're senior.
- *Counter:* Decide your lines in advance. Treat authority as a claim to check, not a reason. One refusal makes the next one easier for everyone.

## Self-Perception

**Self-serving bias.** Crediting yourself for success and blaming circumstances for failure.
- *Example:* "I closed that deal." "The market killed this one."
- *Counter:* Apply the same standard of explanation to wins and losses. Ask someone who'll tell you the truth.

**Optimism bias.** Believing you're less likely than others to suffer bad outcomes and more likely to enjoy good ones (Neil Weinstein's "unrealistic optimism," 1980).
- *Example:* "Divorce, injury, business failure: those happen to other people."
- *Counter:* Plan for the base-rate outcome. Insure against what you can't absorb. Run a pre-mortem.

## Structural Fixes That Beat Awareness

- **Checklists.** Atul Gawande's *The Checklist Manifesto* (2009) makes the case. A 2009 study of the WHO surgical checklist in eight hospitals (Haynes et al., *NEJM*) reported fewer complications and deaths; a later province-wide rollout in Ontario (Urbach et al., 2014) found no significant change. Checklists work when they're used, not when they're filed.
- **Pre-commitment.** Set rules while calm: sell rules, spending limits, walk-away points. Ulysses had himself tied to the mast before he heard the Sirens.
- **The outside view.** Before analyzing your case, find how similar cases turned out. See [mental-models/decision-making.md](mental-models/decision-making.md).
- **Independent estimates first.** Averaging many guesses tends to beat most individuals, but the advantage shrinks when people influence each other first. Have everyone write their number before discussion; the most senior person speaks last.
- **Consider the opposite.** Charles Lord, Mark Lepper, and Elizabeth Preston (1984) found that asking people to consider the opposite reduced biased judgment more than simply asking them to be unbiased.
- **Pre-mortem and red team.** Assume failure and explain it; assign someone to argue against. See [intelligence-tradecraft.md](intelligence-tradecraft.md).
- **Simple rules and formulas.** Paul Meehl's *Clinical Versus Statistical Prediction* (1954) found simple statistical rules generally matched or beat expert judgment in the studies he reviewed. Use scoring rubrics wherever you make the same kind of judgment repeatedly.
- **Cooling-off periods.** No major decision within 24 hours of a strong emotion.
- **Keep score.** Written predictions with probabilities, reviewed later: [decision-journal.md](decision-journal.md).

## Bias by Situation

| Situation | Biases most likely | Structural fix |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------|
| Negotiating, buying | Anchoring, framing, loss aversion | Write your number and walk-away point beforehand |
| Hiring, judging people | Halo, attribution error, in-group | Structured interviews, work samples, independent scoring |
| Investing | Loss aversion, recency, overconfidence, sunk cost | Written rules, scheduled rebalancing, sensible defaults |
| Planning a project | Planning fallacy, optimism | Reference class, buffer from past overruns, pre-mortem |
| Reviewing a win or loss | Hindsight, outcome, self-serving | Compare against the prediction written at the time |
| Reading the news | Availability, affect, confirmation | Ask for rates; wait before sharing; read the best opposing case |
| Group meetings | Conformity, authority, in-group | Silent written input first; leader speaks last; assigned dissent |
| Health choices | Affect, availability, framing | Absolute risks, number needed to treat, second opinion |

## The Pre-Decision Bias Check

Before any significant decision, answer in writing:

1. What was my estimate before I saw anyone else's? *(anchoring)*
2. How do situations like this usually turn out? *(base rates, planning fallacy, optimism)*
3. What evidence would change my mind, and have I looked for it? *(confirmation)*
4. If I were starting fresh today, would I choose this? *(sunk cost, status quo)*
5. Does it look different stated as a loss, a gain, or in absolute numbers? *(framing, loss aversion)*
6. Am I reacting to something vivid or recent? *(availability, recency)*
7. Would I accept this argument from the other side? *(in-group)*
8. Did I form my view before hearing the group or the boss? *(conformity, authority)*
9. How sure am I, and how often am I right at that level of confidence? *(overconfidence)*
10. Would I decide the same way tomorrow, calm and rested? *(affect)*
