# Epistemic Self-Defense

Your mind is the primary target of the information age. Adversarial content, tribal epistemics, manufactured outrage, and algorithmic manipulation are running in the background of every digital interaction. This is how you protect the operating system.

---

## The Threat Model

The information environment is not neutral. It is designed — by algorithms, by political actors, by attention merchants — to hijack your beliefs, decisions, and identity for purposes that are not yours.

What you're defending against:
- **Algorithmic manipulation** — systems optimized for engagement, which means outrage, fear, and tribal identity
- **Motivated reasoning** — your own brain's tendency to find evidence for what you already believe
- **Social proof cascades** — large numbers of people believing something is not evidence that it's true
- **Manufactured consensus** — manufactured urgency, false dichotomies, overton window manipulation
- **Identity-based epistemics** — believing things because your tribe believes them, not because they're true

## First Principles Thinking

Don't start from what you've been told. Start from what you can verify.

**The Feynman method:**
1. State the question as simply as possible
2. Identify what you *actually* know (vs. what you've accepted)
3. Identify where the gap is
4. Fill the gap from primary sources, not summaries of summaries

*"I would rather have questions that can't be answered than answers that can't be questioned."* — Feynman

**Ask of every important belief you hold:** How would I know if this were wrong? If you cannot answer that question, you don't hold a belief — a belief holds you.

## Bayesian Updating

Rational belief change: update your confidence in proportion to the evidence.

The practice:
1. **Start with a prior** — what do I currently believe, and how confident am I? (0–100%)
2. **Encounter new evidence** — does this make my belief more or less likely?
3. **Update proportionally** — strong evidence = large update; weak evidence = small update
4. **Never update to certainty** — 100% and 0% are not Bayesian positions

The anti-pattern: treating every piece of new evidence as either total confirmation or total invalidation. That's not updating — it's oscillating.

## Cognitive Bias Armor

The most dangerous biases to know and watch for in yourself:

- **Confirmation bias** — seeking evidence that confirms what you already believe. Counter: actively seek disconfirming evidence.
- **Availability heuristic** — judging likelihood by how easily an example comes to mind. Counter: look for base rates, not vivid examples.
- **In-group bias** — trusting sources more because they share your identity. Counter: evaluate the argument, not the arguer.
- **Dunning-Kruger** — peak confidence at low competence. Counter: the more certain you feel, the more carefully you should verify.
- **Sunk cost fallacy** — continuing a losing path because of past investment. Counter: ask "would I start this today knowing what I know?"
- **Narrative bias** — preferring a coherent story over accurate probability. Counter: facts don't owe you a satisfying arc.

## Memetic Hygiene

Memes — ideas, frames, slogans — are the viruses of the mind. Some are useful. Many are parasitic.

**Signs of a memetic parasite:**
- It produces strong emotion with weak evidence
- It requires you to view a large group of people as sub-human or purely evil
- It spreads by shame or social pressure rather than logic
- It benefits someone else more than it benefits you
- It makes you feel uniquely enlightened or uniquely victimized

**The quarantine protocol:** when encountering content that triggers strong immediate emotion, don't share it for 24 hours. The urgency to spread it is almost always manufactured.

## Information Diet Design

You become what you repeatedly input. Curate accordingly.

**High-quality inputs:**
- Long-form journalism with primary sourcing
- Books (the filter of time removes most low-quality ideas)
- Direct conversations with people smarter than you in specific domains
- Primary sources when stakes are high

**Minimize:**
- Algorithmic feeds (optimized for engagement, not accuracy or your wellbeing)
- Outrage content of any ideological flavor
- Breaking news (first reports are almost always wrong)
- Opinions from people with no skin in the game

**The test for a source:** Do they update publicly when they're wrong? If not, they're building a persona, not a track record.

## Steelmanning

Before rejecting any position — especially one you disagree with — construct the strongest possible version of the argument.

Not the version that's easy to knock down. The version that would be hardest to refute.

Two benefits:
1. You sometimes find you were wrong
2. If the steelman still fails, your counter-argument is genuinely strong

*The opposite — arguing against the weakest version of an opposing view — is the straw man. It feels like winning. It is not.*

## The Socratic Practice

Powerful questions that reveal where your beliefs are load-bearing vs. borrowed:

- How do I know this?
- What would change my mind?
- What's the strongest argument against this?
- Who benefits from me believing this?
- Am I reasoning toward the truth, or away from an uncomfortable conclusion?

Ask these in private, in writing. The answers matter more than the questions.
