# The Decision Journal

You cannot improve what you do not measure, and almost nobody measures their own judgment. Most people evaluate their decisions by outcomes, remember their reasoning inaccurately, and repeat the same mistakes for decades while feeling experienced.

The decision journal is the simplest high-leverage practice in this playbook. A few minutes at the moment of decision, a short review later, and over time you build the one dataset that matters: how well you actually think.

[mental-models/decision-making.md](mental-models/decision-making.md) and [mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md](mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md) give you the models. This file is the feedback loop that makes the models yours.

---

## Why You Need It

**Hindsight bias.** Once you know how something turned out, you misremember having predicted it (Baruch Fischhoff's research). Without a written record, your memory edits the past to flatter your judgment. "I knew it" is usually reconstruction.

**Resulting.** Annie Duke (*Thinking in Bets*, from her poker background) names the habit of judging a decision by its outcome. A good decision can lose (you made the right bet and got unlucky), and a bad decision can win (you got lucky). Judge the process, not just the result, or you will learn the wrong lessons: repeating lucky mistakes and abandoning sound bets.

**Overconfidence.** Most people are less accurate than they feel. Expressing confidence as a probability and checking it later is the only reliable cure.

**Noise.** Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein (*Noise*, 2021): the same person facing the same decision on different days, in different moods, gives different answers. Writing your reasoning and state exposes the variability.

**Learning velocity.** The experienced person who reflects on a written record improves with each cycle. The experienced person who doesn't merely repeats a year of experience for twenty years.

## The Entry

Write it at the time of the decision, before the outcome is known. Keep it short; it must be easy to do or you won't. Five to ten minutes for a meaningful decision.

**Template:**

1. **Date and decision.** What are you deciding?
2. **Context and constraints.** What's the situation? What can't change? What's the time pressure?
3. **Options considered.** At least three, including "do nothing" and a creative one.
4. **What you expect to happen,** in specific terms, for the option chosen. Include a number or a date where possible.
5. **Probability.** How confident are you that it works out as expected? Give a percentage. 90%? 60%? Be honest.
6. **Reasoning.** The key reasons and the main assumptions. What would have to be true for this to be right?
7. **What would change your mind.** Name the evidence that would make you reverse. This guards against escalation of commitment.
8. **Biggest risk / pre-mortem.** Imagine it failed in a year. Why? (Gary Klein's pre-mortem.) See [failure.md](failure.md).
9. **Your state.** Rested or tired, calm or angry, hungry, rushed, anxious. Decisions made while angry, hungry, lonely, or tired are suspect. See [anger.md](anger.md), [health/sleep.md](health/sleep.md).
10. **Who else weighed in,** and any pressure you felt.
11. **Review date.** When will you check this? Put it in your calendar.

**What to log:** Decisions that are consequential, hard to reverse, or recurring: hires, investments, major purchases, career moves, relationship decisions, strategic bets, medical choices, big commitments of time. Not what to eat for lunch.

**Reversible vs. irreversible (Bezos's two-way doors):** Reversible decisions deserve speed and light journaling. One-way doors deserve the full template and sleep on it.

## The Review

At the review date, open the entry before you look at anything else.

1. **What actually happened?** Facts only.
2. **Compare to the prediction.** Was the outcome within your expected range?
3. **Separate luck from skill.** Using Duke's framing, sort the outcome:
   - *Good process, good outcome:* earned. Reinforce.
   - *Good process, bad outcome:* bad luck. Don't change the process; note the variance.
   - *Bad process, good outcome:* dumb luck. Dangerous: it teaches the wrong lesson. Fix the process anyway.
   - *Bad process, bad outcome:* deserved. Learn and correct.
4. **What did you miss?** Information you had and ignored, information you could have gotten, assumptions that proved wrong.
5. **What does this teach about your tendencies?** Look across entries for patterns (see below).
6. **Update.** One sentence of lesson. Record changes to your checklists or defaults.

Do the review without self-punishment. This is data collection, not a trial. See the debrief protocol in [failure.md](failure.md).

## Calibration: Measuring Your Confidence

If you say "80% confident" across many decisions, about 80% should turn out right. Most people's 90% is closer to 70%.

**How to track:**
- Group your predictions by stated confidence: 50–60%, 60–70%, 70–80%, 80–90%, 90%+.
- At the end of a quarter or year, compute the hit rate in each bucket.
- Compare. If your 90% bucket hits 70%, you are overconfident. If your 60% bucket hits 80%, you are underconfident.

**The Brier score** is the standard accuracy measure for probability forecasts: the mean squared difference between your stated probability and what happened (1 or 0). Lower is better, and 0.25 is what you'd get by always saying 50%. Philip Tetlock's Good Judgment research found that a small set of "superforecasters" reliably beat experts by doing exactly these things: breaking questions into parts, using base rates, updating in small increments, and keeping score.

**Practices from superforecasting:**
- Start with the **base rate** (the outside view) before the specifics of your case. See [mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md](mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md).
- **Update incrementally** as evidence arrives; avoid both stubbornness and overreaction.
- **Hold views as probabilities,** not certainties.
- **Seek disconfirming views,** and actively argue the other side.

## Finding Your Patterns

After 20–30 entries, read them in sequence. Look for recurring signatures:

- Which kinds of decisions do you get wrong? (Money, people, timing, risk.)
- Does your mood or sleep correlate with worse calls?
- Are you over-optimistic on timelines? (The planning fallacy is nearly universal. Add a buffer based on how your past estimates compared with reality.)
- Do you overweight vivid information, recent events, or the last person you spoke to?
- Do you decide faster than the situation requires, or avoid deciding at all?
- Do certain people or contexts degrade your judgment?
- Where do you ignore your own earlier "what would change my mind" list?

Convert patterns into **rules and checklists.** For instance: "No major financial decision within 24 hours of a strong emotion." "Never accept a job offer on the call." "For any estimate, add 40%." Rules replace recurring failures with defaults. See [heuristics.md](heuristics.md) and [maxims.md](maxims.md); your own are the most valuable ones in the playbook.

## Group and Team Use

- **Pre-mortems in teams:** Everyone writes privately why the project failed, then shares. Privately first prevents the highest-status voice from anchoring the group.
- **Red team / devil's advocate:** Assign someone to argue the opposite. See [intelligence-tradecraft.md](intelligence-tradecraft.md).
- **Record major team decisions** with reasoning, owner, and review date. A culture that reviews decisions rather than hunting for culprits learns faster. See [leadership.md](leadership.md).
- **Independent estimates before discussion** reduce noise and anchoring.

---

## Tools

- A plain notebook or a single document works. The best system is the one you'll use.
- A spreadsheet helps with calibration tracking: date, decision, probability, outcome, result.
- Set calendar reminders for reviews. A journal without reviews is a diary.
- Keep it private so you can be honest.

**Minimum viable version:** For each important decision, write three lines: what I'm deciding and why, what I expect (with a probability), and when I'll check. Do this for a month before adding more.
