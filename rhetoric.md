# Rhetoric

Rhetoric is the art of making a case so that a particular audience understands it and moves. It has a bad name because it can serve lies. It has been a core discipline of every free society because the alternative to persuasion is force. Where people decide by argument, the person who can argue well shapes the outcome.

[tactics/public-speaking.md](tactics/public-speaking.md) covers delivery in a room. [communication.md](communication.md) covers clear exchange. [negotiation.md](negotiation.md) and [sales.md](sales.md) cover persuasion in transactions. This file covers the architecture of argument itself: how to build one, how to deliver it with force, how to see through others', and how to stay honest while doing it.

## Aristotle's Foundation

Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (4th century BCE) remains the best framework. He defined rhetoric as the faculty of observing, in any given case, the available means of persuasion. Three appeals:

**Ethos (character).** The audience's judgment of your credibility, goodwill, and good sense. Aristotle held it is the most potent of the three: people believe those they trust. You build ethos by competence, integrity, and evident goodwill toward the audience, mostly *before* you speak, through your record. See [networks.md](networks.md).

**Pathos (emotion).** The audience's state of mind. People decide with emotion and justify with reason ([epistemic-self-defense.md](epistemic-self-defense.md)). A good speaker engages emotion truthfully and in proportion to the facts. Manufacturing emotion untethered from the facts is manipulation.

**Logos (reason).** The argument: claims, evidence, and inference. Without it, ethos and pathos are decoration.

A complete case has all three. Pure logic with no feeling fails to move. Pure feeling with no substance collapses under scrutiny. Charisma with no character is a warning sign.

**Kairos.** The opportune moment. The same argument fails at one time and succeeds at another. Read the room, the mood, and the moment. See [time.md](time.md).

**The three kinds of speech:**
- **Deliberative:** persuading about future action (what should we do?). Politics, strategy, business decisions.
- **Forensic (judicial):** about past events (what happened, who is at fault?).
- **Epideictic (ceremonial):** praise and blame, shared values (eulogies, toasts, inaugurals).

Know which you are doing; they have different standards and tones.

## The Five Canons

The classical workflow from Cicero and Quintilian for building a speech or an argument:

1. **Invention:** Find what to say: the issue, the arguments, the evidence. Ask what the audience believes, what they need, and what the disputed point really is (the *stasis*: is it a question of fact, definition, quality, or policy?).
2. **Arrangement:** Order it. A classical structure: introduction (win attention and goodwill), statement of the case, proof, refutation of objections, conclusion (summarize and move to action).
3. **Style:** Express it clearly and vividly. See below.
4. **Memory:** Know it well enough to deliver freely. See [memory-systems.md](memory-systems.md).
5. **Delivery:** Voice, pace, pause, presence. See [tactics/public-speaking.md](tactics/public-speaking.md).

Most people skip invention and arrangement and go straight to wording. Strong arguments are won in the first two.

## Building the Argument

### The Toulmin model
Stephen Toulmin's structure for real-world argument, more realistic than formal logic:

- **Claim:** What you assert.
- **Grounds:** The evidence for it.
- **Warrant:** The principle connecting grounds to claim (often unstated, and where most disputes hide).
- **Backing:** Support for the warrant.
- **Qualifier:** How strongly you claim it ("probably," "in most cases").
- **Rebuttal:** The conditions under which the claim wouldn't hold, and your answer to them.

Use it to audit your own case: what is the warrant, and would a skeptic accept it? Use it on others: when someone's conclusion seems wrong, find the hidden warrant.

### Principles of a strong case
1. **State the claim plainly and early.** Don't make people hunt for it.
2. **One main point.** Many points dilute. See the one-core-idea rule in [tactics/public-speaking.md](tactics/public-speaking.md).
3. **Reasons in threes** or fewer, ordered by strength (strongest first or last, not buried in the middle).
4. **Evidence that the audience trusts:** concrete examples, data from credible sources, analogies they understand.
5. **Address the strongest objection yourself.** Raising and answering it before the opponent does builds ethos and removes their best card.
6. **Make the ask specific.** What should they believe or do?
7. **Fit the audience.** Their values, knowledge, and what they stand to gain or lose. The same case is built differently for a board, a jury, a friend, a crowd.

## Style: Making It Land

Words that move share traits: clarity, concreteness, rhythm, and surprise.

**Clarity.** Plain words, short sentences, active voice (see Orwell's rules in [writing.md](writing.md)). Clear writing signals clear thinking and respect for the audience.

**Concreteness.** Pictures and stories beat abstractions. "Forty children in a classroom with no heat" lands where "inadequate infrastructure" doesn't.

**Rhythm and figures.** Classical devices that work because they match how minds process:
- **Tricolon / rule of three:** "Veni, vidi, vici." "Government of the people, by the people, for the people."
- **Anaphora:** repetition at the beginning of clauses for momentum. Churchill: "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds..." King: "I have a dream..."
- **Antithesis:** contrast for clarity and punch. "Ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country."
- **Chiasmus:** mirrored structure (A-B-B-A). "Fair is foul, and foul is fair." "Ask not what your country can do for you" works partly because it mirrors its own clauses.
- **Metaphor and analogy:** Compress complex ideas into familiar images. Choose them carefully: they frame the issue (George Lakoff's work on framing).
- **Brevity.** Lincoln's Gettysburg Address is 272 words (by common counts), spoken after a two-hour oration by the featured speaker. Which is remembered?

**Pause and emphasis.** Silence before a key phrase. Slow down for the line that matters.

**Use ornament sparingly.** A figure that calls attention to itself weakens trust. The best rhetoric hides the craft.

**Study the masters:** Cicero, Demosthenes, Lincoln, Churchill, Frederick Douglass, Martin Luther King Jr., and Marcus Aurelius' private plainness. Read their speeches aloud; copy a passage by hand to feel the construction.

## Framing

Whoever frames the issue shapes the debate. The same facts under "tax relief" and "tax cut," "estate tax" and "death tax," produce different reactions. Lakoff and others emphasize that facts rarely override a frame; people reject facts that don't fit the frame they hold.

- **Define the terms** before the argument begins. Accepting an opponent's framing concedes ground.
- **Reframe** rather than argue within a bad frame: ask what the real question is.
- **Notice frames used on you.** What is assumed? What is left out?
- **Be honest:** framing that selects and organizes truth is rhetoric; framing that deceives is lying. See the ethics line in [negotiation.md](negotiation.md).

## Disagreeing Well

### Steelmanning (the opposite of the straw man)
State the other side's position so well that they say, "Yes, that's what I think, and you put it better than I did." Daniel Dennett's rules (drawing on Anatol Rapoport): (1) restate their position clearly and fairly; (2) list points of agreement; (3) mention anything you learned; (4) only then rebut or criticize. This builds trust, finds the real disagreement, and sometimes changes your mind. It also leaves the audience trusting you.

### Socratic questioning
Instead of asserting, ask questions that lead the other to examine their own claim: What do you mean by...? What is the evidence? What follows if that's true? What would change your mind? Questions invite thought where statements invite defense. This also underlies the Voss calibrated questions in [negotiation.md](negotiation.md).

### Change minds by lowering the threat
People defend beliefs tied to identity. To persuade: affirm their worth, find common ground, ask permission, let them reach conclusions, offer a face-saving path to change. A person told "you're wrong" digs in; a person helped to see a new angle may move. See [communication.md](communication.md).

### Be willing to lose
The mark of an honest arguer: updating when shown wrong. "If someone is able to show me that what I think or do is not right, I will happily change," wrote Marcus Aurelius. See [quotes.md](quotes.md).

## Spotting Bad Arguments

Know these to avoid them and to defuse them in others.

**Common logical fallacies:**
- **Ad hominem:** attacking the person rather than the claim.
- **Straw man:** refuting a distorted version of the position.
- **False dilemma:** presenting two options when more exist.
- **Appeal to authority:** "an expert said so" in place of evidence (credible expertise within its field is a legitimate input; fame or irrelevant credentials are not).
- **Slippery slope:** asserting a chain of consequences without support.
- **Begging the question:** assuming the conclusion in the premises.
- **Post hoc:** assuming correlation in sequence is causation.
- **Appeal to emotion / popularity / tradition** as sole support.
- **Moving the goalposts:** changing the standard of proof after it's met.
- **Whataboutism:** deflecting by pointing to someone else's fault.
- **Gish gallop:** overwhelming with many weak claims faster than they can be answered. Counter: choose the strongest one or two, answer well, and say you won't chase the rest.
- **Motte-and-bailey:** defending a strong, controversial claim (the bailey) but retreating to a modest, defensible one (the motte) when challenged. Counter: pin them to the claim they actually made.

**Don't just name the fallacy; address the substance.** Calling "fallacy!" without explaining wins nothing and often sounds evasive. Show the gap: "That assumes X; why should we accept X?"

**Counter-moves against rhetorical pressure:** slow down, ask for definitions and evidence, restate their claim, insist on specifics, and separate the speaker's confidence from their proof. See [counter-strategies.md](counter-strategies.md) and [intelligence-tradecraft.md](intelligence-tradecraft.md).

## Rhetoric in Different Settings

**Writing:** Lead with the conclusion. Short paragraphs, concrete words, an ask. See [writing.md](writing.md).

**Meetings and organizations:** Pre-wire key people before the meeting; make your recommendation up front; show the decision criteria; anticipate objections. See [power-dynamics.md](power-dynamics.md) on coalitions and agenda.

**Debate and public disagreement:** Define the question, speak to the audience (often not the opponent), concede true points quickly (it builds credibility), keep calm. Calm is persuasive.

**One-on-one:** Listen first; most persuasion is understanding. Tell a story. Make it easy to say yes.

**Under hostile questioning:** See the Q&A protocol in [tactics/public-speaking.md](tactics/public-speaking.md).

## The Ethics of Persuasion

The power to move people carries obligation.

- **Persuade; don't deceive.** The test: would your audience still be persuaded if they saw exactly what you were doing and why?
- **Use emotion that fits the facts.** Fear-mongering and outrage-farming are cheap and corrosive.
- **Respect the audience's autonomy.** Give them what they need to decide, including the strongest counterarguments.
- **Be the same in private and public.** Ethos is the sum of your conduct.
- **Don't win at the cost of truth.** A debate victory over a falsehood you know is true is a loss of character.

Cicero's ideal orator was *vir bonus dicendi peritus*, a good man skilled in speaking. The order matters.

## Training Protocol

1. **Daily:** Write one persuasive paragraph making a case for something you believe, then one making the best case for the opposite. This builds steelmanning and breadth.
2. **Weekly:** Deliver a 2-minute argument aloud, recorded. Review for clarity, pace, filler words. See [tactics/public-speaking.md](tactics/public-speaking.md).
3. **Monthly:** Study one great speech: read it, mark the structure, devices, and appeals, and copy a passage.
4. **Practice in the wild:** Toastmasters-style groups, debate clubs, volunteering to present, writing publicly.
5. **Analyze arguments you read:** Find the claim, grounds, and warrant. Spot the weakest link.
6. **Argue the other side** on topics you care about until you can state it well.
