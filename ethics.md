# Ethics

Ethics is not a seminar subject. It is what you do when the rules run out, nobody is watching, and the right move costs you something.

Most moral failure is not villainy. It is decent people sliding: an incentive that bends judgment, a small compromise that resets the baseline, a plausible story about why this case is different. You don't need more moral opinions. You need better instruments and a trained habit of using them before the pressure arrives.

This file is the working machinery: the lenses, the tests, the failure modes, and a protocol for hard calls. [principles.md](principles.md) makes integrity the top value. [warrior-codes.md](warrior-codes.md) gives the ideal of honor: a private standard you hold yourself to, not a reputation you manage. This is how you keep it when it gets expensive.

## Three Lenses, Not Three Religions

Philosophers have argued for centuries about which moral theory is correct. You don't have to settle it. Use the three major traditions as lenses: each sees something the others miss. A choice that passes all three is almost certainly sound. One that fails all three is almost certainly wrong. The hard cases live where they disagree, and that is where judgment earns its keep.

**Consequentialism: what happens?** (Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill.) The right act produces the best outcomes for everyone affected. Bentham built it on the greatest happiness principle. Mill refined it, arguing some pleasures are higher than others: "better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."
- *What it sees:* real effects on real people. It counts everyone, including strangers, and won't let you hide behind clean hands while your inaction hurts someone.
- *What it misses:* rights and justice. Pure outcome math can justify sacrificing one innocent to save five. It also pretends to predictive precision you rarely have, which makes it the favorite tool of rationalization.

**Deontology: what is the duty?** (Immanuel Kant.) Some acts are right or wrong in themselves, whatever the outcome. Two of Kant's formulations of the categorical imperative: act only on a principle you could will as a universal law, and treat people never merely as a means but always also as ends in themselves.
- *What it sees:* dignity, rights, promises, the wrongness of using people. It gives you bright lines that hold under pressure.
- *What it misses:* conflicting duties and catastrophe. Kant notoriously held that you may not lie even to a murderer at the door asking where your friend is hiding. Rules applied without judgment can become cruelty.

**Virtue ethics: who are you becoming?** (Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*.) The question is less which act than what kind of person. Virtues are trained dispositions, and each sits at a mean between two vices: courage between cowardice and recklessness. The master virtue is *phronesis*, practical wisdom, the ability to see what this situation actually calls for.
- *What it sees:* character, habit, the long arc. Most ethical life isn't dilemmas; it's thousands of small acts that make you someone. This is the lens closest to the warrior tradition.
- *What it misses:* specific guidance in a novel hard case. "Do what the virtuous person would do" is circular when you don't know what that is.

**The working synthesis:** virtue ethics is your operating mode (build character so the right act is habitual), deontology is your guardrail (lines you don't cross for any payoff), consequentialism is your audit (who actually gets hurt, who benefits, and how much).

## Intuition First, Reasoning Second

Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model (2001, "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail") argues that moral judgments mostly arrive as fast intuitions, and reasoning usually comes afterward to justify them. Your reasoning behaves more like a lawyer than a judge.

**Moral Foundations Theory** (Haidt, Jesse Graham, Craig Joseph and colleagues) proposes that people draw on several intuitive foundations: **care**, **fairness**, **loyalty**, **authority**, **sanctity**, and **liberty** (added in *The Righteous Mind*, 2012). In their research, self-identified liberals lean mainly on care and fairness; conservatives draw more evenly across all of them. The theory is influential but contested, and the list keeps being revised: a 2023 version of the questionnaire splits fairness into equality and proportionality and treats liberty as a candidate. Use it as a map of the terrain, not a settled taxonomy.

Joshua Greene (*Moral Tribes*, 2013) offers a complementary dual-process view: fast gut reactions work well for everyday cooperation within a group; slow, deliberate reasoning is needed when groups with different intuitions collide.

What this means in practice:
1. **Your intuitions are data, not verdicts.** The flash of "this is wrong" often encodes real wisdom. Sometimes it encodes tribal loyalty, disgust, or bias. Ask which.
2. **Your reasoning is a press secretary.** When you find a brilliant argument for doing exactly what you already wanted to do, raise your suspicion, not your confidence.
3. **Most moral disagreement is foundation mismatch.** Your opponent is usually weighting a different foundation, not choosing evil. If you want to move someone, argue in the language of the foundation they care about. See [rhetoric.md](rhetoric.md).

## The Practical Tests

Fast filters. Run several. When they converge, act. When one fails, stop and look harder.

1. **The front-page test.** Warren Buffett's standard when he took over Salomon Brothers in 1991: would you be comfortable seeing this act reported on the front page of your local paper, by an informed and critical reporter, read by your spouse, children, and friends? It catches what you would hide. Limit: it tests shame, not rightness. Some right acts are unpopular.
2. **The reversal test.** Would you accept this if it were done to you, in their position, with their information? Some version of the Golden Rule appears across many traditions, from Confucius to Hillel to the Gospels. Refinement: ask what *they* would want, not what you would want in their shoes.
3. **The universalizability test.** What if everyone in my position did this? If the practice only works because others don't do it (lying, free-riding, cutting the line), it's parasitic.
4. **The veil of ignorance.** John Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971): design the rule as if you didn't know which seat you'd occupy: boss or junior, seller or buyer, strong or weak. Best for policies, team rules, family rules, and deal structures.
5. **The exemplar test.** What would the person you most admire do? Name a specific person, ideally someone you've known. Virtue ethics in a single question.
6. **The explanation test.** Could you explain your reasoning, out loud, to the person most affected? If you'd need to spin or hide it, you have your answer.
7. **The future-self test.** Will you respect this decision in ten years, or at the end? See [mortality-integration.md](mortality-integration.md).

These are filters, not proofs. Passing all of them is strong evidence. Failing any one is a reason to slow down.

## Integrity: Closing the Gap

Integrity shares a root with *integer*: whole, undivided. One person in every room. Nearly everyone endorses honesty and fairness on paper. The difference between people is the gap between stated values and behavior under pressure.

**Measure the gap.** Your real values show up in your calendar, your bank statement, and what you do when doing right costs something. If you say family comes first and give it the dregs of your energy, your operating value is something else. See [self-knowledge.md](self-knowledge.md).

**Decide your lines in calm.** Clayton Christensen (*How Will You Measure Your Life?*, 2012) argued that it is easier to hold a principle 100 percent of the time than 98 percent. Once "just this once" is permitted, every future case becomes a candidate exception. Write your bright lines down before the moment that tests them.

**Honor without the costume.** In the warrior codes, honor was never mainly about reputation. It was being someone whose word needs no contract, and whom death could not shame. Reputation follows from that. Chasing reputation directly produces image management, which is the opposite of honor.

## Moral Courage

Knowing the right thing is the easy half. [courage.md](courage.md) covers the training. The ethical specifics:

**Most ethical failure is silence.** Darley and Latané's bystander research (1968) found that people are less likely to help when others are present and responsibility feels shared. (The Kitty Genovese case that inspired it was exaggerated in the original news reporting, but the lab findings stand.) In any group, assume everyone is waiting for someone else.

**Authority bends ordinary people.** In Stanley Milgram's best-known obedience experiment (1963), about two-thirds of participants continued to the maximum shock level when an experimenter insisted. Later scholars have questioned parts of his procedure and how many participants believed it was real, but a 2009 partial replication by Jerry Burger found obedience rates only somewhat lower than Milgram's at the comparable point. The lesson holds: you will defer to authority more than you predict.

**One ally changes everything.** In Solomon Asch's conformity studies, a single dissenting partner sharply reduced conformity to a wrong majority. Find your one. Be someone else's.

Rushworth Kidder (*Moral Courage*, 2005) framed moral courage as the meeting of three things: commitment to principle, awareness of the danger, and willingness to endure it. Take away any one and you have something else: principle without endurance folds, endurance without principle is stubbornness, and action blind to the danger is luck.

## Right vs. Right: Competing Duties

The hardest cases are not right vs. wrong. They are right vs. right. Kidder (*How Good People Make Tough Choices*, 1995) named four recurring patterns:
- **Truth vs. loyalty:** your friend's affair, your colleague's padded expenses
- **Individual vs. community:** your team member's need vs. the team's
- **Short-term vs. long-term:** this quarter vs. the company's trust
- **Justice vs. mercy:** the rule vs. this person's circumstances

W.D. Ross (*The Right and the Good*, 1930) offered the most useful structure: we have several *prima facie* duties (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, self-improvement), each binding unless outweighed by a stronger one in the situation. No formula ranks them. Judgment does.

**Handling right vs. right:**
1. **Name both duties out loud.** "I owe him loyalty. I owe her the truth."
2. **Hunt for the third option.** Often you can be loyal to the person and honest about the act: tell your friend privately, first, before anyone else, and give them the chance to come clean.
3. **Weight vulnerability and irreversibility.** Loyalty is owed to persons, not to their wrongdoing. The closer the harm to vulnerable third parties, and the harder it is to undo, the more honesty wins.
4. **Own the residue.** Even the right choice can leave a duty unmet. Acknowledge it to the person you couldn't serve. Repair where you can. See [forgiveness-and-apology.md](forgiveness-and-apology.md).

## The Slippery Slope Is Real

Large failures are built from small ones. The mechanisms are well described:

- **Ethical fading** (Ann Tenbrunsel and David Messick, 2004): the ethical dimension drops out of view, and the decision gets reframed as merely a business, legal, or technical question.
- **Moral disengagement** (Albert Bandura): the mental moves that let decent people do harm without feeling it. Moral justification, euphemistic labeling, advantageous comparison ("others do worse"), displacement and diffusion of responsibility, minimizing consequences, dehumanizing or blaming the victim.
- **Normalization of deviance** (Diane Vaughan, *The Challenger Launch Decision*, 1996): each accepted deviation resets what counts as normal, until the unacceptable looks routine.
- **Moral licensing:** having done good, people may feel entitled to lapse. The evidence is mixed and the effect appears smaller than early studies suggested, but it's worth watching in yourself.

**Defenses:**
1. **Listen for euphemism.** When you need a softer word for what you're doing, you've found the problem.
2. **Treat "just this once" as an alarm**, not a permission.
3. **Count small compromises**, not only big decisions. In your evening review: where did I shade the truth, break a small promise, or stay silent? See [stoic-practice.md](stoic-practice.md).

## Power and Incentives Bend Everyone

Lord Acton, in an 1887 letter: "Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely." Dacher Keltner (*The Power Paradox*, 2016) summarizes research suggesting that feeling powerful tends to reduce attention to others and loosen restraint. Individual studies vary and not all have replicated cleanly, but the direction matches history.

Upton Sinclair: "It is difficult to get a man to understand something, when his salary depends upon his not understanding it."

Incentives do quiet work. In the Wells Fargo scandal that became public in 2016, aggressive sales targets led employees to open millions of accounts customers never authorized. That is not a story about a few bad people. It is what happens when a system rewards the wrong number and nobody checks how it's being hit.

1. **Assume your incentives are bending your judgment.** Name them, to yourself first.
2. **The more power you hold, the less truth reaches you.** Deliberately build people who will tell you no. See [leadership.md](leadership.md).
3. **Design incentives assuming they'll be gamed.** Goodhart's law: once a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring well.
4. **Before you take power, decide what you won't do with it.**

## The Ethics of Influence

Negotiation, sales, leadership, parenting: all influence. The question is not whether to influence but how.

**The line: persuasion works through a person's judgment; manipulation works around it.**

1. **Disclosure test:** would the technique still work if they knew exactly what you were doing? Honest argument survives disclosure. Most manipulation doesn't.
2. **Interest test:** does the outcome serve them too? Would they thank you in a year?
3. **Truth test:** are all factual claims true? Keeping your walk-away point private is fair; inventing a competing offer is not. See [negotiation.md](negotiation.md).
4. **Vulnerability test:** are you leaning on grief, fear, loneliness, addiction, or confusion? The more vulnerable the person, the higher the bar.
5. **Reversal:** would you feel respected if someone did this to you?

Learn the full arsenal so you can defend against it ([dark-psychology.md](dark-psychology.md), [power-dynamics.md](power-dynamics.md)). Use only what passes these tests ([sales.md](sales.md) draws the same line in commerce).

## The Hard-Decision Protocol

For choices with real moral weight. Thirty minutes to a few days.

1. **Name it.** Write: "This is an ethical decision because ___." This alone counters ethical fading.
2. **Get the facts.** Many dilemmas dissolve with better information. Separate what you know from what you assume.
3. **List every stakeholder,** especially those not in the room and least able to protect themselves.
4. **Name the competing duties.** Truth vs. loyalty, individual vs. community, short vs. long, justice vs. mercy.
5. **Run the three lenses.** Consequences: who gains, who loses, how much. Duties: what promises, rights, lines are in play. Character: who does this make me?
6. **Run the tests.** Front page, reversal, universalizability, veil, exemplar, explanation.
7. **Check your state and your incentives.** Angry, afraid, exhausted, or paid to see it one way? Wait if you can. See [anger.md](anger.md).
8. **Search for the third option** that honors more of the duties.
9. **Consult one person** with sound judgment and no stake in the outcome.
10. **Decide, act, and own it,** including the cost.
11. **Repair the residue.** Tell anyone you let down what you chose and why.
12. **Log it** with a review date in your [decision journal](decision-journal.md).

**Keep a bright-lines list:** five to seven things you will not do regardless of payoff. Examples: I don't lie about material facts. I don't take credit for others' work. I don't use someone's vulnerability against them. I don't sign what I haven't read. Review it quarterly. Add to it every time you catch yourself near an edge.
