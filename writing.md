# Writing

Writing is not communication. Communication is what writing enables. Writing itself is thinking — the specific kind of thinking that occurs when you force language to become precise enough to survive contact with the page.

The test: think about a subject you believe you understand. Then write about it for fifteen minutes without stopping. The gaps, the hedges, the sentences that trail off, the moments where you reach for a word and find nothing there — that is the real map of what you know. Writing is the diagnostic tool that reveals what is actually understood versus what is held in vague, inarticulate suspension.

This is why the clearest thinkers of history were almost universally writers. Not because writing was their medium — but because writing *is* thinking at a particular level of rigor, and there is no shortcut to that rigor.

---

## Writing as Cognitive Technology

George Orwell, *Politics and the English Language* (1946): "If people cannot write well, they cannot think well, and if they cannot think well, others will do their thinking for them."

This is the central claim. Not that writing is useful for communication — that is secondary. The primary claim is that the inability to write clearly is the inability to think clearly, and that unclear thinking makes a person susceptible to having their thinking done by others.

The mechanism: language is not just the vehicle for thought; it is partially constitutive of thought. Hazy language is hazy thinking. Precise language is precise thinking. The discipline of writing forces the mind to resolve ambiguities that ordinary speech and internal monologue can sustain indefinitely without being noticed.

**The William Zinsser test:** Zinsser, *On Writing Well*: "The secret of good writing is to strip every sentence to its cleanest components. Every word that serves no function, every long word that could be a short word, every adverb that carries the same meaning that's already in the verb, every passive construction that leaves the reader unsure of who is doing what — these are the thousand and one adulterants that weaken the strength of a sentence."

Apply this test to any piece of writing as a test of the underlying thinking: how much survives stripping? What remains is what was actually thought.

## The Daily Writing Practice

The highest-leverage writing practice is not the polished essay or the professional report. It is daily private writing — the journal, the morning pages, the rough daily record.

**Why daily writing produces daily clarity:**
- Forces the processing of accumulated experience before it calcifies into unexamined assumption
- Creates a record for pattern recognition across time — you can see your own thinking evolving, recurring, or stagnating
- Generates the mental models that later appear in professional and creative writing, already formed
- The act of writing down difficult thoughts reduces their emotional charge (James Pennebaker's expressive writing research: 15–20 minutes of writing about difficult events, three days in a row, measurably reduces psychological distress and improves immune function)

**The morning pages protocol (Julia Cameron, *The Artist's Way*):** Three pages of longhand stream-of-consciousness writing, immediately after waking, before engaging with the day's inputs. Not intended to be good. Not intended to be read. Intended to clear the cognitive debris and surface what is actually present in the mind before the day's noise drowns it.

The discipline: write through the resistance. The pages that feel like nothing are not nothing — they are the clearing work that makes the pages that follow useful.

**Journaling as distinct from morning pages:** Where morning pages are a clearing mechanism, journaling is a reflection mechanism — the evening practice of reviewing what happened, what it means, what you would do differently, what you want to preserve. See [routines/daily.md](routines/daily.md).

## Orwell's Six Rules

The most efficient summary of good writing practice ever written:

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. **Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.**

Rule 6 is the essential one — it means these are principles of judgment, not laws. The test is always clarity and honesty, not rule compliance. But the first five rules, followed when they apply, remove most of the fog that obscures most writing.

**The root cause of bad writing (Orwell's deeper argument):** Writers reach for pre-formed phrases and jargon not because it is clearer but because it is easier — it allows the writer to avoid committing to what they actually think. "Leverage synergistic efficiencies" means nothing precisely *because* it cannot be criticized. The vague formulation is the coward's option. Precise writing requires the courage to say something specific that could be specifically wrong.

## The Essay as a Thinking Form

Michel de Montaigne invented the personal essay in the 16th century. *Essais* means "attempts" — the essay is an attempt, an exploration, not a conclusion. Its structural principle is thinking in public — following an idea wherever it leads, including into contradiction and uncertainty, with the honest admission that the writer is discovering rather than reporting.

This form is underused. Most professional and academic writing requires the conclusion first — the thesis, then the argument. But the thinking that matters most often happens in the essay form: "I'm not sure about X. Let me think through it here." The thinking is the point. The conclusion is where the thinking landed — but next month it might land somewhere else.

**The essay as a regular practice:** Once per week or once per month, write 500–1500 words on a subject that genuinely unsettles or interests you. No audience required. No pressure to conclude. The goal is to find out what you think — and to find where your thinking breaks down.

**Format:**
1. Open with the question or unresolved tension — not a thesis, a question
2. Think through the strongest case for each side or possibility
3. Find where the analysis pulls you and follow it honestly
4. End with where you landed and what remains unresolved

The person who does this consistently builds a private library of examined thinking. Over years, that library becomes a resource — a set of worked-out positions on real questions that can be accessed and expressed when they become relevant.

## The Voice Question

Every writer of substance has a voice — a set of characteristic rhythms, preferences, habits of mind, and ways of seeing that make their writing recognizable across subject matter. The voice is not style applied from the outside; it is the interior self expressed through language.

Voice is developed, not chosen. It emerges from:
- Volume: the person who has written millions of words has discovered, through iteration, which moves feel natural and which feel imposed
- Reading: the writer's voice is partly a synthesis of the voices they have read most deeply; not imitation but absorption and metabolization
- The gap between taste and ability (Ira Glass's formulation): everyone who writes has taste before they have the skill to match it; the gap is closed only by continuing to write through the gap; the people who stop during the gap never develop the voice

**The imitation exercise:** Take a writer whose voice you admire. Write two pages deliberately imitating their syntax, rhythm, and approach — not their content, their form. This is not plagiarism preparation; it is the way musicians learn by transcribing solos. You internalize the structure of the thing by reproducing it, and that structure then becomes available to your own voice.

## Precision as Respect

Imprecise language is a form of disrespect — to the reader, to the subject, and to the truth. When a writer uses vague language ("kind of," "sort of," "somewhat," "in a way"), they are offloading the work of precision onto the reader. The reader must supply the meaning the writer declined to provide.

The commitment to precision is the commitment to doing the cognitive work before handing the sentence to someone else.

**The precision test for any sentence:** Could this sentence be read to mean something other than what I intend? If yes, revise until no. The revision process is often where the real thinking happens — the sentence that seemed clear turns out to rely on an assumption you hadn't examined.

**Vocabulary as precision:** A large vocabulary is not pretension — it is access to precision. Words that appear to be synonyms rarely are: *angry* and *furious* describe different intensities; *sad* and *melancholic* describe different qualities; *courage* and *bravery* have distinct connotations around danger. The writer who knows these distinctions can say what they mean. The writer who doesn't must gesture.

## Writing and Memory

The act of writing encodes memory differently than the act of experiencing. The person who writes about an experience within 24–48 hours retains it at a qualitatively different level than the person who does not. The writing is not just a record — it is a second processing pass that moves the experience into more accessible, more articulate, more organized memory.

**The practice of writing to remember:**
- After any significant conversation: write a paragraph of what you took from it
- After any significant book: write a one-page synthesis before moving to the next
- After any significant period (a trip, a project, a crisis): write an essay-length reflection before the experience fades to outline
- After any significant decision: write out the reasoning, the alternatives considered, the expected outcome — to be reviewed when the outcome is known

The review is as important as the initial writing. The person who can read what they thought three years ago and compare it to what happened has a feedback mechanism on their own judgment. That feedback mechanism, maintained across decades, produces genuine wisdom rather than merely accumulated experience.

## On Length and Compression

Blaise Pascal's apology in a letter: *"I have made this longer than usual because I have not had time to make it shorter."*

Compression is harder than expansion. Anyone can write more. Saying something precisely in fewer words requires understanding it more deeply — understanding which elements are essential and which are noise.

The compression test: after completing a piece of writing, ask what could be cut without loss of meaning. The answer is almost always "more than I think." The first draft typically contains 20–30% words serving no function — filler transitions, qualifying phrases, redundant examples. Cutting them tightens the argument and tests it: if cutting a sentence collapses the argument, it was doing real work. If cutting it has no effect, it should be cut.

**The one-page principle:** Almost anything can be said in one page. If it cannot, the writer has usually not yet decided what they are trying to say. The constraint of one page forces the selection that reveals the argument. Write long, then cut to one page — what survives is what matters.

## Writing Disciplines

**The daily minimum:** One page, every day. Not polished — generated. The habit of daily generation prevents the paralysis of the blank page and builds the muscle of sitting down and producing even on low-energy days. The days that feel most blocked often produce the most unexpected turns.

**The read-aloud edit:** Every piece of writing should be read aloud before it is finished. The ear catches what the eye misses — awkward rhythm, run-on sentences, words that don't quite fit, logical gaps that need a bridge. If you cannot read it aloud smoothly, you cannot read it smoothly at all. Reading aloud is the most reliable edit pass.

**The cold read:** Return to any significant piece of writing 24–72 hours after completion, before finalizing it. Fresh eyes see the holes, the redundancies, the unclear antecedents, the overconfident claims that cooler assessment would qualify. Nothing survives a cold read exactly as written. This is the read that matters.

**The kill-your-darlings principle (attributed to Faulkner):** The sentences you are most pleased with are the ones most likely to need cutting. They pleased you because you were proud of their cleverness or beauty — which means they served your ego rather than the argument. When a sentence primarily draws attention to itself rather than to what it says, cut it.
