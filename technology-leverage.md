# Technology Leverage

Every era has one dominant lever. For the Roman it was the legion. For the industrialist it was capital and machinery. For this era it is software, and increasingly, AI. The person who uses it well does the work of ten. The person who ignores it competes against people who do.

This file is about using technology as a force multiplier without becoming its instrument. The tool serves the mission. The moment the mission starts serving the tool, you have been captured. See [attention.md](attention.md).

---

## The Three Forms of Leverage

Naval Ravikant's framing, which holds up: wealth and output come from leverage, and leverage comes in three forms.

1. **Labor:** Other people working for you. The oldest form. Requires permission, management, and trust. Capped by human bandwidth.
2. **Capital:** Money working for you. Requires having it first. Unlocks ownership and scale.
3. **Code and media:** Software, content, and ideas that replicate at zero marginal cost. Requires no permission and no one's payroll. A program written once runs for a million users. A book written once is read by a million readers.

The third form is the new one. It is permissionless. A single person with a laptop can build a product, a body of work, or an automation that serves thousands while they sleep. This is the structural shift of the last thirty years, and AI extends it: the cost of turning an idea into a working draft, a prototype, or an analysis has collapsed.

**The implication:** Your output is no longer limited by hours worked. It is limited by judgment, taste, and the quality of what you point the machinery at. These are the human scarce resources, and they are developed through the rest of this playbook.

## Using AI as a Force Multiplier

AI is a capable, fast, tireless junior colleague with uneven reliability. Treat it that way and it multiplies you. Treat it as an oracle and it will confidently mislead you.

### What it does well
- First drafts of anything: writing, code, analysis, outlines. Fast iteration beats a blank page.
- Summarizing and extracting structure from large amounts of text.
- Explaining unfamiliar domains at your level, with unlimited patience and follow-up questions.
- Generating alternatives: ten framings, twenty names, five strategies to critique.
- Playing the opponent: red-teaming your plan, arguing the other side, simulating a skeptical buyer.
- Automating repetitive digital work.

### Where it fails
- Facts stated with confidence that are wrong. Verify anything that matters: numbers, citations, legal, medical, financial claims.
- Taste and judgment. It produces the average of what it has seen. Distinctive work requires your direction and your edit.
- Knowing your context. It does not know your constraints, your history, or what you actually want unless you say so.
- Accountability. You own the output. "The AI said so" is not a defense.

### The operating protocol
1. **Specify before delegating.** The quality of the output tracks the quality of the brief. State the goal, the audience, the constraints, the format, and what good looks like. Vague in, vague out.
2. **Iterate in loops.** First pass is raw material. Critique it, redirect it, tighten it. Three short rounds beat one long prompt.
3. **Verify what matters.** Match checking effort to the cost of being wrong. A brainstorm needs none. A contract clause or a dosage needs primary sources.
4. **Keep the thinking.** Use it to extend your reasoning, not replace it. If you cannot explain and defend the result, you do not understand it, and you should not ship it.
5. **Protect sensitive data.** Know where your inputs go. Do not paste credentials, private client data, or anything you would not put in an email to a stranger unless you know the terms.

### The skill atrophy risk
Outsourcing thinking weakens the faculty. If you let a tool do all your writing, you lose the clarity writing forces (see [writing.md](writing.md)). If you let it do all your problem-solving, you lose the struggle that builds skill (see [learning/skill-building.md](learning/skill-building.md)). Rule: **use it to go further, not to skip the reps that make you capable.** Do the hard part yourself first on anything you are trying to master, then use the tool to check and extend.

## Automation: Buying Back Time

Anything you do more than a few times, on a predictable pattern, is a candidate for a system.

**The automation audit (once per quarter):**
1. List recurring tasks for two weeks: invoices, scheduling, reports, file organization, reminders, data entry, follow-ups.
2. Score each by frequency and time cost.
3. For the top five, ask: can this be eliminated, templated, delegated, or automated, in that order?
4. Build the automation for the highest return. Measure the hours returned.

**Elimination comes first.** Automating a pointless task is efficient waste. Ask whether it should exist before asking how to speed it up.

**Where the leverage is highest:** Money flows (automatic transfers to savings and investments, see [career/finances.md](career/finances.md)), information intake (filtered feeds and digests rather than live streams), scheduling (booking links instead of email ping-pong), and communication templates for recurring situations.

## Learning to Build

You do not need to become a software engineer. You do need enough fluency to turn your own ideas into working things without waiting on anyone.

**Minimum viable technical literacy:**
- Understand how the web, databases, and APIs fit together at a conceptual level.
- Be able to read and lightly modify code, and to use spreadsheets and scripting for your own analysis.
- Be able to build a simple site, tool, or automation, with AI assistance, in a weekend.
- Know enough to evaluate technical claims and spot when someone is selling you complexity.

**The path:** Pick a small real problem you actually have. Build the solution. Ship it to yourself first. Each project teaches more than ten tutorials. Deliberate practice applies: build at the edge of your ability, get fast feedback, repeat. See [learning/skill-building.md](learning/skill-building.md).

## Digital Sovereignty

Leverage you do not control can be taken away. Own your core assets.

- **Own your platform.** Social accounts are rented land. Keep a personal site, an email list, and local copies of what matters. Algorithms and policies change without notice.
- **Own your data.** Back up important files in at least two places, one offsite. Follow the 3-2-1 rule: three copies, two media types, one offsite.
- **Secure your accounts.** A password manager, unique passwords, hardware or app-based two-factor authentication on email, banking, and anything that controls the rest. Your email account is the master key to everything else; protect it first.
- **Minimize single points of failure.** If one vendor disappearing would stop your income or your life, build the second path now. This is [antifragility.md](antifragility.md) applied to infrastructure.
- **Understand the business model.** If a product is free, find out what is being sold. Usually it is your attention or your data. See [epistemic-self-defense.md](epistemic-self-defense.md).

## Technology and the Self

The same tools that multiply capability can hollow out the person using them.

**The rules of engagement:**
1. **Tools on a schedule, not on a stream.** Batch email, messages, and news. Constant availability is a tax on [attention](attention.md).
2. **No device in the first hour or last hour of the day.** The first hour sets the tone of the day; the last one shapes your sleep. See [reality-engineering.md](reality-engineering.md).
3. **Consume less, create more.** The ratio of output to input is a health metric. If you are consuming ten times what you produce, you are a spectator of your own life.
4. **Protect real-world presence.** Phone away during meals, conversations, and training. The people in front of you are the scarce resource. See [friendship.md](friendship.md).
5. **Periodic fasts.** One day per week or one week per quarter with minimal screens recalibrates what normal feels like and exposes dependence you cannot see from inside it.

## The Strategic View

Technology shifts reward the early and the adaptable, and punish the complacent. You do not need to chase every trend. You need to:

1. **Track the frontier lightly.** A fixed weekly slot to read, test new tools, and note what changed. Do not let it become a feed habit.
2. **Experiment cheaply.** Try new tools on small, low-stakes tasks. Adopt what proves out. Drop the rest.
3. **Build skills that compound across shifts.** Clear thinking, writing, sales, judgment, and relationships stay valuable whatever the tools do. Tool-specific skills depreciate. Invest in the durable layer first. See [force-multipliers.md](force-multipliers.md).
4. **Position where the human part matters.** As routine cognitive work gets automated, the premium moves to judgment, trust, taste, leadership, and physical-world presence. Build there.

The sequence: durable human skills first, leverage tools second, and the discipline to stay the master of both.
