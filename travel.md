# Travel

Most travel today is consumption. A list of sights, a feed of photos, a comfortable bubble moved from one country to another. You come home tanned, tired and unchanged.

Travel done right is education and initiation. It puts you where your defaults stop working: the language, the food, the rules, the pace. You have to adapt, and the adapting is the point. Mark Twain, closing *The Innocents Abroad* (1869): "Travel is fatal to prejudice, bigotry, and narrow-mindedness."

This file covers how to travel to learn, how to plan and stay safe, and how to bring the trip home so it actually changes something.

---

## Tourist vs. Traveler

In *The Sheltering Sky*, Paul Bowles draws the line partly in terms of time: the tourist hurries home after a few weeks; the traveler moves slowly and belongs no more to one place than to the next. You don't need years. The difference is mostly posture.

| Tourist | Traveler |
|---------|----------|
| Collects sights | Seeks encounters |
| The itinerary drives | Curiosity drives, with structure underneath |
| Stays in the bubble | Enters local life |
| Photographs | Observes, asks, writes |
| Avoids discomfort | Treats discomfort as tuition |
| Brings home souvenirs | Brings home changed defaults |

No snobbery. Sometimes you just need a beach and a book. Call that rest, not travel, and know which one you're doing.

**Depth beats breadth.** William Maddux and Adam Galinsky (2009) found that living abroad, not merely traveling abroad, was associated with greater creativity, and that adapting to the local culture seemed to be what mattered. The research is correlational, but the lesson is practical: the value is in engagement, not in passport stamps.

## Slow Travel

1. **Fewer places, longer stays.** One city for a week teaches more than five cities in a week.
2. **Live in a neighborhood.** Rent an apartment, shop at the same market, become a regular at one café. Being recognized is the first step toward belonging.
3. **Move overland when you can.** Trains, buses, ferries. The country between the highlights is the country.
4. **Leave unscheduled days.** Serendipity needs slack.
5. **A working rule:** at least three nights per stop, and no more than one major sight per day.

## Solo Travel

Alone, every decision is yours and so is every problem. That is what makes it valuable.

**What it builds:** self-reliance, decisiveness, the nerve to start conversations with strangers or eat alone without a phone as a shield, and comfort in your own company ([solitude.md](solitude.md)). Without a companion to retreat into, you are more open to encounters, and more people approach you.

**How to do it well:** share your itinerary and agree on check-in times with someone at home. Arrive in new places in daylight. Stay sober enough to stay sharp. Trust the discomfort signal and leave. Risks vary by destination and by who you are; read official advisories and recent accounts from travelers like you.

## How to Travel Deeply

1. **Learn the basics of the language before you land.** Hello, please, thank you, excuse me, sorry, the numbers one to ten, "how much?", "where is...?", "I don't understand." Effort earns goodwill far beyond your skill level.
2. **Read before you go.** One history, one novel by a local author, one piece on current affairs. Context turns sights into meaning. See [learning/reading-system.md](learning/reading-system.md).
3. **Eat local.** Markets, street food from busy stalls with high turnover, places with locals in the queue. Eat what's regional and in season. Food is the most open door into any culture.
4. **Talk to locals.** Ask questions people enjoy answering: What should I eat here? What do you love about this place? What do visitors always get wrong? Barbers, shopkeepers, drivers and bartenders are good sources.
5. **Walk.** Places reveal themselves at walking speed. Get lost on purpose, in safe areas.
6. **Do what locals do.** A football match, a public bath, a festival, a service, a local gym or martial arts class ([martial-arts.md](martial-arts.md)).
7. **Journal every day.** Ten minutes: what I saw, what surprised me, what I misunderstood, one conversation. The surprise is the lesson; it marks where your model of the world was wrong. See [writing.md](writing.md).
8. **Ration the phone.** Photos are fine; the feed is not. Try one full day without taking a single picture.

## Cultural Humility

You are a guest. Act like one.

- **Learn the norms before you arrive:** dress at religious sites, shoes, tipping, gestures, greetings, alcohol, public affection, photographing people (ask first).
- **Local law applies to you.** Drug, alcohol, speech, photography and morality laws differ, and penalties for drug offenses in some countries are severe. Some common prescription medications are restricted in some countries. Your embassy can offer limited consular help; it cannot get you out of a foreign jail.
- **Understand before you judge.** When something seems strange, ask why it's done that way. There is usually a reason.
- **Don't turn people into scenery.** No poverty tourism, no photographs of people without consent.
- **Spend locally.** Local guides, family-run guesthouses, neighborhood restaurants.
- **Watch your reactions.** Irritation at difference is information about you. See [shadow-work.md](shadow-work.md).

## Planning and Logistics

**Documents**
- **Passport:** check the expiry date and blank pages. Many countries require validity for some months beyond your stay; six months is a common rule.
- **Visas and electronic travel authorizations** (for example the US ESTA or UK ETA): check the destination government's official site. Avoid third-party sites that add fees.
- **Copies:** passport, visas, insurance and bookings stored offline on your phone and in secure cloud storage, plus one paper copy kept separate from the originals.
- **Register with your government** where possible. US citizens: STEP, the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program.
- **Driving:** carry an International Driving Permit where it's required.

**Insurance**
- Buy travel insurance with medical cover and medical evacuation. Many domestic health plans don't cover you abroad, and US Medicare generally doesn't. Evacuation can cost tens of thousands of dollars.
- Read the exclusions: adventure sports, motorbikes, alcohol-related incidents, pre-existing conditions.

**Health**
- See your doctor or a travel clinic ideally a month or more before you leave. Check CDC Travelers' Health (or your country's equivalent) for destination vaccines and malaria advice.
- Carry medications in original packaging with copies of prescriptions, and confirm they're legal where you're going.
- Pack a small medical kit. Follow local guidance on tap water.

**Money**
- Carry two cards on different networks, stored separately. Know your foreign transaction fees.
- Use ATMs inside banks where possible. When a card machine offers to charge you in your home currency, decline and pay in local currency; the conversion rate is usually worse.
- Keep emergency cash hidden apart from your wallet.

**Phone and data**
- Arrange roaming, an eSIM or a local SIM. Download offline maps and translation packs.
- Make sure you can still receive two-factor codes abroad.
- Carry a power bank, the right plug adapter, and key numbers written on paper.

## Safety

**Official advisories first.** US State Department travel advisories (Levels 1 to 4), UK FCDO travel advice, Canada's travel.gc.ca, Australia's Smartraveller. Read the region-specific guidance, not just the country headline.

**Situational awareness.** Relaxed alertness, not paranoia. Don't look lost; step into a shop or café to check your map. Know your exits. Full framework: [tactics/field.md](tactics/field.md).

**Transport.** Use official taxis or reputable ride apps, check the plate, sit in the back.

**Nights.** A lot of travel trouble starts late, with alcohol. Watch your drink, watch your count, know how you're getting home.

**Common scams:**
- **"It's closed today."** A friendly stranger says the sight is closed and steers you to a shop or a tour.
- **The broken meter.** Agree the fare or insist on the meter before you get in.
- **The gift.** A bracelet tied on your wrist or a "free" sprig of rosemary, then a demand for payment.
- **The clipboard or the spill.** A petition, a map, or something spilled on your clothes while an accomplice works your pockets.
- **Fake police** asking to inspect your wallet or passport. Ask for ID and offer to go to the station.
- **The bar invitation.** A charming new friend, a drink or tea ceremony, and a staggering bill.
- **ATM tricks.** Skimmers and helpful strangers at the machine.

**The pattern:** unsolicited help plus urgency plus friendliness that leads toward money. Smile and walk away.

**Your emergency plan:** embassy contact, insurer's hotline, and the local emergency number (it isn't always 911; across the EU it's 112). Split your valuables. See [preparedness.md](preparedness.md).

## Jet Lag Basics

Switch to local time immediately. Get bright light in the local morning, avoid long naps on arrival, keep caffeine early, and expect eastward trips to be harder than westward. The full protocol, including low-dose melatonin, is in [health/sleep.md](health/sleep.md).

## Budget Approaches

- **Flights and lodging are the expensive part.** The most valuable parts (walking, markets, conversations) are cheap.
- **Travel in shoulder season,** stay flexible on dates, go overland.
- **Use guesthouses, hostels, house-sitting or work exchanges** (check the visa rules before you work for room and board).
- **Set a daily budget and track it.**
- **Spend on what compounds:** a local guide for a day, a cooking class, a language lesson. Save on what doesn't: souvenirs, a luxury room you only sleep in.
- **Never go into debt to travel.** See [career/finances.md](career/finances.md).

## Adventure and Pilgrimage

Some trips are meant to change you. Design them that way.

**Pilgrimage** is walking to a meaningful place with intention: the Camino de Santiago in Spain, the 88-temple pilgrimage on Shikoku or the Kumano Kodo in Japan. Religious or not, the walking does the work: long days, simple needs, the same strangers met again and again, nothing to do but move and think. To earn the Compostela certificate in Santiago, you walk at least the final 100 km.

**Adventure** is a trip at the edge of your competence: a long trek, a river descent, a cycle tour, a month of language immersion. For wilderness specifically, see [nature-and-awe.md](nature-and-awe.md).

**Design it as a rite of passage** ([transformation-science.md](transformation-science.md)):
1. A question you carry from the start.
2. A real challenge.
3. A vow for the duration: no social media, a daily journal, a phrase a day.
4. A deliberate ending: a ritual, a letter to yourself, a debrief.

## Integrating the Trip

Jeroen Nawijn and colleagues (2010) found that Dutch vacationers were happier than non-vacationers before their trips, largely from anticipation, but after returning most were no happier than people who hadn't gone; only very relaxing trips produced a brief boost. Trips fade fast unless you carry them home on purpose.

1. **Within a week of returning,** complete the post-trip reflection below.
2. **Import one habit:** a dish, a pace, an evening walk, a way of greeting people, a ritual.
3. **Keep one thread alive:** fifteen minutes of the language a day, a letter to someone you met, a book from the region.
4. **Reread your travel journal** during your [annual review](routines/annual-review.md).

## Pre-Trip Checklist

- [ ] Passport valid well beyond your return date, with blank pages
- [ ] Visa or electronic authorization checked on the official site
- [ ] Official advisory read for every region; registered with STEP or equivalent
- [ ] Travel insurance with medical and evacuation cover; policy number saved
- [ ] Travel clinic or CDC check done; vaccines, prescriptions and copies sorted
- [ ] Document copies stored offline, in secure cloud, and on paper
- [ ] Two cards plus local cash; fees known; emergency stash hidden
- [ ] Phone plan or eSIM, offline maps, translation app, power bank, adapter
- [ ] Itinerary shared; check-in schedule agreed
- [ ] Emergency numbers saved: embassy, insurer, local emergency services
- [ ] Laws and customs checked: dress, drugs, medications, photography
- [ ] Basic phrases learned; one book about the place read
- [ ] Home handled: bills, mail, keys, pets, plants
- [ ] Intention written: what do I want to learn on this trip?

## Post-Trip Reflection Template

1. **Where did I go, and what did I actually do?** Facts only.
2. **Three moments I'll still remember in ten years.**
3. **What surprised me? Which assumption turned out wrong?**
4. **Who did I meet, and what did they teach me?**
5. **What did I see done better than at home?**
6. **How did I handle friction:** tired, lost, misunderstood, ripped off?
7. **One habit I'm bringing home,** and the date I start.
8. **One relationship or practice I'll keep alive.**
9. **What I'd do differently next time.**
10. **The question for the next trip.**
