# Heuristics

Rules of thumb that work 80%+ of the time. Faster than full frameworks. Use when you need a quick read.

Format: **Rule** — *Why it works.*

---

## People

- **First impression rule:** How someone treats service staff is who they are. — *Service staff have no power to reward or punish, so behavior toward them is unconstrained by incentive. It reveals the baseline.*

- **Belief rule:** When someone shows you who they are the first time, believe them. — *First-time behavior is usually unguarded. People reveal more before they've calibrated their presentation to your expectations.*

- **Words vs. actions:** Watch what people do, not what they say they'll do. — *Intentions are cheap to state and costly to execute. Behavior is the only variable that actually predicts behavior.*

- **Consistency is the tell:** Unreliable people are reliably unreliable. Trust the pattern, not the exception. — *Character expresses itself as pattern, not incident. One exception doesn't override twenty data points.*

- **The third story:** In any conflict, there's your side, their side, and what actually happened. Assume you don't have the full picture. — *You have access to your own internal state and not theirs. The asymmetry produces systematic distortion in your version.*

- **Status signals:** People who need to tell you they're important usually aren't. — *Genuine status doesn't require announcing. People with real standing have learned that it speaks for itself.*

- **Energy audit:** After spending time with someone, do you feel charged or drained? That's data. — *Your nervous system tracks relational dynamics below conscious threshold. The gut read is often correct and worth surfacing.*

- **Motive heuristic:** Before accepting advice, ask: what does this person want from this situation? — *Advice is never disinterested. Identifying the advisor's stake helps you weight their recommendation accurately.*

- **Escalation heuristic:** Someone who escalates small conflicts will escalate large ones. The behavior is the pattern, not the exception. — *Conflict escalation is a habit of nervous system regulation, not a rational response to stakes. The stimulus size changes; the response pattern doesn't.*

- **The loyalty test:** Watch how someone talks about their enemies, not their friends. — *Malice toward the out-group with warmth toward the in-group is the oldest signal of tribal rather than principled behavior. You will eventually be in the out-group.*

- **Generosity signal:** Notice who celebrates your wins without a hint of resentment. Those people are rare and worth keeping. — *Most people experience others' success as implicit commentary on their own. Genuine celebration of others requires security. It is uncommon and diagnostic.*

- **Slowness to anger:** The person who is slow to anger in low-stakes situations can be trusted in high-stakes ones. — *Emotional regulation is a resource. People who exhaust it on small provocations have none left when it counts. The inverse is also true.*

- **Reciprocity baseline:** Relationships that are consistently asymmetric will eventually crack. Note who initiates and who follows up. — *Human relationships have an implicit accounting system. Long-running imbalances generate resentment even when neither party is consciously tracking.*

- **The crisis reveal:** You learn more about a person in their first crisis than in their first five years. — *Crisis strips away the managed presentation. What is left is what the person actually is, not what they are willing to perform.*

- **Proxy test:** Someone who makes small promises and keeps them reliably will keep large ones. — *Reliability is not calibrated to stakes — it is a trait expressed at all levels. Small commitments honored consistently are the best predictor of large ones.*

- **The victim narrative:** When someone casts themselves as the victim in every story they tell, believe the pattern, not the individual stories. — *Repeated victimhood narrative is not usually evidence of repeated bad luck. It is a stable cognitive habit that organizes experience around powerlessness.*

## Decisions

- **The 2-minute rule:** If it takes less than 2 minutes, do it now. — *Small tasks left undone accumulate into cognitive overhead that costs far more attention than the tasks themselves.*

- **The overnight test:** If you're unsure, sleep on it. Almost nothing is as urgent as it feels in the moment. — *Emotional valence of a situation diminishes overnight; logical structure remains. The gap between night and morning decision often reveals whether urgency is real or manufactured.*

- **The "tell a friend" test:** If you'd be embarrassed to explain this decision to someone you respect, reconsider. — *Imagined third-party perspective activates a different part of your evaluative system than first-person. It often surfaces the thing you were avoiding.*

- **The reversibility test:** Reversible decisions → move fast. Irreversible decisions → slow down, get more data. — *The cost of error in reversible decisions is correction time. The cost of error in irreversible decisions is permanent. Calibrate deliberation to stakes.*

- **The subtraction test:** Before adding something, ask if removing something would solve the problem. — *Most systems fail from accumulation, not from deficiency. Addition feels like action; subtraction requires more insight and produces better outcomes.*

- **Default to no:** When unsure, the default answer is no. Let the people and projects worth your yes prove it. — *Attention and time are finite. The cost of a mistaken yes is paid in resource diversion. The cost of a mistaken no is usually just a missed opportunity, which is recoverable.*

- **The 10x rule:** If you can't find at least one reason the opposite is true, you haven't thought hard enough. — *Strong confirmation bias masquerades as thorough analysis. Forcing yourself to generate the counterargument breaks the loop.*

- **The premortem:** Before committing to a major decision, ask: if this fails in 12 months, what will have caused it? — *Prospective hindsight is more accurate than prospective prediction. The failure scenario surfaces risks your optimism is suppressing.*

- **The cost of optionality:** Keeping all doors open is a decision. It costs the compounding you would have gotten from closing one. — *Optionality is not free. It has a maintenance cost in attention and a compounding cost in uncommitted resources. The open door you're not walking through is still costing you.*

- **Strong opinions, weakly held:** Commit to a position firmly enough to act, then update immediately if evidence contradicts it. — *Uncertainty is not a license for inaction. Provisional commitment produces the action that generates the evidence that refines the view.*

- **Sunk cost divorce:** What you've already spent is irrelevant to whether you should continue. Ask only whether the future looks worthwhile from here. — *Sunk costs are psychologically real and economically irrelevant. Conflating them produces the most common category of bad decision: staying in something bad because leaving feels like waste.*

- **Regret minimization:** When genuinely unsure, ask which choice you'd regret more at age 80. Then do that one. — *Present emotion is a noisy signal. Projected regret, properly imagined, often reveals a clearer preference because it strips away short-term fear.*

- **The forcing function:** Set a deadline for the decision, even an arbitrary one. Decisions under time pressure are usually better than perpetually deferred ones. — *Open-ended deliberation does not converge on a better answer — it converges on analysis paralysis. A deadline forces the question.*

- **The complexity tax:** Complicated plans fail more often than simple ones. Prefer the simpler path even when it feels less sophisticated. — *Each dependency in a plan is a failure point. Complexity multiplies failure modes exponentially.*

## Risk

- **Bet size rule:** Never bet what you can't afford to lose, even on a sure thing. — *The sure thing fails often enough. The only variable you fully control is size. Size determines whether a loss is survivable.*

- **Tail risk:** It's not the average outcome that kills you — it's the worst-case. Model that first. — *Expected value calculations are dominated by tail events in fat-tailed distributions. The right question is not "what usually happens?" but "what happens when it fails badly?"*

- **Concentration warning:** When everyone agrees something is safe, ask what they're missing. — *Consensus about safety is often the last phase before the systemic failure that consensus made possible. Risk that no one is pricing is being accumulated.*

- **Skin in the game:** Only take advice from people who bear consequences for being wrong. — *Costless advice is systematically biased toward action and away from caution, because the advisor gains from giving advice and loses nothing from it being wrong.*

- **Variance tolerance:** High variance is only acceptable when the downside is survivable and the upside is life-changing. — *Asymmetric outcomes justify asymmetric risk. The bet that can kill you does not justify any expected-value calculation, no matter how positive.*

- **The ruin condition:** Avoid any strategy that could produce total ruin, no matter how good the expected value. — *Expected value is only meaningful across repeated trials. You have one life. A strategy with positive expected value and non-negligible ruin probability is irrational over a single run.*

- **The liquidity premium:** Maintain more liquidity than you think you need. — *Crises and opportunities both arrive unpredictably. Liquidity is the option on both. Illiquidity forecloses both.*

- **The correlation failure:** Assets that seem uncorrelated go correlated in a crisis. Plan for it. — *Correlations measured in normal conditions systematically understate crisis correlations. Diversification that relies on normal-period correlation data will fail in the scenario where you most need it.*

- **The overconfidence default:** In novel situations, default to smaller positions and larger margins of safety. — *Confidence is calibrated by track record. In genuinely novel situations, there is no valid track record. The competence from familiar domains does not transfer as completely as it feels like it does.*

- **The horizon mismatch:** Make sure your risk tolerance matches your time horizon. — *Risk that resolves in five years is not the same risk as something you need in six months. Most risk management failures involve assets or strategies with the wrong horizon for the need they're serving.*

- **The narrative risk:** If you can tell a compelling story for why an investment works, so can everyone else. Compelling stories get fully priced quickly. — *Markets are efficient at pricing consensus narratives. Differentiated returns require differentiated information or differentiated patience.*

- **The insurance principle:** Insurance against ruin is worth its premium even if it never pays off. — *The value of ruin prevention is not its expected value — it is the preservation of all future expected value. Not paying for ruin protection because it's "expensive" is a category error.*

## Time & Energy

- **Meeting filter:** If the meeting has no clear outcome, it's a conversation. Schedule accordingly — or don't. — *Meetings without defined outcomes are attention taxes with no deliverable. The question "what does a good outcome look like?" instantly separates necessary meetings from social time.*

- **The agenda rule:** Never go to a meeting without knowing why you're there and what a good outcome looks like. — *Preparation converts meeting time from passive attendance to directed action. The prepared person gets ten times more from the same meeting.*

- **The top-3 rule:** Three priorities per day max. More than that means none of them are actually priorities. — *Attention is finite and non-divisible. A list of ten priorities is a list of ten things you will not do fully.*

- **Energy before time:** Protect your highest-energy hours for your most important work. Clock hours are not equal. — *Cognitive output per hour varies dramatically by energy state. An hour at 90% capacity produces work that three hours at 40% cannot match.*

- **The "good enough" threshold:** For low-stakes decisions, good enough is optimal. Spend the extra time on things that matter. — *Marginal quality improvement in low-stakes areas consumes resources that produce exponential returns in high-stakes ones. Optimization should be asymmetrically allocated.*

- **Saying no rule:** "No" to one thing is always "yes" to something else. Know what you're trading. — *Every commitment is a resource allocation. The implicit trade, made visible, often changes the decision.*

- **Parkinson's Law:** Work expands to fill the time available. Set tighter deadlines. — *Effort spent on a task is not proportional to the quality of output — it is proportional to the time allocated. Tighter constraints produce equivalent or better results with less time.*

- **The single-tasking advantage:** Do one thing at a time. Context-switching costs 20–40% of productive time. — *Every context switch carries a reloading cost — the time to restore the mental state needed for the prior task. Multitasking is high-frequency switching that never allows full restoration.*

- **The weekly review:** Schedule 60 minutes at the end of every week to review what you shipped, what slipped, and what the next week requires. — *Without a structured review, the week's unfinished business bleeds into the next week as low-level cognitive noise. The review closes the loop and resets the system.*

- **The maker-manager distinction:** Protect large blocks for creation. Manage in the gaps. — *Creative work requires uninterrupted runway. A schedule full of 30-minute slots is a schedule where nothing generative gets built. Creation and coordination require different time architectures.*

- **The response latency rule:** Not every message requires an immediate response. Batch communications into defined windows. — *Immediate response to every message is the same as letting other people schedule your day. Batching reclaims the calendar.*

- **The rest premium:** Recovery is not subtracted from productive time. It multiplies it. — *Fatigued decision-making, fatigued creative work, and fatigued execution all degrade faster than people model. Rest is not a cost — it is leverage.*

- **The planning horizon mismatch:** Day-level planning handles execution. Week-level planning handles priorities. Month-level planning handles direction. Don't use the wrong tool for the wrong level. — *Confusing operational planning with strategic direction produces the state of being busy and going nowhere. Each planning horizon requires different inputs and produces different outputs.*

## Health & Recovery

- **The sleep-first rule:** When something is degrading — mood, performance, judgment, resilience — check sleep before anything else. — *Sleep affects every physiological and cognitive system. It is the most common root cause of performance degradation and the most underdiagnosed because people adapt to chronic impairment and lose the reference point.*

- **The minimum effective dose:** Find the smallest amount of training that produces the desired adaptation, then add from there. — *More training is not always better training. Training is a stress stimulus that requires adequate recovery. More stress without more recovery produces injury and regression, not adaptation.*

- **The consistency premium:** Training 4 days a week for a year beats training 7 days a week for three months and burning out. — *Compounding physical adaptation requires time. Unsustainable intensity produces the cycle of burst-and-recovery that negates the compounding. Consistent moderate training accumulates more total adaptation than variable intense training.*

- **Movement minimum:** If you can't do your full training session, do 20 minutes of something. — *The hardest part of training is showing up. Twenty minutes maintains the habit, maintains some stimulus, and often becomes a full session once you've started. Zero breaks the chain.*

- **The inflammation tell:** Chronic fatigue, brain fog, slow recovery, and low mood often share a root cause in systemic inflammation. Address diet and sleep before adding supplements or changing training. — *Most performance problems that people solve with complexity (supplements, recovery protocols, periodization) are actually solved by removing the obvious inflammatory inputs first.*

- **Stress-recovery symmetry:** For every major stressor — psychological or physical — plan the corresponding recovery. — *The body and mind do not distinguish between sources of stress. Psychological load reduces recovery from physical training and vice versa. Total load is the relevant variable.*

- **The injury prevention asymmetry:** The time you lose to injury vastly exceeds the training time you were protecting when you skipped the warmup or pushed through warning signs. — *Injury breaks the compounding chain. A six-week injury takes six weeks of training away plus the weeks required to rebuild back to baseline. Prevention is asymmetrically worth the time.*

- **The nutrition simplification:** Before optimizing macros or timing, first ensure: no processed food for the majority of meals, adequate protein, adequate vegetables, adequate water. — *Most nutritional problems are problems of basics, not optimization. People seek complexity before the simple things are locked in.*

- **The mental health / physical health inseparability:** Persistent low mood, high anxiety, and inability to concentrate are medical conditions with physical inputs. Treat the body first; the mind follows. — *Brain function is a physiological process. Sleep, diet, exercise, and social connection have effect sizes that rival pharmaceutical intervention for most mood and anxiety conditions.*

- **The recovery measurement:** If your resting heart rate is elevated and heart rate variability is suppressed, reduce training load before adding to it. — *The nervous system records cumulative load even when subjective feeling has adapted to it. Biometric markers that diverge from subjective perception are the more reliable signal.*

## Learning & Skill

- **The 20-hour rule:** Twenty hours of focused, deliberate practice will take you from zero to functional in almost any skill. — *The initial learning curve is steep — most foundational knowledge concentrates at the beginning. After 20 focused hours, you have enough to self-correct and progress independently.*

- **The spacing effect:** Distributed practice beats massed practice for long-term retention. — *Memory consolidation happens between sessions, not during them. Spacing out practice creates repeated retrieval attempts that strengthen encoding. Cramming produces short-term fluency and rapid forgetting.*

- **Teach it to learn it:** If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough. — *The generation requirement for teaching surfaces exactly the gaps that passive review conceals. You cannot teach what you don't understand without the gap becoming immediately visible.*

- **The interleaving advantage:** Practice multiple skills in the same session rather than blocking one skill per session. — *Blocked practice produces faster short-term improvement and worse long-term retention. Interleaving creates desirable difficulty that forces real discrimination and strengthens durable learning.*

- **The compression test:** Can you state what you learned in one sentence? If not, the learning is not integrated. — *Understanding that cannot be compressed is understanding that has not been fully processed. Compression requires the extraction of the core mechanism from the surrounding detail.*

- **Seek corrective feedback:** Positive feedback feels good. Corrective feedback is what changes the trajectory. — *Improvement requires information about the gap between current and desired performance. Validation closes no gap. Correction closes gaps. The ratio of corrective to positive feedback predicts rate of improvement.*

- **The struggle premium:** Desirable difficulty — tasks that are hard but achievable — produces deeper learning than easy success. — *Effort during learning is a signal of encoding depth. Tasks that require real mental work produce more durable representation than tasks that can be completed automatically.*

- **The reference class:** Before learning a skill, find someone who learned it at your age and stage. Their path is the most relevant roadmap. — *Abstract advice about skill development is less useful than watching the specific path of a comparable person. It calibrates timeline expectations and reveals the specific obstacles.*

- **The output requirement:** For every input (book, course, lecture), produce an output. A summary, an application, a teaching moment. — *Information without application has a short half-life. The output requirement forces processing at a deeper level and creates a retrievable artifact.*

- **The expert interview:** An hour with the best person in a field produces more usable knowledge than a month of independent study. — *Experts carry tacit knowledge — the things too obvious to write down, the shortcuts, the failure modes — that no text contains. Direct access compresses the learning curve dramatically.*

- **The plateau protocol:** When progress stalls, change the input — not the output target. — *Plateaus are feedback that the current practice regime is no longer creating the stress required for adaptation. The target is fine. The method needs change.*

- **The beginner's question:** Ask the question that feels too basic to ask. The answer is almost always instructive. — *Experts systematically underestimate how much implicit knowledge they carry. The "obvious" question often reveals the foundational assumption that, once explicit, changes everything.*

## Relationships

- **The asymmetry detector:** Track who initiates contact over time. Consistent asymmetry is the actual relationship, not the stated one. — *People's willingness to initiate contact is an accurate indicator of their prioritization of the relationship. Stated importance diverges from behavioral importance quickly when initiation is consistently one-directional.*

- **The repair speed measure:** How quickly a relationship recovers from conflict is a better indicator of its health than the absence of conflict. — *Conflict-free relationships are either unusually compatible or unusually avoidant. Repair capacity — the ability to return to functional connection after rupture — predicts long-term durability.*

- **The presence test:** Notice who is fully present when they're with you. That's the real quality of the relationship. — *Physical presence with mental absence is the most common form of relational neglect. The quality of full attention given freely is the metric.*

- **The hard truth rule:** The person who will tell you an uncomfortable truth unprompted is more valuable than the person who tells you what you want to hear. — *Comfortable relationships are easy to maintain and contribute little to growth. The person willing to risk the relationship to tell you the truth has made a real investment in you.*

- **The long game relationship:** Invest in relationships when there's no immediate need. — *Relationships built only when you need something are transactional and recognized as such. Relationships built over time before the need arises are the ones that hold under actual pressure.*

- **The values alignment test:** Shared values matter more than shared interests over a long time horizon. — *Common interests give you things to do together. Common values determine whether you'll agree on what matters when circumstances change. Interests diverge; values are more stable.*

- **The conflict style tell:** How someone fights is how they'll always fight. — *Conflict behavior is deeply habituated. People who fight dirty under stress will fight dirty again. Pattern predicts more than apology.*

- **The boundary clarity rule:** Unclear expectations are the source of most relational resentment. State what you need. — *Resentment accumulates from violated expectations. Violated expectations are almost always expectations that were never communicated. Clarity prevents the accumulation.*

- **The gratitude debt:** People who are never thanked eventually withdraw their effort. Express specific gratitude. — *Acknowledgment confirms that effort was seen and valued. Effort that goes unseen becomes effort that goes undone. The mechanism is not ego — it is the basic human need for visibility.*

- **The life chapter awareness:** People are different in different life chapters. Update your model of someone after a major transition. — *People's capabilities, values, and limitations shift significantly across major life events. Relating to someone as they were three chapters ago is relating to a person who no longer exists.*

## Wealth & Money

- **The savings rate rule:** Your savings rate matters more than your investment returns until the portfolio is substantial. — *At small portfolio sizes, the variance in annual contribution swamps the variance in return rate. Optimize input before optimizing yield.*

- **The lifestyle creep alarm:** When income increases, pause before lifestyle increases. — *Every permanent lifestyle upgrade converts income to obligation. The gap that creates wealth is the gap between earning and spending. Income increases widen the gap only if spending holds.*

- **The emergency fund floor:** Hold 6 months of operating expenses in liquid, low-risk assets. Not as investment. As insurance. — *The emergency fund is not dead money — it is the thing that prevents you from selling long-term assets at the wrong time to cover short-term needs. Its purpose is optionality under duress.*

- **The illiquidity premium:** Illiquid investments should compensate you for the illiquidity, not just for the underlying risk. — *Illiquidity is a real cost — it prevents reallocation when better opportunities arise and forces selling on the market's schedule rather than yours. Demand compensation for it.*

- **The fee compounding effect:** Investment fees compound against you the same way returns compound for you. — *A 1% annual fee doesn't cost 1% — it costs years of compounding. Over 30 years, a 1% annual fee reduces the final portfolio by roughly 25%. Minimize fees with the same seriousness as maximizing returns.*

- **The debt sequencing rule:** Pay off high-interest debt before investing in anything that yields less. — *Risk-free return equal to your debt's interest rate is unavailable in markets. Paying off 20% credit card debt is a 20% guaranteed return.*

- **The tax efficiency rule:** Maximize tax-advantaged accounts before optimizing taxable investments. — *Tax drag compounds the same way investment returns do. Deferring taxes on compound growth for decades is a structural advantage that no investment strategy can easily overcome.*

- **The net worth review:** Calculate your net worth quarterly. What gets measured gets managed. — *Most people's financial situation is hazier than they think because they never force a precise accounting. The quarterly review creates visibility that enables decisions.*

- **The income diversification principle:** If one source of income represents more than 80% of your total, reduce that concentration. — *Single-source income concentration is the financial equivalent of a single point of failure. Diversification of income sources is risk management, not overextension.*

- **The major purchase rule:** For any purchase over a threshold you set, wait 48 hours after the decision. — *Purchase desire is partly emotional arousal that decays on a known timeline. The 48-hour delay eliminates most impulse at the large-purchase level where it does the most damage.*
