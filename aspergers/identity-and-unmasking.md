# Identity and Unmasking

Recognizing the profile in yourself — especially late — changes how you read your whole life. Use it to understand the past, take off the mask where it's safe, and build an identity on what you actually are rather than on years of performing what you're not.

## Late Recognition

Many people realize as adults — after a burnout, a child's diagnosis, a partner's comment, an article that reads like a biography. `[E: weak]` Late-diagnosed adults commonly describe a mix of relief, grief, anger, and a long period of re-reading their past (e.g., Leedham et al., 2020, interviews with women diagnosed in mid-to-late life).

All of these are normal:
- **Relief** — there is a reason, and it isn't a character flaw.
- **Grief** — for the years spent struggling without the explanation, and for the support you didn't get.
- **Anger** — at the people and systems that missed it, or punished you for it.
- **Doubt** — "Am I just making excuses?" Usually the people asking that question are the ones who have tried hardest.
- **Identity disruption** — if you aren't who you thought you were, who are you?

There is no deadline for working through these. See [Grief and Loss](../grief-and-loss.md) — the grief for an unsupported past is real grief.

## Re-Reading the Past

The past doesn't change. What it means can.

**The exercise:**
1. List the events you've carried as failures or shame: the friendships that faded, the jobs that went wrong, the "overreactions," the school reports, the things people said about you.
2. For each one, ask: what was the load? What was the sensory environment, the social demand, the uncertainty, the energy budget? What support was missing?
3. Rewrite each in one sentence, accurately: not as an excuse, and not as a verdict. "I burned out at that job because it was an open office with constant changes and no written expectations — not because I'm lazy."
4. Keep what's still yours to own. Re-reading is not erasing responsibility; it is putting it in the right place.

See [Transformation Science](../transformation-science.md) on rewriting the narrative, and [Failure](../failure.md) on debriefing without self-punishment.

## Unmasking

Masking kept you safe and functional. Taking it off is not a single decision; it is a gradual recalibration. See [Masking Has a Price](energy-and-burnout.md#masking-has-a-price) for the cost.

**Do it in stages:**
1. **Notice.** For a week, note when you're masking: what you're suppressing, forcing, or performing.
2. **Start where it's safe.** Alone first, then with the people you trust most.
3. **One trait at a time.** Let yourself stim. Drop the forced eye contact. Say "I need a quiet minute." Wear what feels right.
4. **Watch the response.** Most people notice less than you fear. Some will notice and not mind. A few will mind — that is information about them.
5. **Keep the mask as a tool.** You can still choose it for the interview or the negotiation. The goal is choice, not exposure everywhere.

**Expect a strange middle.** Many people describe a period of not knowing which preferences are genuinely theirs. That passes. [Solitude](../solitude.md) helps you find the answers.

## Language and Identity

`[E: moderate]` In a large UK survey, "autistic" was the term most endorsed by autistic adults; many also used "on the autism spectrum," and some "Aspie" or "Asperger's" (Kenny et al., 2016). Professionals more often preferred person-first language — "person with autism."

**The rule:** your words for yourself are yours. Use the term that fits how you experience your mind, and let other people use theirs. See [Asperger's and Autism: The Distinction](overview.md#aspergers-and-autism-the-distinction) for why this section uses "Asperger's."

## Identity Is Protective

`[E: moderate]` Autistic adults who identify more positively with being autistic, and with other autistic people, report higher self-esteem and better mental health (Cooper, Smith & Russell, 2017).

That suggests the work isn't just practical. How you hold this part of yourself matters:
- **Not tragedy.** You are not a broken version of someone else.
- **Not superpower.** You don't have to be exceptional to be acceptable.
- **A different design**, with real strengths, real costs, and its own way of living well.

The neurodiversity idea — popularized in the late 1990s by Judy Singer — frames brain differences as natural human variation rather than disease. You don't have to adopt the whole movement to take the useful part: the problem is often the fit between the person and the environment, not the person.

## The Useless Tree

In the *Zhuangzi*, a carpenter passes an enormous old oak and dismisses it: its wood is useless for boats, coffins, or beams. That night the tree appears to him in a dream. Every useful tree, it says, gets cut down before its time. It has lived this long precisely because it would not fit his purposes.

Not fitting the template is not the same as having no worth. Sometimes it is the reason you are still standing. The Taoist work is to know your own grain and live along it — not to carve yourself into a shape the carpenter can use.

## Self-Advocacy

Knowing what you need is half the work. Saying it is the other half.

**The ladder** — start at the bottom and climb as confidence grows:
1. **Preference, no reason:** "I'd prefer to talk by email."
2. **Preference with function:** "I do my best work with written instructions."
3. **Need, stated plainly:** "I need a quieter seat to concentrate."
4. **Need with context, if you choose:** "I have Asperger's; noise is a real barrier for me. A quieter seat would make a big difference."

You don't owe anyone a diagnosis to have a need. Disclosure is a tool, not an obligation — see [Disclosure at Work](work-and-career.md#disclosure-at-work).

**Say needs without apology.** "Sorry, I'm so difficult — could you maybe…" invites people to agree that you are difficult. "Could you send that in writing? Thanks" invites them to send it in writing.

## Finding Your Kind

- **Read and listen to autistic adults** — books, blogs, and podcasts written from the inside. Recognition in someone else's words is powerful.
- **Autistic and neurodivergent communities**, online and local. See [Finding Your People](social-decoding.md#finding-your-people).
- **You don't have to join anything.** Community is useful; it isn't required.
