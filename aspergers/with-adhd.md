# With ADHD

When the Asperger's profile and ADHD come together, needs can pull in opposite directions: routine and novelty, deep focus and distractibility, avoiding stimulation and seeking it. The strategies for each alone can fail for both together. Build systems that serve both at once.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim.

Part of the [Asperger's](overview.md) section. If you don't have ADHD, skip this file — though many of the tools work for anyone with executive-function differences.

---

## How Common It Is

`[E: moderate]` A meta-analysis found about 28% of autistic people also meet criteria for ADHD (Lai et al., 2019); estimates vary widely depending on the sample and method. Until DSM-5 (2013), the manual didn't allow both diagnoses at once, so many adults diagnosed earlier were only ever assessed for one.

Community language for the combination is often "AuDHD." Use whatever term fits.

---

## Where the Needs Collide

`[E: weak]` (consistently described by people with both; little systematic research) The friction is real and specific:

| Asperger's side | ADHD side | The collision |
|-----------------|-----------|---------------|
| Needs routine and predictability | Gets bored by routine; craves novelty | The routine you build gets abandoned within weeks — and abandoning it feels destabilizing |
| Deep, monotropic focus | Distractible, attention jumps | Either locked in for hours or unable to start at all — little middle ground |
| Avoids sensory overload | Seeks stimulation | Wants quiet, then needs noise to focus |
| Wants to finish what's started | Starts many things | A trail of half-finished projects that bothers you more than it would bother most people |
| Plans carefully | Acts impulsively | Detailed plans followed by impulsive departures from them |
| Literal, rule-following | Impulsive speech, interrupting | Saying the blunt thing *and* saying it before thinking |

None of these means you're doing it wrong. They mean the system has to be designed for both.

## Design Principles for Both

**1. Structure the frame, vary the contents.**
Keep a fixed shape — wake time, work blocks, recovery, wind-down — and let what goes *inside* each block rotate. The Asperger's side gets predictability; the ADHD side gets novelty.
- Fixed "deep work 9–11," but choose which project each morning.
- Fixed training days, but rotate the type of workout.
- Fixed meal times, but a rotation of meals rather than one default.

**2. Novelty on purpose.**
Schedule novelty so it doesn't arrive as disruption: a new café once a month, a new interest project each season, a planned "anything goes" afternoon. Chosen novelty feeds the ADHD side without breaking the Asperger's side.

**3. Externalize harder than either alone needs.**
Both profiles struggle with working memory. Together, if it isn't written down, visible, and alarmed, it's gone. See [Executive Function](executive-function.md#the-core-rule-externalize-everything).

**4. Use interest as fuel.**
`[E: weak]` Many people with ADHD describe attention as driven by interest, urgency, novelty, or challenge rather than importance. Combine with the Asperger's depth: attach boring tasks to interesting ones, make tasks into puzzles, set real (not invented) deadlines, and use [body doubling](energy-and-burnout.md#autistic-inertia).

**5. Plan for unfinished.**
Expect some projects to stall. Keep a "parked" list so they're paused, not failed. Review it monthly; finish, revive, or formally drop. Dropping on purpose counts as closure.

## Sensory: Seeking and Avoiding

- **Know which state you're in.** Under-stimulated: restless, foggy, can't start. Over-stimulated: irritable, overloaded, need to leave. The fixes are opposite.
- **Under-stimulated:** movement, music, a fidget, background noise, chewing, a walk before the task.
- **Over-stimulated:** cut input, quiet, dark, deep pressure. See [Sensory](sensory.md).
- **Controlled stimulation** often works best: your own music on noise-cancelling headphones gives the ADHD side input while blocking the unpredictable noise the Asperger's side can't filter.

## Medication

- `[E: strong]` ADHD medication is effective for ADHD in adults.
- `[E: moderate]` In autistic children with ADHD symptoms, stimulant medication helped, though less on average and with more side effects than in children with ADHD alone (RUPP Autism Network, 2005). Evidence in autistic adults is thinner.
- Many clinicians start low and increase slowly. Keep a log of dose and effects — see [Medication](health-and-care.md#medication).
- Some people find medication reduces sensory overwhelm by improving filtering; others find it heightens sensory sensitivity or rigidity. Watch both sides and report them.

## Energy and Burnout

The combination is often doubly expensive: masking the autistic traits *and* compensating for the ADHD ones.

- Expect your [energy budget](energy-and-burnout.md) to run tighter than either profile alone would predict.
- Hyperfocus can carry you past your limits without noticing — set hard stops (see [Managing the Tunnel](special-interests.md#managing-the-tunnel)).
- Impulsive yeses fill the calendar. Use a [social quota](routine-design.md#a-weekly-template) and the rule "let me check and get back to you" (see [Saying No](social-decoding.md#saying-no-and-holding-boundaries)).

## Getting Assessed

If the collisions above sound familiar and you've only been assessed for one profile, a combined assessment is worth it — the treatment options differ, and understanding both explains things neither does alone.
