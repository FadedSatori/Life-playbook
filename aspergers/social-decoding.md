# Social Decoding

The social world runs on rules nobody wrote down. You can learn them explicitly, as a system — without accepting that the way you communicate is the wrong one.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim.

## Double Empathy: The Problem Runs Both Ways

For decades, autism was described as a one-sided deficit in reading other minds. Damian Milton's *double empathy problem* (2012) reframed it: when two people with very different ways of experiencing the world interact, **both** struggle to read each other.

The evidence:
- `[E: moderate]` In an information-passing chain, all-autistic chains passed on a story as accurately as all-non-autistic chains. Mixed chains lost more detail and reported lower rapport (Crompton et al., 2020).
- `[E: moderate]` Non-autistic observers formed less favorable first impressions of autistic people from a few seconds of video or audio — but not from transcripts of what they said (Sasson et al., 2017). The content wasn't the problem. The delivery was being judged.

What it means for you:
- The friction is not proof you are broken.
- It is not proof the other person is wrong, either. It is a translation problem.
- In most rooms you are the minority, so you will usually do more of the translating. That is a cost worth knowing about, and a reason to spend time where you don't have to translate.
- Communication with other autistic people is often easier. Use that.

## Small Talk Is a Protocol, Not a Conversation

Small talk exchanges very little information. It does other jobs:
- **Signals safety** — "I'm friendly, I'm not a threat."
- **Checks bandwidth** — "Are you open to talking right now?"
- **Builds familiarity before trust** — "We've talked before; we're okay."

Treat it as a handshake, not a test. The standard sequence:
1. Greeting and a low-stakes opener — the event, the weather, the weekend.
2. Ask one question back.
3. Roughly match the other person's answer length.
4. When a topic gets energy from both sides, go deeper. When it doesn't, move on or exit.

**Scripts:**
- **Open:** "How do you know [host]?" · "What brings you here?" · "Have you been to one of these before?"
- **Deepen:** "What got you into that?" · "What's the hardest part of it?"
- **Exit:** "Good to talk to you — I'm going to grab a drink." · "I'll let you get back to it. Nice meeting you."

## Subtext: A Translation Table

These patterns are common in many English-speaking workplaces and social settings. They vary by culture and by person, so treat each as a likely reading, not a rule. When it matters and you're unsure, ask.

| They say | It often means |
|----------|---------------|
| "We should get coffee sometime." | Friendly, not a commitment. If you want it to happen, propose a time: "I'd like that — Tuesday?" |
| "How are you?" (in passing) | A greeting, not a question. "Good, you?" is the full expected answer. |
| "Interesting." (flat tone) | Often "I'm not convinced" or "I don't want to discuss this." |
| "Just a thought…" / "You might want to…" | Often a firm request or correction, softened. |
| "No worries if not!" | It matters to them somewhat. A reply is still expected. |
| "Let me think about it." | Often a soft no. |
| "I'm fine." (tense tone) | Possibly not fine. Check in gently later, not right now. |
| Silence after your long explanation | They may have lost the thread or want a turn. Check: "Too much detail?" |

## Ask for Directness

The cleanest fix for subtext is to remove it. Most people will be direct if you ask, and many find the request a relief.

**Scripts:**
- "I take things literally, so if you want something changed, just tell me straight. I won't be offended."
- "Is that a 'yes' or a 'maybe later'?"
- "Do you want my opinion, or do you want me to just listen?"
- "I'm not sure how that landed — was that okay?"

---

## Your Honesty, Delivered Well

Directness is a strength. The delivery decides whether it gets heard.

**The format: care → fact → path forward.**
- "I want this to work, so I'll be straight: the plan has a gap in the budget. Here's how I'd close it."
- "I like you, so I'm telling you: that comment in the meeting came across as dismissive. I think a quick note to Sam would fix it."

Before offering feedback nobody asked for, ask first: "Do you want feedback on this?" For harder conversations, see [Communication](../communication.md).

## Sharing Your Interests

Sharing a deep interest in detail — often called info-dumping — is a real form of connection. `[E: weak]` Many autistic adults describe it as a way they bond and show care. With people who don't share the interest, add reciprocity checks:

- **Watch for saturation:** shorter replies, glancing away, checking the phone.
- **Hand the turn back** after a couple of minutes: "Anyway — what are you into lately?"
- **Offer the short version first** and let them ask for more: "Want the long version?"
- **Find people who want the long version.** They exist. See [Finding Your People](#finding-your-people).

## Eye Contact

`[E: weak]` Many autistic people describe eye contact as uncomfortable or even painful, and say it makes listening harder (Trevisan et al., 2017, analysis of first-hand accounts).

**Options:**
- Look near the eyes — the bridge of the nose, the eyebrows.
- Look while you are speaking; look away while you are listening.
- Tell people once: "I listen better when I'm not looking right at you."

## Groups

Group conversation is the hardest format: several speakers, fast turn-taking, overlapping signals.

- Prefer one-on-one and small groups. Arrive early, while the group is still small.
- Take a role — host, helper, the person running the grill. Structure reduces load.
- Step out for a few minutes every 30–60 minutes.
- Set your leave time in advance. Leaving at it is a plan, not a failure.

## Conflict and Repair

- **Name what happened, then ask what they heard:** "I think what I said came across differently than I meant. What did you hear?"
- **Separate intent from impact:** "I didn't mean it as criticism, and I can see it felt like that. I'm sorry."
- **Pause when overloaded:** "I want to sort this out, and I can't think clearly right now. Can we pick it up tonight?" Overload and flooding make repair impossible; see [Anger](../anger.md) and [Intimate Partnership](../relationships/intimate-partnership.md).
- **Writing can help.** Many people find hard conversations easier in writing, where they have time to process. Tone gets lost in text, so say the warm part out loud: "I'm writing this because I want to get it right, not because I'm upset with you."

## Sarcasm, Jokes, and Teasing

Non-literal language is the hardest kind of subtext: the words mean one thing and the speaker means another.

**Clues that something isn't meant literally:**
- The statement is obviously false or exaggerated in context ("Great, another Monday").
- Tone is flat, exaggerated, or sing-song in a way that doesn't match the words.
- Others laugh or smile.
- It is said between people who know each other well — friends tease; strangers rarely do.

**When you can't tell:** ask lightly. "Was that a joke? I take things literally." Most people will laugh with you, not at you.

**Teasing between friends** is usually a sign of closeness — it says "we're secure enough to joke." Teasing from someone you don't trust, or that targets something you're sensitive about, isn't friendly. You can say so: "I know that's meant as a joke, but it doesn't land well for me."

**Your own humor** may be dry, literal, pun-based, or deadpan. That's a real style with its own audience. If people miss it, a small smile or "(joking)" in text helps.

## Saying No and Holding Boundaries

Many people with this profile say yes to avoid conflict or because they didn't have time to process the request — then pay for it in energy later.

**Buy time first:** "Let me check and get back to you tonight." This is a complete answer and gives you room to decide.

**Decline cleanly:**
- "Thanks for thinking of me — I can't make it."
- "That doesn't work for me, but I'd be glad to [alternative]."
- "I'm not taking on anything new this month."

**You don't owe a long explanation.** One clear reason or none at all. Long justifications invite negotiation.

**Repeat if pushed:** say the same sentence again, calmly. You don't need a new argument each time.

See [Courage](../courage.md) on the social cost being smaller than it feels.

## Phone Calls and Messages

**Phone calls** combine real-time processing, no visual cues, and no time to think — which is why many people find them disproportionately hard.
- Write a script before important calls: who you are, why you're calling, the one thing you need answered.
- Ask for a follow-up email: "Could you send that to me in writing?"
- Use email, chat, or online forms when you can. It is a legitimate preference.

**Texts and messages** have their own unwritten rules:
- Short replies can read as cold. A word of warmth or an exclamation point softens it ("Sounds good!" vs. "OK").
- Not every message needs an immediate reply. "Saw this — will reply properly later" buys time without leaving people hanging.
- Tone doesn't travel. If something matters emotionally, say it explicitly or talk in person.

## Finding Your People

- **Interest-based groups** — clubs, classes, forums, maker spaces, martial arts, chess, game nights. The shared activity carries the conversation, so you don't have to.
- **Autistic peers** — local groups and online communities.
- `[E: weak]` Autistic adults often describe time with other autistic people as easier and more comfortable than time with non-autistic people (Crompton et al., 2020, from autistic adults' accounts).

## Protecting Yourself

`[E: moderate]` Autistic adults report more experiences of exploitation, manipulation, and abuse than non-autistic adults (Griffiths et al., 2019). Taking people at their word is a virtue — and bad actors know how to use it.

**Rules:**
- **Judge by actions, not words.** See [Heuristics](../heuristics.md).
- **Big asks need a pause.** Money, secrecy, or urgency → stop and talk to someone you trust first.
- **"If it has to be decided right now, the answer is no."**
- **Notice who only appears when they need something.**

See [Epistemic Self-Defense](../epistemic-self-defense.md) and [Reading People](../tactics/social.md#reading-people) in Social Tactics.
