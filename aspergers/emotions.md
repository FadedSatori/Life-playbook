# Emotions

Feelings may arrive late, arrive as body sensations instead of words, or arrive all at once. That is not an absence of emotion — it is a different route to it. Learn your route, and build the tools that make it readable.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim.

## Feeling Is Not the Problem — Reading It Is

The old stereotype says autistic people feel less. The evidence points elsewhere.

- `[E: moderate]` Difficulty recognizing emotions — in yourself and in others' faces — is largely explained by co-occurring **alexithymia**, not by autism itself (the "alexithymia hypothesis," Bird & Cook, 2013). In studies that measure both, autistic people without alexithymia show much smaller emotion-recognition difficulties, or none.
- `[E: moderate]` About half of autistic people are alexithymic (see [What Comes With It](overview.md#what-comes-with-it)). The other half aren't. Know which you are before assuming anything.
- `[E: weak]` Many autistic adults describe feeling emotions intensely — sometimes too intensely to sort out in the moment — rather than weakly.

The practical problem is not missing feelings. It is feelings that stay as raw body data, or show up hours later, or arrive all together as "overwhelmed."

## The Delayed Signal

`[E: weak]` Many autistic adults describe processing emotions after the fact: the conversation felt fine, and the hurt, anger, or joy arrives that night or the next day.

**What to do with it:**
- **Don't decide in the moment** on anything that matters emotionally. "Let me get back to you tomorrow" is a complete answer.
- **Expect the delayed wave** after intense events — weddings, conflicts, big meetings — and leave space for it.
- **Revisit when it lands.** It is fine to go back to someone a day later: "I've been thinking about yesterday, and I realize it bothered me more than I said."

## Body First, Label Second

If you can't name the feeling, start with what you can observe. The body usually knows before the mind does.

**The three-step read:**
1. **Body:** What is physically happening? Chest tight, jaw clenched, stomach heavy, hands restless, face hot, energy low.
2. **Context:** What happened in the last few hours? What changed? What am I dreading or waiting for?
3. **Best guess:** Given the body and the context, what is the most likely feeling? Write it as a probability if that's easier — "probably anxious, maybe 70%" (see [Probabilistic Thinking](../mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md)).

**Common body–emotion patterns** (a starting point — your map will differ):

| Body signal | Often means |
|-------------|-------------|
| Tight chest, fast breathing, restless | Anxiety, anticipation |
| Hot face, clenched jaw, urge to move | Anger, frustration |
| Heavy, slow, flat, nothing appeals | Sadness, depletion, early burnout |
| Buzzing, can't sit still, talking fast | Excitement — or overload |
| Sudden need to leave, everything too loud | Overload (see [Energy and Burnout](energy-and-burnout.md#overload-meltdown-shutdown)) |
| Warm, loose, settled | Safety, contentment |

Over a few months, keep notes. You are building a personal translation table from body to feeling.

## Build the Vocabulary

`[E: moderate]` People with more precise emotional vocabulary regulate better (Barrett's work on emotional granularity; see [Cognitive Tactics](../tactics/cognitive.md)). Precision is a skill, and it can be trained like any other.

**The ladder** — move from broad to specific:

| Broad | More specific |
|-------|---------------|
| Bad | Anxious · frustrated · sad · ashamed · lonely · drained |
| Anxious | Worried · dreading · uncertain · overstimulated · on edge |
| Angry | Irritated · resentful · betrayed · disrespected · thwarted |
| Sad | Disappointed · grieving · homesick · left out · hopeless |
| Good | Calm · relieved · proud · absorbed · connected · excited |

**The practice:** once a day, name your state with one word from the right-hand column. If none fit, write the body signals instead. Both count.

## Scheduled Check-Ins

If the signal doesn't come to you, go and get it. Three times a day — tie it to meals or the [body checks](energy-and-burnout.md#building-it-into-the-routine):

1. **Energy:** 1–5?
2. **Body:** anything tight, hot, heavy, or restless?
3. **Feeling:** best guess, one word?
4. **Need:** food, water, rest, quiet, movement, people, or space?

Thirty seconds. The value is in the pattern over weeks, not any single answer.

## Interoception Can Be Trained

`[E: moderate]` In a randomized trial, autistic adults who practiced a heartbeat-detection training program over six sessions became more accurate at reading internal signals and were less likely to report anxiety at follow-up (the ADIE trial; Quadt et al., 2021). One trial — promising, not settled.

**A home version** (not the trial protocol, but the same idea):
- Sit still for one minute. Without touching your pulse, count your heartbeats.
- Then count again while holding your pulse. Compare.
- Repeat a few times a week. Notice whether the gap shrinks.
- During the day, pause and ask: "What is my heart doing right now? My breathing? My stomach?"

## Regulating Big Feelings

**Before the spike:**
- Know your early signs (see [Your Profile](overview.md#your-profile)).
- Lower the load: sensory, social, or task. Most emotional spikes ride on top of an already-full [energy budget](energy-and-burnout.md).

**During:**
- **Down-regulate the body first.** Slow breathing with a long exhale, cold water on the face, deep pressure, walking. `[E: strong]` Slow breathing reliably lowers heart rate and arousal (see [Consciousness Technologies](../consciousness-technologies.md)).
- **Delay decisions and replies.** Nothing important gets sent while flooded.
- **Change the input** — leave the room, put headphones on.

**After:**
- Write out what happened, in order, without judging it.
- Ask: what was the trigger, what was the load already, what was the first sign?
- See [Anger](../anger.md) for anger specifically — including why venting backfires.

## Empathy, Accurately

`[E: moderate]` Research separates two kinds of empathy: *cognitive* (working out what someone else feels) and *affective* (feeling moved by it). In adults with Asperger's, one study found reduced cognitive empathy but intact affective empathy (Dziobek et al., 2008). Autistic people more often find the first harder, not the second — and much of the cognitive difficulty again tracks alexithymia and the [double empathy](social-decoding.md#double-empathy-the-problem-runs-both-ways) gap.

What that means in practice:
- **You may care deeply and still miss the signal.** Ask: "You seem quiet — is something wrong?"
- **Care can be shown in your way.** Practical help, solving the problem, remembering details, showing up. Say what you're doing so it lands: "I fixed your bike because I know you've had a hard week."
- **Ask what they need:** "Do you want help fixing it, or do you want me to listen?"

## When to Get Help

If feelings are mostly anxiety, low mood, or numbness most days for weeks, that is worth professional support — ideally from someone experienced with autistic adults. See [Health and Care](health-and-care.md).
