# Health and Care

Healthcare systems assume you can describe your body on the spot, tolerate a loud waiting room, and read between the lines of a rushed appointment. Prepare for that system, choose support that fits your wiring, and build a plan for bad days while you're well.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim. This file is a guide to navigating care, not medical advice.

Part of the [Asperger's](overview.md) section. General health protocols live in [Fitness](../health/fitness.md), [Sleep](../health/sleep.md), [Nutrition](../health/nutrition.md), and [Longevity Protocols](../longevity-protocols.md).

## Why This Matters

- `[E: moderate]` (large but observational) In a Swedish national register study, autistic people died earlier on average than the general population, across a wide range of causes (Hirvikoski et al., 2016).
- `[E: moderate]` Autistic adults report more unmet healthcare needs, lower satisfaction with care, and more emergency-room use than non-autistic adults (Nicolaidis et al., 2013).

Part of that gap is about access, not biology: missed signals, appointments that are hard to get through, and symptoms that are hard to describe. That part is fixable.

---

## Before an Appointment

- **Write it down first.** Symptoms, when they started, what makes them better or worse, what you've tried, and the questions you need answered. Bring the list; read from it.
- **Lead with the top concern.** Appointments are short. Put the most important thing first.
- **Ask for accommodations:** the first or last appointment of the day (shorter waits), a quiet place to wait, extra time, written instructions.
- **Bring someone** if it helps — to take notes or to speak up if you go quiet.
- **Request a summary in writing** or through the patient portal afterward.

## Describing Symptoms

`[E: weak]` Because of interoception and alexithymia differences (see [What Comes With It](overview.md#what-comes-with-it)), some autistic people under-notice or under-report pain and illness — or describe it in ways clinicians misread.

**Describe function, not just intensity.** A 1–10 pain scale is hard to calibrate. Function is clearer:
- "I've stopped being able to sleep through the night because of it."
- "I can't lift my arm above my shoulder."
- "I've missed three days of work this month."
- "This is different from my normal. I don't usually notice pain, so if I'm mentioning it, it's significant."

**Say how you communicate:** "I may not look distressed, but this is affecting me a lot."

## Mental Health Support

**Finding the right person:**
- Ask directly: "Do you have experience working with autistic adults?"
- Look for someone who treats the profile as context, not as the thing to fix.
- Be cautious of approaches whose goal is to make you *look* less autistic rather than *feel* better — that is masking with a treatment plan, and masking has costs (see [Masking Has a Price](energy-and-burnout.md#masking-has-a-price)).

**What the evidence says:**
- `[E: moderate]` Cognitive behavioral therapy adapted for autistic people reduces anxiety in children and teens across multiple trials. `[E: weak]` In adults there are fewer trials, though adapted approaches look promising.
- **Adaptations that often help:** more structure, concrete examples, written summaries, visual tools, extra time, less reliance on open-ended "how does that make you feel?" questions, and incorporating your interests.

**Things to say in a first session:**
- "I'm better in writing — can I send notes between sessions?"
- "I may take longer to identify what I'm feeling. Direct questions help more than open ones."
- "Please tell me directly if you want me to do something differently."

## Medication

- There is no medication for autism itself. Medications treat co-occurring conditions — anxiety, depression, ADHD, sleep problems — and work for autistic people too.
- `[E: strong]` ADHD medication has strong evidence in adults; if ADHD is part of your picture, it is worth assessing (see [Executive Function](executive-function.md#when-to-get-assessed-for-adhd)).
- `[E: weak]` Some autistic people report being more sensitive to side effects. Many clinicians start with low doses and increase slowly. Ask about this if it worries you.
- Keep a simple log when starting anything new: dose, date, effects, side effects. It makes the follow-up appointment far more useful.

## Sleep

`[E: moderate]` Sleep problems are more common in autistic people than in the general population, in both children and adults.

- Start with the fundamentals in [Sleep](../health/sleep.md) — consistent wake time, morning light, a cool, dark room.
- Sensory factors matter more than usual: noise, light leaks, fabric, temperature. See [Sensory and Health](sensory.md#sensory-and-health).
- `[E: strong]` Melatonin improves sleep in autistic children (e.g., Gringras et al., 2017). `[E: weak]` Evidence for autistic adults specifically is thinner. Ask a doctor before starting, and use low doses (see [Sleep](../health/sleep.md#travel-and-jet-lag-protocol) on dosing).
- Late nights inside an interest are a common cause — set a hard stop (see [Special Interests](special-interests.md#managing-the-tunnel)).

## Substances

`[E: moderate]` Autistic people — especially those who also have ADHD — have higher rates of substance-related problems (Butwicka et al., 2017). Alcohol and other substances are often used to get through social events or to switch off an overloaded brain.

That is understandable, and worth watching:
- Notice whether you're using something to tolerate situations rather than enjoy them.
- If so, the problem may be the situation's load. Reduce that first (see [Energy and Burnout](energy-and-burnout.md)).
- See [Nutrition](../health/nutrition.md#alcohol-clear-accounting) on what alcohol costs.

## Write a Crisis Plan While You're Well

When you're overloaded or in a dark place, you may not have words or the capacity to plan. Write it now, while you can.

## Routine Check-Ups

Interoception differences mean problems can go unnoticed longer. Don't rely on symptoms to tell you when to see a doctor.

- An annual physical and bloodwork — see [The Annual Longevity Bloodwork Panel](../longevity-protocols.md#the-annual-longevity-bloodwork-panel).
- Dental and eye checks on schedule, not only when something hurts.
- Put them all in the calendar a year ahead, as part of the [Annual Review](../routines/annual-review.md).
