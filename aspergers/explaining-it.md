# Explaining It

Sooner or later you will want — or need — to explain the Asperger's profile to someone. Have the words ready at three lengths, tailor them to the listener, and know how to answer the common responses without having to argue your own existence.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim.

## First, Decide Whether to Tell

You never owe anyone an explanation. Ask:
- **What do I want from telling them?** Understanding, an accommodation, closeness, or just to stop hiding?
- **What's the realistic risk?** Misunderstanding, gossip, being treated differently, a job consequence?
- **Is there a smaller step that gets the same result?** Often a need can be stated without the label — see [Self-Advocacy](identity-and-unmasking.md#self-advocacy).
- **Am I telling them in a calm moment?** Not mid-conflict, where it can sound like an excuse.

---

## Three Lengths

**One line** — for acquaintances and quick moments:
> "I have Asperger's — I'm wired a bit differently. Mostly it means I prefer things direct and I need quiet time to recharge."

**One paragraph** — for friends, colleagues, a new manager:
> "I have Asperger's, which is part of the autism spectrum. For me it mainly means three things: I take language literally, so I do best when people are direct; noise and crowds wear me out faster than most people; and I go very deep on the things I work on. If I seem quiet or blunt, it isn't about you. Telling me straight is always welcome."

**Full conversation** — for a partner, family, or close friend: use your [User Manual](user-manual-template.md) as the agenda. Cover what it is for you, what drains you, what helps, what can be misread, and what you'd like from them. Leave room for their questions.

**The rule for all three:** describe *your* version, concretely. "Autism is a spectrum" explains nothing about you. "Loud restaurants exhaust me" explains a lot.

## Tailor It to the Listener

| Listener | Lead with | Example |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| Partner | What it means for the two of you | "When I go quiet after work, I'm recharging, not pulling away from you." |
| Family | What they've always seen, now explained | "You know how I always left parties early? That's part of it." |
| Friend | What makes time together easier | "Smaller groups and a plan help me enjoy things more." |
| Manager | How you do your best work | "I do my best work with written priorities and a quieter space." |
| Doctor | How it affects care | "I may not show pain the usual way, so please take my description seriously." |
| Child | Simple, positive, concrete | "My brain notices sounds really strongly, so I wear headphones. Your brain is great at other things." |

## Answering the Common Responses

**"You don't seem autistic."**
> "Thanks — a lot of that is effort you don't see. Asperger's mostly shows on the inside: how much energy things take, not how they look."

**"Everyone's a little bit autistic."**
> "Everyone has some of the traits. The difference is how many, how strong, and how much they shape daily life. Everyone gets tired; not everyone has a condition that causes fatigue."

`[E: moderate]` Autistic traits do vary continuously across the whole population — so the first half of their sentence is true, and the conclusion isn't.

**"Isn't Asperger's not a diagnosis anymore?"**
> "It was merged into autism spectrum disorder in 2013. Plenty of people diagnosed before then, or who recognize that profile, still use the term. It's the one that fits me." (Background: [The Distinction](overview.md#aspergers-and-autism-the-distinction).)

**"Aren't you just using it as an excuse?"**
> "It's an explanation, not an excuse. I still take responsibility for what I do. Knowing why something is hard just lets me handle it better."

**"So you're like [famous fictional character]?"**
> "Some of it, maybe. Every autistic person is different — those characters are usually written for drama, not accuracy."

**"Did something cause it?"**
> "It's mostly genetic. It isn't caused by parenting, and it isn't caused by vaccines."

`[E: strong]` Family studies across five countries put heritability around 80% (Bai et al., 2019), and large studies have found no link to vaccines (e.g., Hviid et al., 2019, over 650,000 children).

**"You can't be — you have a job / a partner / friends."**
> "Lots of autistic people do. It doesn't mean things are easy — it means I've built ways to handle them."

## When It Goes Badly

Some people will respond with disbelief, discomfort, or unhelpful advice.

- **You don't have to win the argument.** "I understand it's new to you. I'm happy to talk more another time." Then stop.
- **Give them time.** Many people respond better on the second conversation than the first, once they've thought about it.
- **Notice what it tells you.** A person who can't accept a calm, honest explanation of how you work has shown you something about the relationship.
- **Debrief yourself afterward,** kindly. You did something hard.

## Explaining Without the Label

You can explain almost every need without ever naming Asperger's:
- "I'm someone who needs things in writing."
- "I find big groups draining — can we meet one-on-one?"
- "I'm very literal, so please tell me directly if something's wrong."
- "I need an hour of quiet after work before I'm good company."

The label adds context. The needs stand on their own.
