# Sensory

Your senses may be turned up, turned down, or both — and the world is built for a nervous system that isn't yours. Map your profile, then design your environment around it instead of paying the cost all day.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim.

## Why This Comes First

`[E: strong]` Sensory differences are part of the autism diagnostic criteria (DSM-5). A meta-analysis found large, consistent differences in sensory responsiveness between autistic and non-autistic children (Ben-Sasson et al., 2009), and over 90% of autistic children showed sensory differences in one large study (Leekam et al., 2007). Most autistic adults describe them as lifelong.

Sensory load is the most invisible drain in your [energy budget](energy-and-burnout.md). A social event shows up on the calendar. The buzzing light, the scratchy shirt, and the three overlapping conversations in the open office never do — but they draw on the same budget all day.

---

## Over, Under, Seeking, Avoiding

- **Over-responsive:** input arrives too loud, too bright, too much. Ordinary things hurt.
- **Under-responsive:** input barely registers. You miss your name being called, or don't notice cold or injury.
- **Seeking:** you go looking for input — pressure, movement, strong flavors, a particular texture.
- **Avoiding:** you steer away from input you know will overload you.

Most people are a mix: over-responsive to sound, under-responsive to hunger, seeking deep pressure. `[E: moderate]` Winnie Dunn's sensory processing model maps the same pattern into four quadrants — seeking, avoiding, sensitivity, and low registration (Dunn, 1997).

## The Eight-Sense Audit

Five senses is the schoolbook version. Three more matter here: **vestibular** (balance and motion), **proprioception** (where your body is in space), and **interoception** (internal signals like hunger and fatigue).

| Sense | Too much often looks like | Too little often looks like | Common tools |
|-------|--------------------------|----------------------------|--------------|
| Sound | Noise feels painful; can't separate voices in a crowd; specific sounds (chewing, buzzing, clicking) intolerable | Not hearing your name | Noise-cancelling headphones, filtered earplugs, white or brown noise, quiet venues and off-peak times |
| Sight | Fluorescent or flickering light, clutter, busy patterns, bright screens | Missing visual cues | Warm, dim light; a cap or tinted lenses; a clutter-free work zone; dark mode |
| Touch | Tags, seams, certain fabrics; light or unexpected touch | Not noticing cuts, bruises, or temperature | Soft, tagless clothing; the same trusted clothes; asking before touch |
| Smell | Perfume, cleaning products, food smells overwhelming | Not noticing smoke or spoiled food | Unscented products, fresh air, a familiar scent to anchor |
| Taste and texture | Many foods intolerable for texture | Seeking very strong flavors | A list of safe foods; build nutrition into foods you tolerate |
| Vestibular | Motion sickness; dislike of heights, spinning, elevators | Seeking rocking, swinging, spinning | Rocking chair, walking, swimming, swinging |
| Proprioception | — | Clumsiness, bumping into things, seeking pressure or heavy effort | Strength training, weighted items, compression clothing, carrying heavy things |
| Interoception | — | Missing hunger, thirst, fatigue, illness, or emotion until it's extreme | Scheduled body checks, meal and water alarms, sleep tracking |

**Your audit:** for each sense, write down what overloads you, what calms you, and what you seek. Put the results in [Your Profile](overview.md#your-profile).

## Design the Environment

**Principle:** reduce load at the source before you try to tolerate it. Tolerating costs energy every single time; changing the environment costs once. This is [Reality Engineering](../reality-engineering.md) applied to the nervous system.

**At home:**
- One zero-demand space — a room or a corner. Dim, quiet, soft, nothing to do.
- Lighting you control: warm bulbs, lamps instead of overheads, dimmers.
- Clothes you never have to think about: the same soft, tagless pieces.

**At work:**
- A seat away from foot traffic and noise; headphones treated as normal.
- Control over lighting where possible; a desk lamp instead of the overhead.
- Remote days after heavy days. See [Work and Career](work-and-career.md#accommodations).

**Out in the world:**
- Go at off-peak times.
- Scout new places in advance — photos, layout, where it's quiet.
- Know where the exit and the quiet spot are.
- Decide your time limit before you go.

## The Sensory Kit

Carry it the way you'd carry keys:
- Noise-cancelling headphones or filtered earplugs
- Sunglasses or a cap
- A familiar fidget or texture
- Water and a snack (backup for missed hunger signals)
- An extra layer for temperature
- Calming audio saved offline

## Stimming

`[E: weak]` Autistic adults describe stimming — repetitive movement, sound, or touch — as a way to regulate emotion and sensory input, and describe suppressing it as exhausting (Kapp et al., 2019, from autistic adults' accounts).

**The rule:** don't suppress stims that harm no one. Where you need to be discreet, have a low-visibility option — a fidget ring, a textured object in your pocket, foot movement. Change only stims that cause injury, and replace them rather than just blocking them.

---

## Deep Pressure and Weighted Tools

`[E: weak]` Many autistic people find deep pressure calming, but trial results are mixed. A weighted-blanket trial in autistic children found no improvement in objectively measured sleep, although the children and parents preferred the weighted blanket (Gringras et al., 2014). Use it if it works for you; don't expect it to fix sleep on its own.

---

## Recovering From Sensory Overload

1. **Leave, or cut input** — headphones, eyes closed, a dark room.
2. **One steady input instead of many** — silence, or a single constant sound.
3. **Deep pressure and rhythm** — a blanket, rocking, walking.
4. **Water and food** if you've missed them.
5. **Time.** Twenty minutes to several hours. Plan for the longer end after a big day.

See also [Overload, Meltdown, Shutdown](energy-and-burnout.md#overload-meltdown-shutdown).

## Sensory and Health

- **Sleep:** sound and light sensitivity make the sleep environment critical. Darkness, quiet or steady noise, and the right temperature — see [Sleep](../health/sleep.md).
- **Nutrition:** `[E: moderate]` restricted eating is more common in autistic people, often for texture reasons. Don't fight it — build protein, fiber, and vegetables into foods you already tolerate. If your diet is very narrow, ask a doctor to check micronutrients. See [Nutrition](../health/nutrition.md).
- **Fitness:** strength training is excellent proprioceptive input and often regulating. Gyms are loud and bright — train at off-peak hours with headphones, or at home. See [Fitness](../health/fitness.md).
