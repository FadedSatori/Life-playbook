# For the People Close to You

A page written for a partner, family member, friend, or manager of someone with the Asperger's profile. Hand it to them alongside the person's own [User Manual](user-manual-template.md). It explains the profile in plain terms and what actually helps.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim.

## If You Read Nothing Else

1. **They mean what they say.** Take their words at face value, and give them yours the same way. Hints and hidden meanings get missed — not out of carelessness.
2. **Quiet isn't rejection.** Time alone is how they recharge. They will come back with more to give.
3. **Some environments cost far more energy than you'd guess.** Noise, crowds, bright light, and constant change can exhaust them even when they look fine.
4. **Bluntness is usually honesty, not unkindness.** If something lands badly, tell them directly. They'll usually want to fix it.
5. **Ask, don't assume.** Their needs are specific. Their [User Manual](user-manual-template.md) tells you what they are.

## What It Is, Briefly

Asperger's is a profile within the autism spectrum: fluent language and often high ability, alongside real differences in social communication, sensory processing, and how attention and energy work. It is lifelong, it is mostly genetic `[E: strong]` (heritability around 80%; Bai et al., 2019), and it is nobody's fault.

Because the language is fluent, the difference is easy to miss — and people often expect more social ease than the person has. Much of what they handle smoothly in public they handle with effort you don't see. See [The Distinction](overview.md#aspergers-and-autism-the-distinction).

## Communication That Works

- **Say it directly.** "I'd like you to take the trash out tonight" works better than "The trash is getting full."
- **Say the important things in words,** even if they seem obvious: "I'm not upset with you." "I loved that you did that."
- **Give processing time.** A pause before answering is thinking, not avoidance. A reply tomorrow is often a better reply.
- **Use writing for important things.** Many people with this profile communicate best in text or email.
- **Check what landed:** "What did you hear me say?" clears up misunderstandings early.
- **Tell them about tone.** "I'm joking." "I'm actually serious." "That's just how I talk when I'm tired."

## When They're Overloaded

You may see irritability, going quiet, short answers, a need to leave, or — at the far end — a meltdown or shutdown. See [Overload, Meltdown, Shutdown](energy-and-burnout.md#overload-meltdown-shutdown).

**What helps:**
- Fewer words, lower voice
- Reducing noise and light, or helping them get somewhere quiet
- Space — but stay available: "I'm here when you're ready."
- Not touching unless they've said it helps
- Waiting to talk it through until they've recovered — that may be hours, not minutes

**What doesn't:**
- Asking "What's wrong?" repeatedly
- Demanding an explanation in the moment
- Treating it as a tantrum or as manipulation — it isn't
- Taking it personally

## How to Show Care So It Lands

- **Practical help often means more than words** — and their practical help is often how *they* show care. Fixing your bike, researching your problem, remembering your preferences: that is love in their language. See [Love in Different Languages](relationships.md#love-in-different-languages).
- **Respect their interests.** Asking about them, even listening for a while, is a strong signal of care.
- **Predictability is a gift.** Advance notice of plans and changes lowers their load more than you might think.
- **Defend their recovery time** — especially from other people who don't understand it.

## Common Misreadings

| What you might see | What it often means |
|--------------------|---------------------|
| Little eye contact | They're listening — eye contact can make listening harder |
| Leaving early | They've hit their limit, not that they didn't enjoy it |
| Correcting a detail | They care about getting it right |
| No reaction to your news | They're processing; the reaction may come later |
| Repeating the same food, route, or routine | It's regulating, not rigid for its own sake |
| Talking at length about one topic | They're sharing something they love — a form of connection |
| "I need to be alone" | A maintenance need, not a verdict on you |

## Your Needs Count Too

In a relationship between an autistic and a non-autistic person, both people are translating — see [Double Empathy](social-decoding.md#double-empathy-the-problem-runs-both-ways). Your needs are real as well.

- **Say what you need, directly.** They can't read hints, but they can usually meet a clearly stated need.
- **Agree on systems together** — check-ins, signals for alone time, how to raise problems. See [Relationships](relationships.md).
- **Get support for yourself** if you need it: friends, other partners and families of autistic people, a counselor who understands neurodivergent relationships.
- **Don't become their full-time translator** to the rest of the world. Support them; don't carry them.

## For Managers

- **Clarity is the accommodation that helps most.** Written priorities, clear deadlines, explicit expectations.
- **Direct feedback, specifically.** "This section is unclear; add an example" is more useful and kinder than vague hints.
- **Ask what environment works best** — and mean it. Quieter seats, headphones, and remote days often cost nothing.
- **Judge on output.** Small talk, eye contact, and presence at social events say little about performance.
- See [Accommodations](work-and-career.md#accommodations).
