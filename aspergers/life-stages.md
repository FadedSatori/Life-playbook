# Life Stages

The profile is lifelong, but the demands on it change: leaving school, studying, building a career, partnering, parenting, midlife, retirement, growing old. Each transition reshuffles the energy budget and removes some of the structure that was holding things together. See each one coming, and rebuild the structure before you need it.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim. Research on autistic adults past early adulthood is still thin; much here is tagged accordingly.

## The Pattern Across Every Stage

Every major life stage does three things at once:
1. **Removes structure** — the school timetable, the job, the children at home.
2. **Adds new demands** — new places, people, expectations, and rules nobody explains.
3. **Changes your identity** — student, employee, partner, parent, retiree.

For this profile, each of those costs energy, and transitions stack all three. `[E: weak]` Autistic adults frequently describe major transitions as triggers for burnout (see [Autistic Burnout](energy-and-burnout.md#autistic-burnout)).

**The general rule:** before each stage begins, decide what structure will replace the one you're losing.

---

## Leaving School and Early Adulthood

- **The structure cliff.** School supplies a timetable, a place to be, peers, and someone checking in. All of it ends at once. Build a replacement: fixed wake time, a weekly schedule, at least one regular place to be.
- **Support often drops at the same time.** In many countries, services and protections tied to school end in early adulthood. Find out in advance what adult support exists where you live.
- **Independent living skills** — cooking, money, cleaning, appointments — go into checklists and routines, not memory. See [Executive Function](executive-function.md).
- **Don't add every change at once.** New city, new job, new relationship, and leaving home in the same year is a lot of uncertainty stacked together.

---

## Higher Education

- **Register with the disability or accessibility office early,** before you need anything. In the US, colleges must provide reasonable accommodations under the ADA and Section 504 once you register and provide documentation; other countries have similar rules.
- **Common accommodations:** extra exam time, a quiet exam room, recorded lectures or notes, flexibility on group work, advance notice of schedule changes.
- **Group projects** are often the hardest part. Ask for roles, deadlines, and responsibilities in writing at the start.
- **The unstructured time** between classes is where students often drift. Fixed study blocks in a fixed place help.
- **Find your people** through clubs built around your interests. See [Finding Your People](social-decoding.md#finding-your-people).

## Partnership and Living Together

See [Relationships](relationships.md). The stage-specific point: moving in together removes your solitude by default. Design it back in — a room, a time, a signal — before you move, not after the first burnout.

## Parenthood

`[E: moderate]` In a large survey, autistic mothers reported more difficulty with multitasking, the social side of parenting (such as other parents and school staff), and feeling judged, alongside the same commitment to their children as non-autistic mothers (Pohl et al., 2020).

**What tends to help:**
- **Sensory planning.** Children are loud, unpredictable, and touch-heavy. Headphones during noisy play, a short quiet break when your partner or another adult can take over, and a recovery block at the end of the day.
- **Routines for the family** benefit everyone, and children often thrive on predictability too.
- **Delegate the social-heavy parts** where you can — school events, other parents, birthday parties — or plan them like any high-load event.
- **Your strengths are parenting strengths:** honesty, consistency, deep engagement with your child's interests, and taking their differences seriously.
- **Your child may share your profile.** `[E: strong]` Autism is highly heritable (see [Common Misconceptions](overview.md#common-misconceptions)). Understanding yourself gives you a head start in understanding them.
- **Ask for help early.** Parenting on an empty energy budget is how burnout happens.

---

## Midlife

- **Late recognition is common here** — often prompted by a child's diagnosis or a burnout. See [Identity and Unmasking](identity-and-unmasking.md).
- **Accumulated masking costs** can surface as exhaustion that no longer responds to rest.
- **For women,** `[E: weak]` autistic women describe perimenopause and menopause as intensifying sensory sensitivity, executive-function difficulty, and emotional load (Moseley et al., 2020, from interviews). If things get harder in this period, it may be hormonal, not a personal failing — raise it with a doctor.
- **Midlife is a good time to redesign.** You know far more about your wiring than you did at 25. Rebuild work, home, and relationships around what you've learned.

---

## Retirement

Retirement removes the biggest source of structure most adults have.

- **Plan the replacement structure before you stop working:** fixed wake time, regular commitments, a place to be on certain days.
- **Interests can become the structure.** Retirement is often the first chance in decades to go fully deep. See [Special Interests](special-interests.md).
- **Watch for isolation.** Work supplied social contact without effort. Replace some of it deliberately — interest groups, volunteering, a regular meeting.

---

## Growing Older

`[E: moderate]` A large study of US Medicare data found autistic older adults had higher rates of nearly every physical and mental health condition examined than the general older population (Hand et al., 2020). Research on autistic aging is still young.

- **Health monitoring becomes more important,** and interoception differences make self-detection harder. Keep check-ups on a schedule. See [Routine Check-Ups](health-and-care.md#routine-check-ups).
- **Routines become more protective,** not less.
- **Advocate early** for how you want care delivered if you ever need it: write down your sensory needs, communication preferences, and routines, and give a copy to the people who would be involved. Your [User Manual](user-manual-template.md) is a good start.
- **Keep the connections** that don't drain you. They matter more as other structure falls away.
