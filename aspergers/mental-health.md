# Mental Health

Anxiety and depression are the most common companions of the Asperger's profile — and the hardest to spot, because they hide inside traits you already have. Learn to tell the profile from the condition, treat the condition, and keep the profile.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim. This file is a self-management guide, not a substitute for professional care.

Part of the [Asperger's](overview.md) section. How to find and work with a therapist or doctor is covered in [Health and Care](health-and-care.md#mental-health-support); this file covers the conditions themselves.

---

## How Common

`[E: strong]` A meta-analysis of studies on autistic adults found about 27% had a current anxiety disorder and 42% had one at some point in life; for depression, about 23% current and 37% lifetime (Hollocks et al., 2019). Both rates are several times higher than in the general population.

These are not part of the profile. They are conditions that commonly come with it — and they are treatable.

---

## Telling the Profile From the Condition

Clinicians call it *diagnostic overshadowing* when a real condition is dismissed as "just your autism." It runs the other way too: autistic traits get mislabeled as disorders. Knowing the difference protects you from both.

| Profile trait | Possible condition | How they differ |
|---------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Preferring routine and sameness | **OCD** | Routines feel comforting and chosen; disruption is upsetting because the plan breaks. OCD compulsions are driven by an intrusive, feared thought, feel unwanted, and bring only brief relief. |
| Social difficulty — decoding by hand | **Social anxiety** | The profile is about *processing* social information. Social anxiety is *fear* of being judged. They often occur together, especially after years of being misread. |
| Needing withdrawal to recover | **Depression** | Withdrawal that restores you is recovery. Withdrawal where nothing restores you — not even your interests — points to depression. See [Burnout or Depression?](energy-and-burnout.md#autistic-burnout). |
| Flat facial expression or tone | **Low mood** | A flat presentation is common in this profile and says little about mood. Ask about loss of pleasure, hopelessness, and changes in sleep and appetite instead. |
| Intense focus on an interest | **Mania or obsession** | Interests are enjoyable, long-running, and stable. A sudden, unusual surge of energy, little need for sleep, and risky behavior are different — and worth a doctor's attention. |

When you're unsure, describe the *function*, not the behavior: "I do this because it calms me" points to the profile; "I do this because something bad will happen if I don't" points to anxiety or OCD.

## Anxiety

**Why it's so common here:**
- `[E: moderate]` Intolerance of uncertainty is a major driver (see [Uncertainty and Change](uncertainty-and-change.md#why-uncertainty-costs-so-much)).
- Years of social misreading and correction teach a reasonable fear of getting it wrong.
- A full [energy budget](energy-and-burnout.md) and sensory load leave little margin before stress tips into anxiety.
- `[E: moderate]` Alexithymia can make anxiety show up mainly in the body — stomach, chest, tension, sleep — rather than as a feeling you can name (see [Emotions](emotions.md)).

**The toolkit:**
- **Read the body first.** The three-step read in [Body First, Label Second](emotions.md#body-first-label-second) catches anxiety you might not name.
- **Cut the uncertainty you can.** Preview, ask, get it in writing. See [Reduce What You Can](uncertainty-and-change.md#reduce-what-you-can).
- **Worries as hypotheses.** Write the worry, then the probability you'd put on it, then the evidence for and against. Anxiety deals in certainties; you can answer in probabilities. See [Probabilistic Thinking](../mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md).
- **Scheduled worry time.** `[E: moderate]` Postponing worry to a fixed daily 15–20 minute slot reduced worry in trials (e.g., McGowan & Behar, 2013). When a worry comes up outside the slot, write it down and leave it for later.
- **Graded exposure,** when your energy is healthy. See [Building Tolerance, Gently](uncertainty-and-change.md#building-tolerance-gently).
- **Move.** `[E: strong]` Regular exercise reduces anxiety symptoms (meta-analyses in the general population). See [Fitness](../health/fitness.md).
- **Protect sleep.** Anxiety and poor sleep feed each other. See [Sleep](../health/sleep.md).
- **Treat it if it persists.** Adapted CBT, and sometimes medication, help. See [Mental Health Support](health-and-care.md#mental-health-support).

## Depression

**Signs to watch** — especially when they last more than two weeks:
- Loss of pleasure in things that usually work, including your interests
- Hopelessness, or feeling like a burden
- Sleep and appetite changing noticeably
- Slowed thinking or movement
- Withdrawal that doesn't restore you

`[E: moderate]` Loneliness and social exclusion are strongly linked to depression in autistic adults. Wanting connection and finding the routes to it hard is a painful combination — see [Finding Your People](social-decoding.md#finding-your-people).

**What helps:**
- **Behavioral activation.** `[E: strong]` Scheduling small, valued activities — even before you feel like doing them — is an effective treatment for depression (meta-analyses in the general population). For this profile, build it around interests: twenty minutes of an interest, a walk on a familiar route, one message to a safe person.
- **Structure.** Depression erodes routine, and lost routine deepens depression. Hold the anchors — wake time, meals, one outing — even at minimum strength. See [Routine Design](routine-design.md#when-the-routine-breaks).
- **Light and movement** in the morning. See [Daily Routine](../routines/daily.md).
- **Professional help.** Depression is treatable. Don't wait until it's severe.

## Trauma

`[E: moderate]` Autistic adults report more traumatic experiences than non-autistic adults, and some develop post-traumatic symptoms after events that fall outside standard definitions of trauma — repeated social exclusion, bullying, being restrained, or severe sensory overwhelm (Rumball et al., 2020).

- **Your reaction counts** even if the event "shouldn't" have been traumatic by someone else's standard.
- **Signs:** intrusive memories, avoiding reminders, being constantly on edge, nightmares, feeling numb.
- **Look for trauma-informed therapists** who also understand autism. See [Health and Care](health-and-care.md#mental-health-support).
- See [Grief and Loss](../grief-and-loss.md) and [Resilience](../resilience.md) for the broader frameworks.

## Your Early-Warning System

Because internal signals can be muted, build external ones:
- **Track mood alongside energy** in the weekly log — one word or a 1–5 number each day. See [Building It Into the Routine](energy-and-burnout.md#building-it-into-the-routine).
- **Ask one or two trusted people** to tell you when they notice changes: going quieter than usual, dropping interests, sleeping much more or less.
- **Write your warning signs** into your [crisis plan](health-and-care.md#write-a-crisis-plan-while-youre-well) while you're well.

## When It Gets Dark

If thoughts of not wanting to be here appear, treat them as an emergency signal: tell someone you trust today, and in the US call or text 988 (elsewhere, your local crisis line). The risk in this profile is real and is covered in [Autistic Burnout](energy-and-burnout.md#autistic-burnout).
