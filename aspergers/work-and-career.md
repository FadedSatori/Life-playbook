# Work and Career

The Asperger's profile can do unusually deep, valuable work — in an environment that doesn't spend all your energy on everything around the work. Choose the environment as deliberately as you choose the work.

## The Reality

`[E: moderate]` Autistic adults are employed at far lower rates than the general population, and many who are employed work below their qualifications. The gap comes less from ability than from hiring processes, environments, and social demands that have little to do with the work itself.

That is the problem to engineer around: not your capacity to do the work, but everything wrapped around it.

---

## Depth as Career Capital

Career capital is rare and valuable skill (see [Work Principles](../career/work-principles.md)). Deep, sustained focus on one subject is one of the fastest ways to build it.

**Look for the intersection of three things:**
1. A subject you go deep on without being told to
2. Something people will pay for
3. An environment you can sustain (below)

There is no list of "autism jobs." Any field rewards depth. Pick one you care about, then choose the environment inside it carefully.

## Accommodations

Most are cheap. Many you can ask for without disclosing anything:

- A written agenda before meetings, and notes after
- Questions in writing, or time to respond instead of answering on the spot
- Headphones treated as normal; a quiet seat; remote days
- Camera-off as an option on internal calls
- Clear, direct, written feedback
- Advance notice of changes
- A flexible start time

**Framing without disclosure:** describe how you do your best work, not what you need protecting from.
- "I do my best work with written specs — could you send the key points after we talk?"
- "I think better on my own first. Could I send my thoughts by end of day instead of answering now?"

## Disclosure at Work

| Disclose | Don't disclose |
|----------|----------------|
| Legal protection and formal accommodations | No stigma risk from the label |
| Colleagues read your behavior correctly | No assumptions made about your capability |
| Less masking | You control the narrative |
| — | Can always disclose later; can't un-disclose |

**The law, briefly.** In the US, the Americans with Disabilities Act requires employers with 15 or more employees to provide reasonable accommodations for qualified employees with disabilities — generally once the employer knows, and sometimes with documentation. In the UK, the Equality Act 2010 covers similar ground. Rules differ elsewhere.

**Options:** no disclosure (ask for accommodations as work preferences), partial (manager or HR only), or full.

**Timing:** usually after an offer, or when a specific problem needs an accommodation. Not as an apology after a conflict — then it reads as an excuse, and it deserves better than that.

**Script:** "I have Asperger's. For me that mainly means [two concrete things]. I do my best work when [one or two conditions]. I'm telling you so we can work well together." (Swap in "I'm autistic" if that is the term you use, or if the listener is more likely to recognize it.)

## Interviews

Interviews test social performance as much as ability. Prepare for the format, not just the content.

- **Prepare 6–8 stories** in STAR form (Situation, Task, Action, Result) that cover the common questions: a conflict, a failure, a hard problem, a time you led.
- **Ask about the format in advance:** who you'll meet, for how long, what kind of questions.
- **Ask for accommodations** where it helps: questions in advance, a written exercise instead of a whiteboard, a quiet room.
- **Bring evidence:** a portfolio, a work sample, a project. Let the work talk.
- **Pausing is allowed:** "Let me think for a second."

## Office Politics as a System

The unwritten rules are learnable. Treat them like any other system: observe, model, test.

- **The meeting before the meeting.** Decisions are often shaped in one-on-one conversations beforehand. If something matters, talk to the key people before the meeting, not just in it.
- **Map the stakeholders:** who decides, who influences, who is affected.
- **Observe before acting.** In a new job, watch how things work for a few weeks. Ask one trusted colleague: "What am I missing about how things work here?"
- **Make results visible.** Good work doesn't speak for itself. Send a short written summary when something ships.
- **Correct people privately,** with care → fact → path forward (see [Social Decoding](social-decoding.md#your-honesty-delivered-well)).
- **In meetings:** write your point down before you go in, use the chat, and ask for a turn if you can't find a gap.

## Receiving Feedback

Feedback is often delivered vaguely and softened — the opposite of what this profile processes best.

- **Ask for it specifically:** "What's the one thing I should change on this?" "Can you show me an example of what good looks like?"
- **Ask for it in writing** where you can, so you can process it in your own time.
- **Expect the delayed wave.** Critical feedback may land harder hours later. Don't respond in the moment if you don't have to: "Thanks — let me think about that." See [The Delayed Signal](emotions.md#the-delayed-signal).
- **Separate the work from your worth.** Feedback on a document is information about the document.
- **Ask whether it's a request or an observation.** "Is that something you need me to change, or just a thought?" Softened feedback is often a firm request in disguise (see [Subtext](social-decoding.md#subtext-a-translation-table)).

## Managing Up

Your manager's expectations are the most important unwritten rules at work. Make them written.

- **Clarify priorities explicitly:** "I have A, B, and C on my plate. Which matters most this week?"
- **Confirm in writing:** after a verbal instruction, send a two-line summary — "Just confirming: you'd like X by Thursday, with Y included." It catches misunderstandings early and creates a record.
- **Send a short weekly update** — done, in progress, blocked. It makes your work visible without small talk.
- **Raise problems early,** with a proposed fix: "This will slip by two days because of X. I can do Y to keep it on track — which do you prefer?"
- **Ask how they like to communicate** — chat, email, meetings — and match it for the routine stuff.

## Remote Work: Benefits and Traps

Remote and hybrid work can remove much of the hidden load: commuting, open-plan noise, constant interruptions, unplanned social contact.

**The traps:**
- **Isolation.** Losing all incidental social contact can deepen loneliness. Keep some deliberate connection — a regular one-on-one, a co-working day, a community outside work.
- **Blurred boundaries.** Deep focus plus a laptop at home means working until midnight. Keep a fixed end time and a shutdown ritual (see [Daily Routine](../routines/daily.md)).
- **Invisibility.** Out of sight can mean out of mind for promotions. Use the weekly update above, and turn up in person for the moments that matter.
- **Video-call load.** Cameras on all day is its own sensory and social drain. Ask for camera-optional internal meetings.

## Protecting Your Energy at Work

- Block deep-work time on the calendar and defend it. See [Work Principles](../career/work-principles.md).
- Batch communication instead of answering as it arrives.
- Plan recovery after heavy social demands — presentations, all-day workshops, offsites.
- Put remote or low-demand days after heavy days.
- Watch for the signs in [Autistic Burnout](energy-and-burnout.md#autistic-burnout). Work is the most common place the mismatch builds.

## Working for Yourself

Self-employment offers autonomy and control over your environment — both strong fits. It also adds sales, networking, and the load of running everything yourself.

- Consider a partner or contractor who handles the most social-heavy parts.
- Long-term clients beat constant new-client hunting.
- Systematize the business side early so it doesn't eat your focus.
