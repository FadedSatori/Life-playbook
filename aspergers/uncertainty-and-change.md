# Uncertainty and Change

Unpredictability is one of the heaviest costs this profile carries — not because you can't adapt, but because adapting means rebuilding the whole plan in your head. Reduce the uncertainty you can, prepare for the rest, and handle change with a protocol instead of a scramble.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, theory, or qualitative accounts only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice, framework, or preference, not an empirical claim.

## Why Uncertainty Costs So Much

`[E: moderate]` *Intolerance of uncertainty* — finding the unknown distressing and working hard to avoid it — is strongly linked to anxiety in autistic people, across a meta-analysis of studies (Jenkinson et al., 2020). It may be one of the main routes by which autism leads to anxiety.

Two implications:
- **Much of what looks like rigidity is a reasonable response to a real cost.** The plan in your head is how you hold the day together. Breaking it is expensive.
- **Uncertainty is a lever.** Reducing it — and getting better at tolerating what remains — is one of the most direct ways to lower anxiety.

---

## Reduce What You Can

Most uncertainty is optional. Remove it before it reaches you.

- **Preview the unknown.** Photos of the venue, a map, the menu online, the agenda, who will be there, how long it lasts, where to park.
- **Ask the questions others skip.** "What's the dress code?" "How long will it go?" "What exactly do you need from me by Friday?"
- **Get it in writing.** Plans, expectations, instructions.
- **Arrive early** to new places, before they fill up.
- **Fixed anchors in every day:** the same wake time, the same first hour, the same evening wind-down (see [Daily Routine](../routines/daily.md)). The more fixed the anchors, the more change the rest of the day can absorb.

## Prepare for What You Can't

**If–then plans.** `[E: strong]` Specifying in advance what you'll do when a situation arises reliably improves follow-through (implementation intentions; see [Reality Engineering](../reality-engineering.md)). Use them for disruptions:
- "If the train is canceled, then I take the bus from the north entrance."
- "If the meeting runs over, then I leave at 4:30 and email my notes."
- "If the restaurant is too loud, then I ask for the corner table or suggest the café next door."

**Plan B, written.** For anything important, write one alternative. You probably won't need it. Knowing it exists lowers the cost of the unknown.

**Flex slots.** Leave deliberate empty blocks in the week. When something changes, it has somewhere to go that doesn't break everything else.

## When Plans Change: The Protocol

Sudden changes trigger a spike. Have the steps ready so you don't have to think of them during it.

1. **Pause.** Don't react yet. "Give me a minute to rethink this."
2. **Breathe down** — a long exhale or two.
3. **Get the facts:** what exactly changed, what is still the same, and what the new deadline or time is.
4. **Re-plan on paper.** Write the new sequence for the rest of the day.
5. **Choose and commit.** The new plan is now the plan.
6. **Budget recovery** — a change costs energy even when it goes well.

Separate the change from the person who caused it. The cancellation may feel like disrespect when it is just logistics.

## Building Tolerance, Gently

`[E: strong]` Gradual, voluntary exposure to what you fear is the best-supported approach for anxiety in general. `[E: weak]` Its use specifically for uncertainty in autistic adults has less direct research, and it should never mean forcing yourself through overload.

**How to do it safely:**
- Only when your [energy budget](energy-and-burnout.md) is healthy — never during burnout.
- Start tiny: a new route home, an unfamiliar café on a quiet day, leaving one evening unplanned.
- You choose it, you control it, and you can stop.
- Notice afterward: it was survivable, and usually less bad than predicted. `[E: moderate]` People reliably overestimate how bad uncertain events will feel (see [Courage](../courage.md)).

The goal isn't to stop needing predictability. It is to widen the range you can handle when you have to.

## Big Transitions

Moving house, a new job, a breakup, leaving school, becoming a parent, losing someone. `[E: weak]` Autistic adults often describe major transitions as triggers for burnout (see [Autistic Burnout](energy-and-burnout.md#autistic-burnout)).

**The transition protocol:**
- **One big change at a time** where you have the choice.
- **Keep every routine you can** through the transition — same wake time, same food, same evening ritual. Constants carry you through the variables.
- **Reduce everything else** for the first few weeks. Fewer social commitments, fewer new projects.
- **Visit in advance:** the new office, the new neighborhood, the new commute — more than once.
- **Expect a dip** and plan for it. A hard first month is the normal cost, not a sign it was the wrong decision.

## Travel

Travel stacks every hard thing at once: noise, crowds, schedule changes, unfamiliar places, broken routines.

- **Over-plan the logistics** so your brain doesn't have to: tickets, routes, check-in times, backups, all in one document.
- **Travel off-peak** where possible.
- **Bring the [sensory kit](sensory.md#the-sensory-kit)** plus familiar food and something from home.
- **Keep your anchors** — same wake time, same morning sequence — even across time zones (see [Sleep](../health/sleep.md#travel-and-jet-lag-protocol)).
- **Schedule a recovery day** after you get back, before work resumes.

## Uncertainty About People

The hardest uncertainty is often social: did they mean it, are they upset, what happens next?

- **Ask instead of guessing** — see [Ask for Directness](social-decoding.md#ask-for-directness).
- **Think in probabilities, not certainties:** "It's maybe 70% they're just busy, not annoyed." See [Probabilistic Thinking](../mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md).
- **Separate what you control from what you don't** — the Stoic dichotomy of control is built for this. See [Stoic Practice](../stoic-practice.md).
