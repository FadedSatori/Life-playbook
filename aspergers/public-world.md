# The Public World

Shops, transport, airports, crowds, emergencies, and encounters with police or security are built for people who process noise, change, and strangers easily. Plan for them the way you'd plan for any high-load environment — and know what to say when your behavior might be misread.

## The General Rules

1. **Off-peak beats willpower.** The same shop at 8 a.m. on a Tuesday and 2 p.m. on a Saturday are different environments.
2. **Online beats in person** for anything routine — groceries, banking, prescriptions, tickets.
3. **Know the exit and the quiet spot** before you need them.
4. **Carry the [sensory kit](sensory.md#the-sensory-kit).** Always.
5. **Set the time limit before you go.** Leaving at it is the plan, not a failure.

---

## Shops and Errands

- **List first, route second.** Write the list in the order of the shop's layout. Fewer decisions on the floor.
- **Self-checkout** or contactless payment cuts the social exchange to nothing.
- **Same shop, same time** — familiarity lowers the cost every visit.
- **Batch errands** into one trip on one day, then recover.
- **When it's too much:** leave the basket, step outside, and come back later or order online. Nothing in a shop is worth a meltdown.

---

## Public Transport

- **Plan the route and a backup** before leaving. Know the platform, the stop, and the alternative if the line is down.
- **Use journey apps** with live updates so delays arrive as information, not surprises.
- **Choose the quiet spot:** quiet carriages, the end of the train, off-peak times, a window seat.
- **Headphones on,** with downloaded audio in case of no signal.
- **When plans change mid-journey,** run the [change protocol](uncertainty-and-change.md#when-plans-change-the-protocol): pause, get the facts, re-plan, commit.

---

## Airports and Long-Distance Travel

Airports combine almost every hard thing: crowds, noise, queues, security, time pressure, and announcements you might miss.

- **Ask for assistance in advance.** Many airports and airlines offer support for travelers with non-visible disabilities. In the US, TSA Cares provides help through security if you request it ahead of time. Many airports also have sensory or quiet rooms.
- **The Hidden Disabilities Sunflower** — a green lanyard with sunflowers — is recognized at many airports and transport hubs internationally as a discreet signal that you may need more time or help. Wearing one is optional and doesn't require disclosing a diagnosis.
- **Arrive early** and find the quiet spot first.
- **Security:** watch a few people ahead of you so you know the sequence. It's fine to tell staff, "I have Asperger's — I'm autistic — please tell me clearly what you need me to do." In official settings, naming both helps: "autistic" is the term more staff will recognize.
- See [Travel](uncertainty-and-change.md#travel) for the full protocol.

## Crowds and Events

- **Arrive early,** before it fills, and position yourself near an exit or edge.
- **Earplugs or headphones** at concerts, sports, and festivals — many venues allow filtered earplugs.
- **Take a break every 30–60 minutes** outside or somewhere quieter.
- **Have a meeting point** with whoever you're with, in case you need to step away.
- **Leave before the rush** at the end, or wait until it clears.

## Overload in Public

If it hits while you're out:
1. **Get out of the input** — step outside, into a bathroom, a car, a quiet corner.
2. **Headphones on, eyes down or closed.**
3. **Use your exit line** if anyone asks: "I'm okay — I just need a minute."
4. **Carry a card** if speech tends to go during shutdown (below).
5. **Get home** by the simplest route available, and recover. See [Overload, Meltdown, Shutdown](energy-and-burnout.md#overload-meltdown-shutdown).

## A Communication Card

Keep a card in your wallet and a copy on your phone's lock screen or medical ID. It speaks for you when you can't.

**Example:**
> *I am autistic (Asperger's). In stressful situations I may not be able to speak, may not make eye contact, and may need more time to answer. I am not being rude or uncooperative. Please speak clearly and simply, and give me time.*
> *Emergency contact: [name, phone]*

Add anything a stranger or medic would need: medications, allergies, how to help you calm down.

## Police, Security, and Emergencies

`[E: weak]` Autistic people and advocacy organizations report that ordinary autistic behaviors — little eye contact, delayed or literal answers, stimming, flat tone, needing to move or leave — can be misread by police or security as evasive, suspicious, or noncompliant. Preparation lowers the risk of a stressful encounter escalating.

**If you're stopped:**
- **Stay as calm as you can,** keep your hands visible, and don't make sudden movements or run.
- **Say it early:** "I have Asperger's — I'm autistic. I may need more time to understand and answer. I'm not trying to be difficult." Using both terms makes it more likely the officer recognizes what you mean.
- **Show your card** if words are hard — say that you're reaching for it before you do.
- **Follow clear, lawful instructions.** If an instruction is unclear, say so: "I don't understand — can you tell me exactly what to do?"
- **Answer literally and briefly.** Don't guess or fill silence.
- **If you're detained or arrested,** you can say you want a lawyer and then stop answering questions until one is present. Ask for any accommodations you need.

**In a medical emergency:**
- The card, a phone medical ID, or a bracelet tells responders about communication needs and contacts.
- Responders may misread a quiet, flat presentation as "not that serious." Say it directly, or have someone with you say it: "This is serious for me, even if I don't look distressed." See [Describing Symptoms](health-and-care.md#describing-symptoms).

## Scams and Strangers

Literal trust, politeness under pressure, and discomfort with conflict make this profile a frequent target for scams.

- **Urgency is the tell.** Anyone who needs money, codes, or a decision *right now* gets "I'll call you back on the official number."
- **Never share codes, passwords, or payment details** with anyone who contacted you — not the bank, not tech support, not a delivery company.
- **Verify independently,** using a number or website you found yourself.
- **Hang up, close the chat, shut the door.** You don't owe a stranger politeness when they're pressuring you.
- See [Protecting Yourself](social-decoding.md#protecting-yourself).
