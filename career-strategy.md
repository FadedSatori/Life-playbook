# Career Strategy

A career is a forty-year campaign fought in two-year engagements. Most people manage it one engagement at a time: take the next job that appears, stay until it hurts, take the next one. They end up wherever the current carried them.

Strategy means choosing the terrain. Which skills to build, which rooms to be in, which bets to make, when to stay and when to move. Small choices compound: a role with a fast learning rate at 28 changes the options you have at 45.

The principles of how to work (deep work, career capital, the craftsman mindset) live in [career/work-principles.md](career/work-principles.md). This file is the campaign map: how to navigate the market, the organization, and the decades.

---

## The Foundation: Rare and Valuable

Cal Newport's *So Good They Can't Ignore You* (2012), titled after advice from Steve Martin, makes the core argument: "follow your passion" is poor career advice. Passion tends to follow competence and autonomy. What you need first is **career capital**: rare and valuable skills the market will pay for. The full argument and the career capital audit are in [career/work-principles.md](career/work-principles.md). Don't skip it.

**The strategic additions:**
- **Capital buys control.** Autonomy, flexibility, and mission are the rewards of career capital. Demanding them before you have the capital usually fails.
- **Test with money.** Newport's rule of thumb: before you jump toward more control, check whether people are actually willing to pay you for the thing. Applause is not evidence. Payment is.
- **Know your market.** Rare and valuable is defined by buyers, not by you. A skill is valuable if someone with a budget has a painful problem it solves.

## Skill Stacking

You don't have to be the best in the world at anything. You need a combination of skills that few others hold. Scott Adams's version: being very good (roughly top 25%) at two or more complementary things can beat being the best at one. The principle is covered in [learning/skill-building.md](learning/skill-building.md).

**Applied to career:**
- **Domain plus communication.** An engineer who writes clearly, a lawyer who understands software, a nurse who can analyze data. The second skill multiplies the first.
- **Domain plus domain.** The intersection of two fields is often uncrowded. Finance plus healthcare. Construction plus project software.
- **Domain plus people.** Technical depth plus the ability to lead, sell, or negotiate is the classic path into senior roles.

**The stack audit:** List your top five skills honestly. For each pair, ask: who else has both at this level? Where does this combination solve an expensive problem? Pick the one or two combinations to deepen over the next three years.

## Generalist or Specialist

David Epstein's *Range* (2019) argues that in many fields broad early experience and late specialization outperform early, narrow focus. His central distinction comes from psychologist Robin Hogarth:

- **Kind learning environments:** rules are stable, patterns repeat, feedback is fast and accurate. Chess, golf, many technical trades. Here early specialization and deliberate practice pay. Tiger Woods is the archetype.
- **Wicked learning environments:** rules shift, feedback is delayed or misleading, problems don't repeat. Most strategy, management, entrepreneurship, policy. Here breadth, analogy, and the ability to transfer across domains pay. Epstein contrasts Roger Federer, who played many sports as a child.

Epstein also stresses **match quality**: the fit between the work and the person. Sampling broadly before committing tends to improve it. Philip Tetlock's forecasting research points the same way: in *Expert Political Judgment* (2005), "foxes" who drew on many ideas forecast better than "hedgehogs" committed to one big idea.

**When each pays:**
- **Specialize** when the field is kind, the rewards for top performance are steep, and you've tested the fit.
- **Generalize** when the field is wicked, changing fast, or you are still finding fit.
- **Most people should aim for T-shaped:** one deep spike, broad working knowledge around it. See [polymath-system.md](polymath-system.md).

## Choosing Roles

Judge every role on five factors. Score each 1–5 before deciding.

1. **Learning rate.** Will you be meaningfully more capable in two years? Stretch, feedback, and exposure to hard problems. Early in a career this outweighs almost everything.
2. **The people.** Your manager and your peers set your standards, your network, and your daily experience. Would you want to become like the most senior people there?
3. **The mission.** Do you respect what the organization does? You don't need to love it. You do need to not be ashamed of it.
4. **The pay.** Total compensation, trajectory, and stability. Don't pretend money doesn't matter, and don't let it decide alone. See [career/finances.md](career/finances.md).
5. **The optionality.** Does this role open more doors than it closes? Brand, skills, network, and portability all count.

**Weighting by stage:** early career weights learning rate and people. Mid-career weights optionality and mission. Late career weights mission and autonomy. Whenever you have dependents or debts, pay weighs more. That's fine; just be honest about it. **The disqualifiers:** an employer that asks you to lie, a manager you already distrust, a role whose main skill is becoming obsolete.

## The Hidden Job Market

Many roles are filled through people before or instead of public postings. The exact share is unknown and widely exaggerated. The direction is not in doubt: referrals carry weight that cold applications don't. Mark Granovetter's research on weak ties is covered in [networks.md](networks.md). A large 2022 study by Rajkumar and colleagues in *Science*, using LinkedIn's randomized "People You May Know" experiments across about 20 million users, found that **moderately weak ties** (acquaintances with some mutual connections) did the most for job mobility, more than close friends or near strangers.

**The referral protocol:**
1. **Build before you need it.** A network built in a crisis looks like begging. See [networks.md](networks.md).
2. **Make a target list** of 15–30 organizations you'd want to work for.
3. **Find a path in** to each: alumni, former colleagues, friends of friends.
4. **Ask for conversations, not jobs.** "I'm exploring moves into X. Could I get 20 minutes on what it's like there?"
5. **Be specific and easy to help.** One sentence on what you do and what you're looking for. Offer a short blurb they can forward.
6. **Follow up and give back.** Thank them, report what happened, and help them when you can.

## Interviews Are Two-Way

The interview is not an exam you sit. It is a mutual evaluation, and you are holding the other half.

**Prepare your evidence:**
- Six to eight concise stories of real accomplishments in **STAR** form (situation, task, action, result), each with a number or a concrete outcome.
- Include one failure and what you changed.
- Know why you want this role specifically, in one honest sentence.

**Interview them:**
- "What does success look like in this role at six months and one year?"
- "What's the hardest problem this team is facing right now?"
- "Why is this role open?"
- "How are decisions made here when people disagree?"
- "What did the last person who got promoted from this team do?"

**Do your own reference check.** Talk to people who have worked there, especially those who left. Ask about the manager specifically. Most bad jobs are bad managers.

**Red flags:** vague answers about the role, turnover nobody explains, pressure to decide immediately, interviewers who disparage colleagues, a mismatch between the stated values and how you are treated during the process.

## Salary Negotiation

The tactics are in [negotiation.md](negotiation.md): anchoring, BATNA, packaging, the scripts. The career-specific points:

- **Compounding.** Raises and future offers are often calculated from your current pay. A gap at the start of a job can persist for years. One uncomfortable conversation can be worth a great deal over a career.
- **Your BATNA is your leverage.** A competing offer or a job you're happy to keep changes the conversation. Never fabricate one.
- **Know the market.** Use salary surveys, posted ranges, and conversations with peers. A growing number of jurisdictions require employers to post pay ranges, and some prohibit asking about your salary history. Check the rules where you live.
- **Negotiate the whole package:** base, bonus, equity, title, start date, remote flexibility, learning budget, review timing.
- **Equity needs scrutiny.** Understand vesting, strike price, dilution, and what happens if you leave. Treat early-stage equity as a lottery ticket until proven otherwise. See [wealth-building.md](wealth-building.md).
- **Get it in writing** before you resign from anything.

## Managing Up and Getting Promoted

**Managing up** is not flattery. John Gabarro and John Kotter's Harvard Business Review article "Managing Your Boss" (1980) framed it as managing a relationship of mutual dependence. Understand your boss's goals, pressures, strengths, blind spots, and preferred working style. Then make their job easier.

**The rules:**
- **No surprises.** Bad news early, with a proposed fix.
- **Learn their format.** Some want a one-line summary, some want the data. Give it to them that way.
- **Bring options, not just problems.** "Here's the issue, here are two ways forward, I recommend A."
- **Clarify priorities explicitly.** "Of these three, which matters most this month?"
- **Make commitments small and keep every one.**

**Getting promoted:**
1. **Perform at the next level before you get it.** Promotions usually ratify work you're already doing.
2. **Make impact visible.** Keep a running record of accomplishments with outcomes. Julia Evans calls this a brag document. It writes your review, your promotion case, and your résumé.
3. **Ask explicitly.** "What would I need to demonstrate to be promoted in the next year?" Get the answer specific, then check in on it.
4. **Earn sponsors, not just mentors.** A sponsor spends their reputation advocating for you in rooms you're not in. See [mentorship.md](mentorship.md).
5. **Watch the glue work.** Engineer Tanya Reilly's term for the coordinating, unblocking, onboarding work that holds teams together but often goes unrecognized. Do some, make it visible, and don't let it become your whole job unless that path is recognized.

**On visibility:** Harvey Coleman's PIE model argues that advancement depends on Performance, Image, and Exposure, weighted roughly 10/30/60. Those percentages are his opinion, not measured data. The directional point holds: excellent work that nobody with decision power sees rarely gets rewarded.

**The Peter Principle warning.** Laurence Peter's 1969 idea that people get promoted until they reach their level of incompetence has some evidence behind it. A study of sales workers by Benson, Li, and Shue found firms promoted their best salespeople, who tended to be worse managers. Management is a different job. Take it because you want to do that job, not because it's the only way up. Where an expert track exists, consider it seriously. See [power-dynamics.md](power-dynamics.md) for organizational politics and [leadership.md](leadership.md) for leading once you're there.

## When and How to Leave

**Signals it's time:**
- Your learning rate has been flat for a year and nothing on the horizon will change that.
- You no longer respect the leadership, or you are being asked to compromise your ethics.
- The best people are leaving and nobody asks why.
- Your health or relationships are paying for the job, persistently.
- You can't name what you'll gain by staying two more years.

**Before leaving:**
1. **Try to fix it first,** if it's fixable: a direct conversation, a transfer, a new scope.
2. **Build runway.** Several months of expenses saved changes how you negotiate and how you decide. See [career/finances.md](career/finances.md).
3. **Line up the next thing** before you resign, unless your health or integrity demands otherwise.
4. **Read your contract.** Notice periods, non-compete, non-solicitation, IP assignment, and clawbacks on bonuses or equity. Enforceability varies widely by jurisdiction; for anything material, get legal advice.

**Leaving well:**
- Tell your manager first, in person or on a call, before anyone else hears.
- Be gracious and brief about why. You don't owe a grievance list.
- Leave clean handover documents. Finish or hand off everything.
- Treat counteroffers with suspicion. The reasons you wanted to leave usually survive the raise.
- Stay in touch. Former colleagues are the core of your future network.

## Pivots and Portfolio Careers

**Pivot by experiment, not by leap.** Herminia Ibarra's *Working Identity* (2003) found that people who successfully change careers rarely plan their way there. They test and learn: side projects, temporary assignments, new networks, conversations with people already doing the work. Identity shifts through action. Bill Burnett and Dave Evans's *Designing Your Life* (2016) adds two ways to prototype before committing: prototype conversations (talking to people living the life you're considering) and odyssey plans (three sketches of genuinely different five-year paths).

**Adjacent moves win.** The most reliable pivot changes one variable at a time: the same skills in a new industry, or a new role in the same industry. Changing both at once means starting near zero.

**Plan A, B, Z.** Reid Hoffman and Ben Casnocha's *The Start-up of You* (2012): Plan A is your current path, Plan B is the adjacent pivot if A stalls, Plan Z is the fallback that keeps you safe if everything fails. Knowing your Plan Z makes bolder Plan A bets possible.

**Portfolio careers.** Management writer Charles Handy popularized the idea of a portfolio working life: several streams of paid work, plus unpaid work you choose, rather than a single employer. It trades the security of one paycheck for resilience across many. It suits people with established capital and a strong network, not beginners. See [entrepreneurship.md](entrepreneurship.md) and [antifragility.md](antifragility.md).

## Resilience Against Automation and AI

Nobody knows the exact shape of the coming disruption. Be skeptical of confident percentages about which jobs disappear. What the evidence does support:

- **Automation hits tasks, not whole jobs.** Economists including David Autor have shown that technology tends to replace routine tasks and reshape the jobs around them. Ask which of your tasks are routine, and which aren't.
- **What's hard to automate shifts.** Moravec's paradox noted that sensorimotor skills easy for humans were hard for machines, while formal reasoning was comparatively easy. Current AI is eroding routine cognitive work faster than physical-world work, which is a reversal of older expectations.
- **Judgment, trust, and accountability stay human longest.** Someone still has to decide, take responsibility, persuade, and be trusted.

**The protocol:**
1. **Audit your tasks** quarterly: which could a capable AI do today, which in a few years, which are hard to replace?
2. **Use the tools first.** The person who uses AI well tends to beat the person who doesn't, long before AI replaces either. See [technology-leverage.md](technology-leverage.md).
3. **Move toward the hard-to-replace part** of your work: client relationships, ambiguous problems, physical presence, leadership, final accountability.
4. **Keep learning on a schedule,** not in a panic. See [learning/skill-building.md](learning/skill-building.md).
5. **Keep financial runway.** Disruption is easier to survive with savings. See [career/finances.md](career/finances.md).

## The Yearly Career Review

Do it once a year, separate from the life review in [routines/annual-review.md](routines/annual-review.md). Two hours, written.

**Looking back:**
1. What did I accomplish this year? (Pull from your brag document.)
2. What skills grew measurably? Which stagnated?
3. What did I learn about what energizes and drains me?
4. How did my network change? Who are the five most important new relationships?
5. What did I earn, and how does it compare to the market for my skills?

**Assessing position:**
6. Score my current role on the five factors (learning, people, mission, pay, optionality).
7. What is my rarest and most valuable skill combination? Is it stronger than a year ago?
8. Which of my tasks are most exposed to automation?
9. Who are my sponsors? If none, why not?
10. If I lost this job tomorrow, how long would it take to land a comparable one?

**Looking forward:**
11. What three-year position do I want? Write Plan A, B, and Z.
12. What one skill will I build this year, and how will I prove it?
13. What one relationship will I invest in?
14. Do I stay, grow in place, or move? What would change my mind?
15. What's the single most important career action for the next 90 days? Put it into your execution system. See [execution-systems.md](execution-systems.md).
