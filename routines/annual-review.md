# Annual Review

The annual review is the highest-leverage reflection practice in the system. Not because the year is the right unit of time — it isn't, not for everything — but because 12 months is long enough to show the compound effects of decisions, short enough to remember the arc, and carries enough weight (the symbolic boundary of the year) to invite genuine honesty.

Run this in the last week of December or first week of January. Block 2–3 hours minimum. Go somewhere quiet and offline. Bring: this file, your calendar, your journal from the past year, and a blank notebook.

The purpose is not to produce goals. Goals are the output. The purpose is to produce accurate seeing — an honest accounting of where you are, how you got here, what it cost, and what the year revealed. From that foundation, the year ahead can be designed rather than guessed at.

---

## Before You Begin: The Calibration Exercise

Before reviewing the year, do this for 10 minutes:

Write the answer to: **"If I had to describe the year in one paragraph — not the highlights, the actual experience — what would I write?"**

Not the version you'd post. The version you'd tell your closest honest friend. This paragraph becomes the anchor for everything that follows.

---

## Part 1: Year in Review

### The Honest Accounting

Work through these in order. Don't rush. Write full sentences — bullet points produce shallow reflection.

**What were the ten most significant moments of the year?**
Not just accomplishments. Significant moments — good, bad, pivotal. Include the moment a relationship changed, the conversation that shifted something, the decision that cost you, the day you surprised yourself.

**For each life area, what went well, and what didn't?**

Go through each domain honestly:

- **Health (body, energy, performance):** What worked? What did you neglect? What did your body show you about how you're living?
- **Career / Work (output, skill development, direction):** What moved? What stalled? What are you actually building?
- **Finances (savings rate, progress toward freedom, financial clarity):** Where are you on the wealth sequence? What decisions were sound? What was avoidance?
- **Relationships (depth, reciprocity, presence):** Who showed up for you? Who did you show up for? What relationships deepened? What atrophied?
- **Learning / Growth (knowledge, skills, frameworks):** What did you genuinely absorb this year? What shifted how you think or act?
- **Adventure / Experience (novelty, physical challenge, aliveness):** Did you do things that required something of you? Did you choose comfort when you could have chosen growth?
- **Inner life (clarity, spiritual health, coherence between values and actions):** Are you becoming who you intended to become?

**Where did you compromise your values?**
Not catastrophically — the quiet compromises. The time you went along with something you disagreed with. The thing you let slide because confronting it felt expensive. Name them specifically.

**What surprised you — positive and negative?**
The surprises are where the most useful information lives. The world's response to your actions reveals your assumptions.

**What did you learn?**
Identify the top 5 genuine lessons. Not "I learned I need to be better at X" — actual insights that changed how you see or act. "I learned that I overestimate my tolerance for uncertainty when money is involved." "I learned that my best creative work happens when I don't know what I'm making."

## Part 2: The Four-Question Life Audit

These four questions, adapted from Clayton Christensen's framework and expanded, produce more honest data than any goal review:

**1. Am I allocating time to what matters most?**
Look at your actual calendar for the past 12 months (not your intended calendar). Where did your hours actually go? The calendar reveals what you actually prioritized, not what you said you prioritized. Is there alignment?

**2. Am I managing my inputs, or are my inputs managing me?**
What information, relationships, and environments were running you, rather than serving you? Social media? A particular relationship dynamic? A work context that was draining rather than developing? What do you need to change at the input level, not the output level?

**3. Did I follow my principles or manage appearances?**
The specific moments where you compromised are the data here. The person who manages appearances rather than principles accumulates a particular kind of exhaustion — the exhaustion of maintaining a version of themselves that isn't real. Where did that happen?

**4. Who did I become, and is that who I intended to become?**
Not what you did — who you became through what you did. What habits did you consolidate? What ways of seeing and being became more automatic? Are you more or less the person you wanted to be a year ago?

## Part 3: The Deathbed Test

This is the clarifying lens. At the end of your life, looking back at this year, what will you be most glad you did? What will you most regret?

**Write the answer to each:**

"At 85, I will be most glad that in this year I..."

"At 85, I will most regret that in this year I didn't..."

This is not about mortality anxiety. It is about accessing the clear priorities that the noise of daily life obscures. The person dying with clarity about what mattered is giving you the most accurate signal available about how to live now.

---

## Part 4: The Anti-Goals

Most annual reviews generate goals — things to do, achieve, and become. The anti-goals are at least as important: the things you are explicitly committing to *stop doing, stop tolerating, or stop becoming*.

**List 5–10 specific anti-goals for the coming year:**

What are the patterns, behaviors, relationships, or contexts that are costing you in ways that compound? Be specific: not "I'll stop wasting time" but "I'll stop opening social media before 10am" or "I'll stop staying in conversations I know are unproductive out of politeness."

Anti-goals are often more powerful than goals because they remove the friction that goals are fighting against. You cannot run at maximum capacity toward your goals while a known drag is operating in the background.

## Part 5: Year Ahead

### The Theme

Choose one word or phrase that represents what this year is about. Not a goal — a direction. A quality to inhabit, a capacity to develop, a commitment to embody.

**Examples:** "Depth over breadth." "Finally finishing." "Presence." "Ruthless clarity." "The creative year." "Building the foundation."

> This year is the year of _______________

Let the theme emerge from the review, not from aspiration. What does the honest accounting of last year suggest the next year needs to be?

---

### Goals by Area

Set 1–3 goals per area. Fewer is better — most annual goals fail not because they're wrong but because there are too many of them and none receive sufficient attention. An annual goal that doesn't become a monthly focus is aspirational noise.

The format: **outcome** (what changes), **metric** (how you'll know), **first action** (what you'll do in the next 7 days).

### The Non-Negotiables

What will you protect this year regardless of what else happens?

These are the behaviors and practices whose consistent presence predicts everything else functioning. Not goals to achieve — foundations to maintain. For most people: training, sleep, a daily practice, a minimum relationship with the people who matter most, and some form of reflection.

Name them specifically. Five to eight is enough. More than eight is aspirational; the real non-negotiables are few.

---

### The One Dominant Priority

Of everything in the year ahead, what is the one thing — career project, relationship work, inner transformation, health change — that, if it goes well, makes every other area easier or better?

That is the dominant priority. Give it disproportionate attention. Review progress on it every week. The person who achieves their dominant priority and nothing else has had a better year than the person who made small progress on everything.

## Part 6: The Past Year Letter

Optional, but highly recommended.

Write a letter to yourself from the end of this coming year — dated December, written from the perspective of someone who lived the year fully and well. What happened? What did you build? Who did you become? What challenges arose and how did you navigate them?

This is not visualization magic — it is narrative priming. The brain's future-planning systems are partly narrative. Writing the story of who you'll be creates a compelling attractor that influences daily decisions. The letter becomes a reference point.

Seal it or store it, and read it at next year's review.

---

## Part 7: Gut Check

Close with these. Write full-sentence answers.

- Am I living in alignment with my [core principles](../principles.md)?
- Is the life I'm building the one I actually want?
- What would future me wish I'd started this year?
- What am I afraid to admit is true?
- What do I know I need to do that I haven't done yet?

The last two are the most important. The answers to those are almost always already known. The review's job is to make them unavoidable.

---

## Closing

The review is complete when you have an honest accounting of the past year and a clear intention for the next one — not a perfect plan, an intention. The intention is what you carry into daily decisions, weekly reviews, and monthly check-ins.

File this review. Return to it at mid-year. Compare what you wrote to what's actually happening. Adjust.

The annual review is not an annual event. It is the anchor of the whole review system. See [weekly-review.md](weekly-review.md) for the practice that keeps the annual review honest by maintaining continuity through the year.
