# Weekly Review

Without this ritual, the default mode is reactive drift. Weeks blur into months. Months blur into years. You work hard and go nowhere because effort without direction is just expensive wandering. The weekly review is the system that keeps the compass pointing. It is not optional. It is the operating system update that prevents decay.

---

## The Why: Drift vs. Direction

Most people live their weeks reactively. The inbox sets the agenda. The loudest voice wins the hour. The urgent crowds out the important. At the end of the week there is a vague sense of busyness and a vague dissatisfaction that nothing that actually mattered got done.

The weekly review interrupts this. It is a deliberate withdrawal from the current to assess the trajectory. You cannot correct a course you cannot see. The review is the act of seeing.

**What the review does:**
- Closes open loops that generate low-grade anxiety and cognitive drag.
- Forces honest accounting of time and priority — not as you wish they were, but as they actually are.
- Reconnects daily actions to long-range direction. Without this, five years of hard work can leave you at the wrong destination.
- Identifies patterns before they become entrenched. One week of avoidance is information. Three weeks in a row is a system.
- Creates a clean handoff from this week to next, so Monday begins with clarity rather than chaos.

The review does not make life easier. It makes life *legible*.

---

## Time and Environment

**When:** Sunday evening or Monday morning. Pick one and make it immovable. Sunday evening processes the week before rest. Monday morning processes before the week begins. Both work. Inconsistency does not.

**Duration:** 45–60 minutes for the full review. Set a timer. This creates urgency and prevents it from expanding into two hours of journaling.

**Location:** Not your normal workspace. The workspace is associated with reactive work — email, tasks, deliverables. The review requires a different posture. A coffee shop. A different room. Outside if weather permits. The environmental shift signals a different mode of thinking: meta, not object-level.

**Materials:** Pen and paper for the reflective sections. The physical act of writing processes differently than typing — slower, more deliberate, more honest. Use the digital system only for capture and scheduling tasks.

**The one rule:** Complete the review before the week takes over. A review you intend to do after Monday morning has begun is a review that does not happen.

---

## The Full Protocol

Work through these sections in order. Each has a function. Skipping sections is acceptable under time pressure — but always do sections 1, 3, 5, and 6 at minimum. Never skip the next-week architecture.

---

### Section 1: Capture and Clear

**Purpose:** Empty all inboxes and the mental RAM so the review operates on complete information, not a partial picture.

**Time:** 10–15 minutes.

**The rule:** Nothing gets reviewed until everything is captured. Reviewing an incomplete picture produces incomplete conclusions.

**Sweep every inbox:**

- [ ] Email inbox processed to zero (or triaged to actionable folders)
- [ ] Text messages and DMs — anything requiring a response logged or replied
- [ ] Physical notebook or paper scraps — transcribe all captured items to the trusted system
- [ ] Physical inbox/desk — any papers, mail, or objects requiring action
- [ ] Voice memos, photo captures of whiteboards, screenshots of ideas
- [ ] Brain dump: sit for two minutes and write anything still orbiting in mental RAM. Promises made. Things you've been meaning to do. Nagging background concerns. Get them out.

**The goal:** by the end of this section, nothing important lives only in your head or in an un-processed pile. Everything is in the system.

---

### Section 2: Review Last Week

**Purpose:** Honest accounting of what happened. Not what you wish happened. Not a PR version of the week. The truth.

**Time:** 10 minutes.

**The rule:** be specific. "Had a good week" is not useful. "Finished the proposal draft, trained four times, had dinner with Sarah, missed the client call prep entirely" is useful.

**Answer each question with one to three sentences:**

**The wins:**
- What were the three most important things you accomplished this week?
- Be specific. Name the project, the outcome, the decision. Not "I worked hard on fitness" — "I hit every training session and added five pounds to my squat."
- Why do wins matter to name? Because the brain disproportionately registers problems. Forcing the wins creates an accurate record and trains attention toward what is working.

**The failures and the truth about them:**
- What did not get done that was supposed to get done?
- For each: was it a *priority failure* or a *circumstance*? Be ruthlessly honest. Priority failure means you chose something else, consciously or unconsciously. Circumstance means something outside your control intervened.
- Priority failures are information about your actual values versus your stated values. They tell you what you actually choose when the choice is real. Do not look away.

**The energy audit:**
- What drained you this week? Name the specific activities, contexts, or people that cost energy without proportionate return.
- What gave you energy? Name the specific moments of flow, engagement, or aliveness.
- The pattern over three weeks tells you something important about alignment.

**The pattern signal:**
- Is there anything recurring? The same task avoided for the third time? The same person generating friction? The same morning starting strong and collapsing by noon?
- One data point is noise. Three is a pattern. Name the pattern before it names you.

---

### Section 3: Projects Review

**Purpose:** Nothing gets lost. Every active project gets eyes on it once a week. Inattention is how important things die slowly.

**Time:** 10 minutes.

**The two questions for every project:**

1. **Is it moving?** Something has happened on this project since last week. If the answer is no for two consecutive weeks, this is not a live project — it is a source of guilt. Either recommit with a specific next action or remove it from the active list.

2. **What is the next physical action?** Not "work on the proposal." "Draft section 2 of the proposal, estimated 45 minutes, requires the Q3 data from the shared drive." A next physical action is concrete, completable in one sitting, clear enough that the next version of you knows exactly what to do without having to re-figure it out.

**Additional project questions:**
- Is this project still worth doing? Circumstances change. What was important three weeks ago may no longer be. Kill it if it has lost its purpose.
- Is the deadline realistic? If not, renegotiate now, not the day it is due.
- Does this project have a dependency I need to resolve? Who is holding a ball that needs to be retrieved?

**The project list rule:** if you would not feel comfortable if someone important in your life saw everything on your project list, your project list is not your real project list. The real one includes the things you are avoiding.

---

### Section 4: The Five Life Areas Audit

**Purpose:** A life without a career is incomplete. A career without health is unsustainable. Health without relationships is hollow. The five areas are the whole — auditing each prevents the common failure mode of optimizing one area into dysfunction while neglecting the others.

**Time:** 5–8 minutes.

**Rate each area 1–10:**
- 1–3: Broken. Requires immediate triage.
- 4–6: Functional but not thriving. Minimum viable but no surplus.
- 7–8: Strong. Moving. Has momentum.
- 9–10: Exceptional. Reserve these for weeks that genuinely warrant it. Inflation makes them meaningless.

---

**Health**

Score: ___

- Did I train with the frequency and quality I committed to?
- How was my sleep? Quantity and quality.
- Was my nutrition aligned with the current protocol?
- What is my energy level relative to last week?
- What one thing would most improve my health next week?

---

**Work / Mission**

Score: ___

- Did the most important work move forward, or did I spend the week on secondary tasks?
- Am I building toward the long-term mission or just managing the current season?
- What is the highest-leverage thing I could do in this domain next week?
- Am I challenged enough to grow, or running on autopilot?

---

**Relationships**

Score: ___

- Did the people who matter most in my life actually get my presence this week, or just my physical proximity?
- Which relationship needs investment right now and is not getting it?
- Did I do the thing I said I would do for someone else?
- What would the people closest to me say about how I showed up this week?

---

**Finances**

Score: ___

- Did spending align with my stated priorities?
- Am I on track with savings, investment, or debt reduction targets?
- Any financial decisions deferred that need to be made?
- Is there anything I am spending money on that does not reflect my actual values?

---

**Learning**

Score: ___

- What did I read? What did I finish?
- What did I actually learn that changed how I think or act?
- Is my learning input connected to a skill or domain I am actively building, or is it just interesting content?
- What is the gap between what I know now and what I need to know to get to the next level?

---

**The pattern read across all five:**

After scoring, ask: is any area consistently below 6? Three consecutive weeks below 6 is a crisis in slow motion. Treat it as such.

Is any area at 9–10 while another is at 3? The high score is often coming at the cost of the low score. That trade-off is real. Name it.

---

### Section 5: Next Week Architecture

**Purpose:** The week is designed now, before Monday's momentum takes over. Designed weeks outperform reactive weeks by a factor that compounds.

**Time:** 8–10 minutes.

**The three non-negotiable outcomes:**

Write exactly three. Not five. Not eight. Three outcomes that, if achieved, make next week a success regardless of everything else that happens.

The format: "By Friday, [specific, concrete, observable outcome]."

Examples that work:
- "By Friday, the first draft of the restructuring proposal is complete and sent to the team."
- "By Friday, I have run four times and completed the full strength block."
- "By Friday, I have had a real conversation with my father."

Examples that do not work:
- "Work on the project."
- "Be healthier."
- "Connect more."

Vague outcomes are wishes. Specific outcomes are commitments.

**Deep work blocks scheduled:**

Pull up the calendar now. Find the blocks of 90–120 minutes where the most important cognitive work will happen. Put them in the calendar before anything else. Every other meeting, call, or obligation works around these blocks — not the other way around.

If you cannot find three deep work blocks in the coming week, your week is already overcommitted. Something needs to be cut.

**Relationship commitments:**

Name one specific thing for one specific person. "Call Dad Tuesday evening." "Take the kids to the park Saturday morning." "Send the handwritten note to Marcus." Relationships that exist only as vague intentions do not survive schedules.

**The elimination:**

What is on the calendar or the task list that should not be there? One thing to decline, defer, or delegate. Every week, something. The alternative is slow accumulation until the system seizes.

---

### Section 6: The Alignment Check

**Purpose:** The calendar is the most honest document in your life. It shows what you actually chose, not what you say you value.

**Time:** 5 minutes.

**The time audit:**

Look at last week's calendar and compare it to how time actually went.

Ask:
- What percentage of my work time was in deep work versus reactive tasks?
- Did the time allocation match the stated priority order?
- What got more time than it deserved? What got less?

If health is a stated top-three priority and received zero scheduled time last week, it is not actually a top-three priority. The calendar does not lie. Adjust the calendar or adjust the stated priority.

**The values question:**

Take your stated core values — or the principles file — and ask: did this week's actions reflect those values, or contradict them? Not judgment. Data collection. The gap between stated values and enacted values is the distance between who you are and who you are becoming.

**The calendar redesign:**

If the audit reveals misalignment, adjust next week's calendar before closing the review. Not "I'll try to do better." Change the blocks.

---

### Section 7: The Growth Question

**What did I learn this week that I did not know last week?**

One sentence. Something specific. Not "I learned a lot." Name the specific idea, insight, skill, or piece of feedback that moved your understanding.

If you cannot answer this question, the week was information-poor. That is a signal. Not a verdict — a signal. Add more deliberate input: a chapter read, a conversation sought, a skill practiced, a difficult piece of feedback absorbed.

---

### Section 8: The Energy Question

**At the current trajectory, where will I be in 90 days?**

This is the most important question in the review. Everything else is rearview mirror. This is the windshield.

Project forward. If the current week is representative of the next twelve: where does health land? Where does the mission land? What do the relationships look like? What is the financial position?

If the honest answer is "worse," this week is not a rough patch — it is a direction. Change the direction. Not through motivation. Through structural change: different calendar, different environment, different commitments.

If the answer is "better," identify what specifically is driving the improvement. Double it.

## The Quick-Review Version

For weeks where 45–60 minutes is genuinely unavailable. This is not the default — it is the contingency. Run the full review at least three weeks out of four.

**20 minutes. Five prompts. Always in this order.**

1. **Capture (5 min):** Quick sweep — email, notebook, brain dump. Capture everything. Don't process, just capture.

2. **Three wins from last week (2 min):** Write them. No commentary.

3. **Projects: next action for each active project (5 min):** Go through the list. One line each: what is the next physical action? If you don't know, that is the action: figure out the next step.

4. **Three outcomes for next week (3 min):** Write them. Specific and observable. By Friday, _____.

5. **Schedule the deep work (5 min):** Open the calendar. Put in the blocks. Close the laptop.

The quick review does not replace the full review. It maintains minimum viable direction so the week does not become purely reactive.

## Making the Review Non-Negotiable

The review is the hardest habit to maintain because it produces no immediate visible output. You cannot point to what you built. You cannot show someone the work. The return on a well-done weekly review is a life that does not drift — and that benefit is invisible in the moment.

**Environment design:**

Same time. Same place. Same ritual. The brain forms associations between environment and behavior. Remove the decision — the review happens Sunday at 7 PM at the kitchen table with coffee, or it happens Monday morning at the coffee shop before email. It does not happen when it feels convenient. Convenience is the enemy of the ritual.

**The pre-commitment:**

Tell someone. Block the calendar. Make it harder to skip than to do. The friction against doing the review should be lower than the friction against skipping it.

**Track the streak:**

Record that the review was done. A simple checkmark in the monthly tracker. The streak becomes its own motivator. "I have not missed a weekly review in eleven weeks" is a fact worth protecting.

**The cost of skipping:**

Skipping once is manageable — you catch up the following week. Skipping twice begins drift. Skipping three consecutive weeks is a month of operating on stale information, unreviewed projects, and unexamined trajectory.

Two compounding effects of consistent skipping: first, the open-loop cognitive load accumulates, producing the diffuse low-grade anxiety of a person who is always behind and never sure why. Second, the weeks begin to look identical because there is no mechanism for course correction. The review is the mechanism. Without it, the default takes over.

The default is not neutral. The default is decline.

---

## The Closing Statement

Every review ends with one sentence written at the top of next week's planner page or notebook:

**This week, the most important thing is ___________.**

One thing. Not three. Not a list. One. Write it before the week begins so that when the week becomes difficult — and it will — you know what to protect.

The review is complete. The week can begin.
