# Social Tactics

Specific moves for reading and working with people. High-leverage, low-visibility. People are the environment you operate in most — learn to read it.

---

## Reading People

### Baseline First

Before you can read a deviation, you need a baseline. Spend the first 3–5 minutes of any interaction in calibration mode only. What's this person's natural pace, volume, eye contact frequency, blink rate, hand placement, and postural default? That's your reference frame. Changes from that baseline are signals. The baseline itself is noise.

Baseline questions to answer silently:
- Do they make eye contact naturally, or do they look away when thinking?
- Are their gestures small and contained, or expansive?
- What's their default facial expression when listening?
- How much do they touch their face, hair, or neck in normal speech?

**Context-dependency:** Baselines shift with context. Someone at a job interview and someone at a backyard barbecue will show different baselines even with the same personality. Establish baseline per context, not per person globally.

### Paul Ekman's Microexpressions (FACS)

Paul Ekman's Facial Action Coding System (FACS) identified seven universal emotions that flash across the face in 1/25 to 1/5 of a second — fast enough to be missed, slow enough to be trained to catch. These are pre-voluntary; they express before the conscious mind can suppress them.

The seven universals: happiness, sadness, surprise, fear, disgust, anger, contempt.

The operationally critical ones:

**Contempt** — the single reliable asymmetric expression. One side of the mouth pulls back and up, creating a half-smirk. If you see contempt during a negotiation or relationship conversation, the damage is serious. Contempt signals not just disagreement but fundamental disrespect. It predicts relationship breakdown more reliably than anger (John Gottman's research confirms this). When you see it, you're not dealing with a fixable disagreement — you're dealing with a hierarchy problem.

**Fear** — brows raised and pulled together, upper eyelids raised, lower eyelids tense. Lips stretch horizontally. Someone who shows fear microexpressions when you're discussing a topic they claim to be comfortable with is not comfortable. Follow the thread.

**Disgust** — upper lip raised, nose wrinkled. Appears in split-second responses to proposals, plans, people. Disgust is one of the most honest involuntary reactions — it's nearly impossible to fake and nearly impossible to suppress. If someone shows disgust at your proposal before their verbal response, their verbal response is the cover story.

**Anger** — brows lowered and pulled together, vertical lines between brows, eyes hard, lips thinned or pressed together. Controlled anger is the dangerous kind. The person who shows brief anger flashes while speaking calmly is the one to watch.

### Cluster Reading

Single signals mislead. Clusters confirm. You need three consistent signals from the same emotional category before drawing a conclusion.

A cluster of deception-consistent signals:
- Speech rate increases or decreases sharply
- Self-touch (face, neck, collar) increases
- Directional eye gaze shifts from baseline pattern
- Verbal precision drops (vague language, hedging)
- Micro-expression of fear or contempt during supposedly neutral speech

Never accuse based on one signal. Never ignore three consistent signals from the same cluster.

### Deception Tells

**Pacifying gestures:** The limbic system activates under stress or deception and produces self-soothing behaviors — touching the neck, stroking the hair, rubbing the thigh, adjusting clothing. These are the body's attempt to self-regulate. Baseline everyone first; then note what increases. The pacifying gesture tells you *something* is stressful — it doesn't tell you what.

**Speech disfluencies under stress:** "Um," "uh," "you know," "like" — filler rates increase under cognitive load. Lying requires maintaining false narratives, which is cognitively expensive. Filler rates spike. Also watch for: excessive detail (over-explaining unprompted), detail changes across tellings, unusual formality ("I did not do that" instead of "I didn't"), and the use of distance language ("that woman" instead of "she").

**The too-perfect answer:** Rehearsed lies are often cleaner than the truth. When the story is suspiciously smooth, coherent, and complete, probe the edges. Ask about peripheral details — what they were wearing, what time it was, what happened before. Rehearsal only covers the core narrative.

**Eye behavior myths:** The popular NLP claim that eye direction predicts lying has been largely debunked. Don't use gaze direction as a deception indicator. Use gaze *changes from baseline* and *avoidance on specific topics* only.

**What liars often get right:** Confident liars maintain strong eye contact precisely because they know the folklore about eye avoidance. The guilt-consistent behavior is more subtle — slight voice pitch rise, micro-expressions, pacifying gestures.

## Status Dynamics

### Goffman's Face-Work

Erving Goffman's concept: every social interaction is a performance in which participants protect their own "face" (public self-image) and extend courtesy to protect others'. Face threats — moments where someone's status or identity is challenged — create friction, defensiveness, and political fallout. Understanding this is understanding office politics, difficult conversations, and negotiation simultaneously.

Rules:
- **Never publicly embarrass someone** unless you're willing to accept them as a permanent enemy. Embarrassment in front of witnesses is never forgotten. Always allow face-saving exits.
- **Give people a way to change their position without losing face.** "Given new information, I think we should reconsider" is a ladder out. Offer ladders.
- **Disagreements with subordinates in public are almost always mistakes.** The status asymmetry means you're attacking someone who can't effectively defend themselves. They will remember.

### Status Games in Organizations

Status is tracked, traded, and defended constantly — and mostly implicitly. Every meeting, email, and hallway conversation is a status transaction. Ignoring this doesn't make you above it; it makes you blind to it.

**Status signals in meetings:**
- Who speaks first, who speaks last, who gets interrupted, who gets backed up when interrupted
- Whose ideas get credited vs. whose ideas get absorbed and re-attributed
- Who the room physically orients toward when they speak
- Who asks questions vs. who answers them

**The attribution problem:** Ideas spread through organizations without consistent attribution. Track your ideas when they matter. Strategic reminders ("as I mentioned in the proposal last week...") are not ego — they're status maintenance.

**Positional vs. earned status:** Positional status (title, org chart) and earned status (reputation, track record, expertise) operate independently and sometimes in opposition. The junior analyst with deep expertise on a critical topic outranks the VP in that domain. Recognize both ladders and play them appropriately.

### How to Elevate or Lower Status Deliberately

**To elevate someone's status:** Credit them publicly, ask for their opinion in front of others, reference their expertise, defer to their judgment on their domain. This builds loyalty and creates reciprocal elevation.

**To lower status without direct confrontation (use sparingly, ethically):** Ask clarifying questions they can't answer well. Let their mistakes stand without covering for them. Redirect credit appropriately when they've overclaimed. Silence when they expect validation.

**The status penalty of trying too hard:** This is critical. In any room, the person who signals effort — trying to be funny, trying to be liked, trying to impress — loses status relative to the person who is simply present. Calm competence > visible striving. The harder someone tries to demonstrate status, the more they reveal they don't have it automatically. Arrive. Speak when you have something to say. Don't perform.

**Low-status signals to eliminate:**
- Laughing before the joke lands
- Qualifying statements preemptively ("This is probably wrong, but...")
- Seeking validation ("Does that make sense?") when explaining your domain
- Speeding up when someone else starts talking
- Explaining your credentials unprompted

## Negotiation

### Voss's Tactical Empathy Framework

Chris Voss (FBI hostage negotiator, *Never Split the Difference*): the goal in negotiation is to make the other side feel so understood that they become open. Not liked — understood. There's a difference.

**The Late-Night FM DJ Voice:** Slow, calm, slightly downward inflection. Used when you need to make a statement that cannot be argued with, set a boundary, or deliver a difficult truth without triggering defensiveness. The tone carries authority without aggression. Practice it. It's a deployable state.

**Mirroring:** Repeat the last 1–3 words the other person said as a question. "As a question?" Yes. It creates a near-irresistible compulsion to elaborate. The other side fills the silence with useful information. You offer nothing; you gain everything.

**Labeling:** Name what you observe without judgment. "It seems like you're frustrated with how this has been handled." "It sounds like this is more complex than it first appeared." Labels do three things: (1) demonstrate that you're paying attention, (2) slow down the emotional reactivity, (3) invite correction — if you're wrong, they tell you what's actually true, which is useful. Use "It seems like..." or "It sounds like..." — never "I think you feel..." which sounds presumptuous.

**The Accusation Audit:** Before making a difficult request or entering a contentious conversation, preemptively name every negative thing the other side might be thinking about you. "You probably think I'm going to ask for more than is reasonable. You're probably thinking this is the same conversation we've had before. You might even feel like I don't value your constraints." When you name the accusations first, they lose their power. The other side can't use them as weapons because you've already disarmed them.

**"That's right" vs. "You're right":** "You're right" is a capitulation signal — it means "stop talking." "That's right" signals genuine recognition. Work toward getting the other side to say "that's right" — it means they feel understood, not just agreed with. That emotional state is where deals happen.

**Calibrated Questions:** Open-ended questions that invite problem-solving without giving away your position. The most powerful: "How am I supposed to do that?" (in response to an unreasonable demand). It's not a concession. It's not a refusal. It invites them to solve your problem, which psychologically shifts ownership of the constraint. Other high-value calibrated questions:
- "What makes this so important?"
- "How does this decision get made?"
- "What happens if we can't find a path forward?"
- "Who else is affected by this?"

**"No" as an opener:** Voss's counter-intuitive insight — "no" is a useful answer that should be welcomed, not feared. It creates safety. When someone can say no, their subsequent yeses are real. Engineered yes responses are brittle. Ask questions designed to hear a no: "Is now a bad time to talk?" instead of "Do you have a minute?" Getting someone to say no first makes them feel in control, which makes the negotiation safer.

### The Mechanics of Anchoring

The first number in any negotiation exerts gravitational pull. This is the anchoring effect (Tversky and Kahneman): subsequent judgments are biased toward the anchor even when the anchor is arbitrary.

**Anchor first, anchor high (or low).** Before the other side introduces a number. If you're buying, anchor low. If you're selling, anchor high. The anchor sets the range within which bargaining occurs — it doesn't have to be your final position.

**The range anchor:** Voss recommends "bracketing" — offer a range where the bottom is what you actually want. "I was thinking somewhere between X and Y." The other side hears the top and bottoms out at your real number.

**Counter-anchoring:** When the other side anchors first and it's extreme, don't accept the range. Reframe: "I appreciate you sharing that. Let me share what I'm working with on my end..." then anchor hard in the other direction before negotiating toward the middle.

**Never negotiate against yourself.** If they reject your offer, ask for their number. Silence or a label. Do not move preemptively.

### BATNA Architecture

Your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement (Fisher and Ury, *Getting to Yes*) is your actual leverage. Not your position, not your arguments — your alternatives.

Build your BATNA *before* the negotiation begins. The better your alternatives, the less you need this specific deal, and the more confident your posture will be — which is visible and changes the dynamic. Walk-away power is the only real power.

**Improving your BATNA mid-negotiation:** If the negotiation isn't going well, pause. Develop alternatives. Come back with better leverage. Time spent improving your BATNA before returning is never wasted.

**The silence protocol:** After making an offer or ask — stop. Do not speak. The discomfort of silence is almost universal. Most people will fill it. What they say in that silence is the most honest data you'll get. Whoever breaks first cedes leverage.

## Persuasion

### Cialdini's Six Principles

Robert Cialdini (*Influence*) identified six principles that trigger compliance automatically — they short-circuit deliberate analysis and produce near-automatic agreement responses.

**1. Reciprocity:** People repay what they receive. Give first, give genuinely, and give unexpectedly. The unsolicited gift creates the most powerful obligation. In professional contexts: share useful information freely, make introductions without being asked, solve problems they didn't ask you to solve. The obligation this creates is real.

**2. Commitment and Consistency:** Once people commit to a position — especially publicly — they feel internal pressure to behave consistently with it. The foot-in-the-door technique: small initial commitment → larger subsequent request. Written commitments are more powerful than verbal ones. Public commitments are more powerful than private ones. Before asking for a large commitment, engineer a small, aligned one first.

**3. Social Proof:** Under uncertainty, people look to what others are doing. "Most people in your position do X" is persuasively potent — not because it's logical but because belonging and conformity are deep biological drives. This explains why testimonials, case studies, user counts, and expert consensus all work. Use it ethically. Recognize it when it's being used on you.

**4. Authority:** People defer to credible expertise. Titles, credentials, experience markers, and confident delivery all activate this. You don't always need to establish authority yourself — having someone else establish it for you is more effective (the third-party introduction). When presenting recommendations, lead with why you're credible on this specific topic.

**5. Liking:** We say yes to people we like. Similarity, familiarity, compliments, and association with positive things all increase liking. Practically: find genuine common ground early, use names, express authentic appreciation. The key word is *genuine* — manufactured rapport is detectable and backfires.

**6. Scarcity:** Opportunities appear more valuable when they're rare. Deadlines, limited availability, unique access — these trigger loss aversion (Kahneman), which is roughly twice as motivating as equivalent gains. "This offer is available through Friday" is not just a sales tactic — it's a cognitive architecture fact. To resist this in yourself: when urgency is injected into a decision, deliberately pause and evaluate why.

### When Logic Works vs. When It Backfires

Logic persuades people who are already in an analytical, low-threat state and who don't have an emotional stake in the opposite position. This is less common than you think.

When logic backfires:
- When the other person has publicly committed to the opposing view (consistency principle)
- When the topic is identity-adjacent (political, moral, religious, tribal)
- When there's a power differential and you're the subordinate making the logical case (it reads as challenging)
- When the person is in an emotionally activated state (the prefrontal cortex goes offline under stress)

**The sequence:** emotion → relationship → logic. In that order. Get the emotion acknowledged first. Build or affirm the relationship. Then make the logical case. Reversing the order produces resistance.

### Narrative Persuasion

Stories bypass analytical defenses. The brain processes narrative differently than it processes argument — it activates default mode network (simulation) rather than analytical circuits. You're not being argued at; you're experiencing a simulated reality.

**Transport:** the degree to which a story pulls someone into its world. Higher transport = lower counter-arguing = more persuasion. Make stories vivid, specific, and emotionally coherent. Abstractions don't transport. Details do.

**The single character identification:** the most persuasive stories have one character the audience identifies with (not a team, not a concept — a person). That person has a problem, takes action, faces setback, and reaches resolution. This is the Hero's Journey in 15 seconds.

**Case studies beat statistics.** One identifiable victim moves more resources than aggregate data about thousands (Kahneman's "singularity effect"). This is a bias you should recognize and work against in your own reasoning, but use when persuading.

### Loaded Framing and the Overton Window

Framing is the context in which a choice is presented. Identical options produce different choices depending on how they're framed (Tversky and Kahneman's framing effect): "90% survival rate" and "10% mortality rate" describe the same outcome but activate different responses.

**Frame control as a primary persuasion tactic:** whoever sets the frame wins more often than whoever makes the best argument within the frame. Reframing is therefore more valuable than refuting. Don't argue on their terms; shift the terms.

**The Overton Window** (Joseph Overton): there is a range of positions that are considered acceptable in any context. Ideas outside the window are dismissed as extreme. The window can be moved by introducing positions beyond the current extreme — suddenly the previously extreme position looks moderate. Negotiators, politicians, and change agents use this deliberately. When you see an extreme proposal, ask what position it's designed to make look reasonable.

## Social Proof and Tribalism

Most decisions are not individual — they're tribal. The question isn't "is this logical?" but "is this what people like me do?" Belonging is a primary human drive that supersedes reason in most conditions.

**How to work with tribalism, not against it:**
- Frame new behavior as consistent with existing group identity, not as a change from it. "This is what leaders at this level do" beats "you should try something different."
- Identify the tribes your audience belongs to before choosing your argument
- When you're the outsider trying to influence a group, find an insider champion before you make the case yourself — social proof from a trusted peer is worth more than any external logic
- The fastest way to create belonging: shared adversity, shared language, shared ritual. Groups that have been through something together have stronger bonds than groups that have merely agreed about something.

**The boomerang effect:** directly challenging someone's tribal belief triggers reactive reassertion — they hold it more strongly. Don't attack the belief. Acknowledge the legitimate concern behind it, then address that concern.

## Handling Difficult People

### The High-Conflict Personality Pattern

Bill Eddy's high-conflict personality (HCP) pattern: approximately 10–15% of people have persistent patterns of all-or-nothing thinking, unmanaged emotional reactivity, crisis-generating behavior, and unresolved trauma that they project outward. They are not bad people — they are genuinely in pain — but they require a different operating protocol.

**HCP markers:**
- Every conflict is existential to them
- There is always a villain (and it's usually whoever disappointed them last)
- They recruit allies aggressively (triangulation — see below)
- They escalate instead of de-escalating when confronted
- Pattern repeats across relationships and contexts

**Protocol with HCPs:** BIFF — Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm. Keep communication short. Don't explain extensively — HCPs use explanations as material for counter-argument. Don't defend — it reads as confirmation that there's something to defend. Don't capitulate — it reinforces the crisis pattern. Do maintain boundaries consistently.

### Triangulation

Triangulation: the HCP or manipulative person introduces a third party to create coalition pressure on you. "Everyone agrees with me on this." "I talked to X and they think you're wrong." The goal is to make you feel isolated and to use social proof as a weapon.

**Counter-triangulation:**
- Don't accept secondhand characterizations of what third parties think. Go direct.
- Don't enter the triangle. Respond only about your own relationship with the person in front of you.
- "I can't speak to what others think. What I can speak to is what I see between us."

### The Manipulator's Playbook and Counter-Moves

**Guilt-tripping** ("After everything I've done for you..."): Counter — acknowledge the contribution without accepting the debt. "I appreciate what you've done. That doesn't change what I'm deciding."

**Moving the goalposts:** They agreed to a standard; when you meet it, the standard changes. Counter — document agreements. Cite them when the posts move. "We agreed the standard was X. Here's where we are on X."

**Weaponized incompetence:** They perform helplessness to avoid responsibility. Counter — document requests and responses. Stop rescuing. Allow natural consequences.

**The pressure campaign:** They repeat the same pressure until you capitulate. Counter — the broken record (calm, consistent restatement of your position without engaging with the pressure). Most pressure campaigns collapse within 3–5 cycles when met with calm refusal.

**Love bombing / devaluation cycling:** Extreme approval followed by extreme criticism in cycles. Counter — recognize the pattern. The high phases are not real; the low phases are not your fault. Exit when the pattern is confirmed.

## Building Deep Rapport: The FBI RAVEN Model

FBI hostage negotiators use RAVEN as a rapport framework:

**R — Rapport:** Establish before you need it. You cannot build rapport in crisis. Rapport is built in the ordinary moments. Find genuine commonality: background, interest, experience. Commonality → trust → openness → cooperation.

**A — Active Listening:** Active listening is not waiting for your turn. It's full attention on the other person's meaning, subtext, and emotional state — not just their words. Behavioral markers of active listening: matching posture subtly, nodding on emphasis (not constantly), asking follow-up questions about what they said, not about what you want to know next.

**V — Verbal Communication:** Use their language. Match vocabulary register (formal/informal). Refer back to things they said earlier. Verbal callback signals you were genuinely listening and elevates their sense of being understood.

**E — Empathy:** Not sympathy (feeling what they feel) — empathy (accurately understanding and naming what they feel without judgment). Demonstrated through labels, questions, and behavioral adjustment based on their emotional state. Empathy does not require agreement. You can empathize with a position you disagree with completely.

**N — Nonverbal Communication:** 55–65% of communication is nonverbal (Mehrabian's approximate figures, often oversimplified but directionally valid). Posture, pace, physical proximity, gesture, facial expression. Deliberate nonverbal adjustments: lean in slightly when they're sharing something important, create appropriate physical space when they seem pressured, slow your own pace to bring down an accelerating conversation.

**The rapport shortcut: reflective listening loop.** Listen → label → pause → let them correct or expand → label again. Four cycles of this and you know more about what someone actually thinks than they've consciously articulated.

## Exit Tactics

**The clean conversation exit:** "I need to let you go" (makes the break their freedom, not your rejection). "I want to think more carefully about what you said before I respond" (elevates them, creates space). "I'll follow up by [specific date/action]" (replaces awkward closing with forward motion).

**The meeting exit:** Begin wrapping up 5 minutes before you need to leave, not at the moment. "Before we run out of time, let me make sure we've covered X" is both a signal and a service.

**The relationship exit:** Clean exits are better than slow fades. Slow fades create ambiguity and often more pain. A direct, kind, specific closing ("I've valued this, and I need to step back from it for X reason") is harder in the moment and better in the long run. It preserves dignity on both sides.

**The toxic relationship exit protocol:** Don't explain extensively. The explanation becomes negotiating material. State the decision (not the debate). Execute consistently. Expect pressure and increased pursuit immediately after (the relationship extinction burst). Maintain the boundary through it.

## Digital Social Intelligence

**Email/text tone reading:**
- Short responses from someone who usually writes long ones = disengagement, irritation, or time pressure. Don't catastrophize — ask.
- Unusual formality = emotional distance. Someone who usually writes informally switching to complete sentences and titles is creating psychological space.
- Lack of questions = low engagement. Conversational investment shows up as curiosity.
- "Noted" = your message was received and the person has nothing positive to say about it.
- All-caps (outside of normal usage) = emotional activation.

**Response latency as signal:**
- Long delay from someone who usually responds quickly = either they don't know what to say (complexity/conflict) or they're deprioritizing you (status signal)
- Immediate response from someone who usually takes time = something made this urgent for them
- Don't read latency in isolation — read it against their baseline and the topic of the last message

**The read receipt trap:** Seen but not responded to is information. It's not always deliberate. But if it's consistent across topics that matter to you, it's a pattern to address directly rather than stew over.

**Professional email discipline:** The quality of your written communication is a constant status signal. Grammar, precision, appropriate length, and logical structure are read as indicators of how you think. A sloppy email to a senior person is a status deduction. Reread before sending anything consequential.

**Never write what you wouldn't say.** The permanence of written communication means anything in writing can be screenshotted, forwarded, quoted, and used outside context. If you wouldn't say it in a room, don't write it. This is not timidity — it's strategic self-preservation.
