# Field Tactics

Situational awareness, environmental intelligence, improvised problem-solving, performance under pressure. The body is in the field even when the mind thinks it's elsewhere.

---

## Situational Awareness

### Jeff Cooper's Full Color Code

Jeff Cooper's color code defines mental readiness states with specific behavioral protocols at each level. Most people conflate this with a threat-detection system; it's actually a preparedness state management system.

**White — Unaware/Switched Off:**
Mentally elsewhere. Attention fully internalized. No environmental scanning occurring. Normal for sleep, trusted private environments, established routines in genuinely safe contexts.

*The mistake:* defaulting to white in public, unfamiliar environments, transitions (entering/exiting buildings, parking structures, transport hubs). When something happens in white, reaction time is measured in surprised seconds.

*The HALT condition:* awareness degrades predictably when you are Hungry, Angry, Lonely, or Tired. Under HALT conditions, you will drift toward white without noticing. Recognize when you're in a HALT state and compensate deliberately — slow down, take stock more frequently, make decisions more conservatively.

**Yellow — Relaxed Alertness:**
Externally aware, internally calm. Scanning is continuous but not tense. You're asking "What's here?" not "Where's the threat?" This is the default state for all public and unfamiliar environments.

Yellow is sustainable indefinitely — it is not vigilance, which is exhausting and unsustainable. It's more like peripheral vision than staring: wide-aperture awareness without fixation.

*Behavioral protocol in Yellow:*
- Location of exits (two minimum)
- Position relative to choke points and open space
- Who is in the space and what's the baseline behavior?
- Where would you go if the environment changed suddenly?
- What in this space could serve as cover vs. concealment?

Yellow is the mode where you notice the person who doesn't fit the environment's pattern, the bag left unattended, the car that has passed three times. You're not looking for threats specifically — you're registering the full picture, which makes anomalies visible.

**Orange — Specific Alert:**
A specific person, object, or situation has triggered your attention. Focus narrows. You've asked "What is that?" and haven't yet answered it.

*Behavioral protocol in Orange:*
- Continue scanning the rest of the environment (the thing you're watching may be a distraction)
- Increase physical readiness — no crossed arms or legs, weight forward, unencumbered hands
- Begin pre-positioning: move toward cover, toward an exit, toward high ground
- Do not stare fixedly at the trigger — use peripheral attention, which is harder to detect
- Begin developing a response option: "If X, I will Y"

The transition from yellow to orange is the critical skill. Most people go from white to orange only when something has already happened. Train to transition from yellow to orange on early indicators.

**Red — Action State:**
You've decided to act or act is already required. The decision was made in yellow and orange — red is execution.

Performance in red is determined almost entirely by what was done in yellow and orange (positioning, awareness, pre-loaded decision) and by training. Under real threat conditions, fine motor control degrades, tunnel vision narrows, time perception distorts. Only gross motor patterns that have been drilled to automaticity are reliable.

*The Cooper principle:* "You do not rise to the occasion — you fall to your training." This is why the work happens in white and yellow, not red.

**Black — Overwhelm:**
Cortical function severely degraded. Freeze response or panic. Occurs when the event exceeds training, when conditions are beyond any schema in memory, or when physical state (exhaustion, injury) has degraded the nervous system. The exit from black is a single action commitment: pick the one most important thing and do only that until the immediate crisis is resolved.

## Advanced OODA: Boyd's Full Theory

### The Loop Is Not Just Speed

John Boyd's OODA (Observe-Orient-Decide-Act) is commonly reduced to "whoever cycles the loop faster wins." Boyd's actual insight was more sophisticated.

The loop:

**Observe:** raw sensory data — what you can see, hear, feel, and sense in the environment. Quality of observation depends on how many sensors you're using (not just eyes), your positioning, and your attentional state. Observation is degraded by fixation (tunnel vision), distraction, and mental busyness.

**Orient:** the most important and least understood element. Orientation is the mental model you bring to interpreting observations. It includes:
- Previous experience and training
- Cultural conditioning and assumptions
- Mental models and analogies
- Current analysis and synthesis

Boyd's key insight: **orientation shapes what you observe and what you decide, not just how you interpret.** A degraded or incorrect mental model means you'll misinterpret observations and make bad decisions even if you're fast. The quality of your orientation is determined by the quality of your mental models.

**Decide:** select from available options. Decision quality is a function of how many relevant options exist in your orientation (have you trained for this?), your cognitive state (can you think?), and time pressure.

**Act:** execute the decision. Action generates new observations, feeding the next loop cycle.

### Speeding Up Your Own Loop

- **Observation speed:** reduce cognitive load on observation by eliminating irrelevant stimuli (environment design), improving positioning (more information faster from better vantage), and maintaining yellow baseline (available attention to notice new data).
- **Orientation speed:** built by training — more reference cases, wider cross-domain mental models, explicit after-action review that encodes learning into memory. The operator who has mentally rehearsed 1,000 scenarios orients faster than one who has 10.
- **Decision speed:** pre-built decision rules eliminate real-time deliberation. Implementation intentions ("if X, then Y") remove the need to decide under pressure. The decision was made in training, not in the moment.
- **Action speed:** pattern drill. Trained actions execute faster and more reliably than improvised ones.

### Slowing Down the Opponent's Loop

This is the second half of Boyd's theory and it's largely omitted in popular retellings. You can win by going faster, but you can also win by making the opponent unable to complete their loop reliably.

**Observe degradation:** create ambiguity, obscure your intentions, present multiple stimuli simultaneously. When they can't read the environment reliably, their loop breaks at the first step.

**Orientation degradation:** behave inconsistently with their mental model. Do what they do not expect. Their orientation is built for a certain version of you — be a different version.

**Decision degradation:** increase the complexity and pace of events beyond their processing capacity. Present multiple simultaneous problems. Force decisions with inadequate time and information.

**Action degradation:** disrupt execution before it completes. The physical application of this (in combat: interrupt their movement) is obvious, but the business/negotiation application is equally real: if they commit to an action and circumstances change mid-execution, they're degraded.

### Mental Models as the Real Competitive Advantage

Boyd ultimately argued that the quality of your mental models — your orientation — is the decisive variable. Speed is an amplifier; model quality is the determinant.

Mental models relevant to field work:
- Crowd dynamics (how groups behave under stress vs. normal conditions)
- Human movement patterns (what normal pedestrian flow looks like; what deviations signal)
- Environment reading (architectural chokepoints, sight lines, concealment vs. cover)
- Threat behavior patterns (how pre-attack behavior presents; how criminals select targets)
- Emergency response patterns (what happens in the first 2–5 minutes of an incident)

Building mental model library: study case histories, read incident reports, train scenarios (not just technique), debrief your own experiences. The faster you can say "this is a version of that" — the faster your orientation.

## Risk Assessment

### Threat Modeling Framework

Risk = Asset × Threat × Vulnerability × Likelihood.

**Asset:** what are you protecting? Physical safety, information, resources, relationships, reputation. Be explicit. You cannot protect everything with equal priority. Rank your assets.

**Threat:** who or what could harm the asset? Be specific — "crime" is not a useful threat. "Opportunistic property theft in an unmonitored parking structure" is actionable. "Identity theft through phishing targeted at my professional email" is actionable.

**Vulnerability:** what weaknesses allow the threat to affect the asset? Vulnerabilities are what you can directly improve. The unlit parking structure, the predictable commute route, the reused password, the unlocked car, the isolated position in the restaurant.

**Likelihood:** realistic probability given context. Most people overweight dramatic, vivid threats (shark attack, stranger abduction) and underweight statistically significant ones (car accidents, falls, interpersonal violence from known parties). Use base rate data to calibrate.

### Probability vs. Impact Matrix

For each identified risk: estimate probability (low/medium/high) and impact (low/medium/high) independently.

Priority order:
1. High probability / High impact — address immediately, mitigate as close to zero as possible
2. Low probability / High impact — pre-position resources (insurance, contingency plans, emergency gear)
3. High probability / Low impact — reduce friction, build tolerance, accept some occurrence
4. Low probability / Low impact — accept, do not spend resources

**The catastrophic risk exception:** very low probability events with catastrophic and unrecoverable impact (severe injury, death, permanent loss) warrant mitigation disproportionate to their likelihood. A 0.1% chance of a fatal outcome deserves more mitigation than a 40% chance of a recoverable financial loss. Irreversibility changes the calculus.

### Pre-Positioning vs. Reactive

Pre-positioning is the deliberate placement of resources, knowledge, and capacity before they're needed. Reactive positioning is improvisation under pressure with degraded cognition.

Pre-positioning examples:
- Medical kit staged at home, in vehicle, in go-bag — before you need it
- Knowing the location of urgent care, emergency rooms, and trauma centers in your city — before you need them
- Cash reserve in known location — before the ATM network is down
- Memorized routes and exits — before the GPS is unavailable
- Knowing your blood type, medication list, and emergency contacts — before you can't communicate them

**The cost of pre-positioning is small and certain. The cost of not pre-positioning is large and contingent but real.** Pay the small cost.

## Surveillance Detection

### Passive vs. Active Surveillance

Surveillance precedes most targeted crimes, terrorist attacks, and personal threats. Recognizing it early changes the outcome.

**Passive surveillance:** watchers who are stationary or moving with the environment — sitting, standing, waiting, loitering in positions with good sight lines to you or your location. Signs:
- Prolonged presence in a position without obvious purpose
- Orientation toward you or your location rather than the normal flow of attention
- Reappearance in multiple locations or at multiple times without explanation

**Active surveillance:** following movement. The challenge is that normal city life includes apparent coincidence. The distinguishing feature of surveillance is repetition across contexts that cannot be plausibly explained by coincidence.

**The three-encounter rule:** one encounter is coincidence. Two encounters is possible coincidence. Three encounters across distinct contexts and time gaps is a pattern that requires a decision.

### SDR: Surveillance Detection Routes

An SDR is a deliberate movement route designed to reveal whether you're being followed, without revealing that you know you might be followed. The goal is detection, not evasion — evasion comes after confirmation.

**SDR design principles:**
- Use chokepoints — narrow passages or doorways through which a follower must pass (and be seen)
- Use view cuts — turns that take you out of sight briefly, then allow observation back toward where a follower would be
- Vary pace and direction unexpectedly but plausibly (stop in a store, change direction for a stated reason)
- The SDR should have a plausible reason for the route — you're not running patterns, you're "running errands"

**Urban SDR basics:**
1. Enter a store, linger near the entrance, watch who enters after you
2. Take a bus or train, get off at the last second
3. Walk around an entire city block — anyone following must also complete the loop
4. Use a reflective surface (store window, parked car) to observe behind you without turning

### The ABC Model

In counter-surveillance: **A (Attacker)** is the primary threat, **B (Blocker)** is a team member controlling your movement options, **C (Cover)** is rear surveillance. Knowing this model lets you recognize coordinated surveillance rather than just individual followers. If you see two people who seem to be coordinating their positions relative to you, you're potentially seeing A and B or A and C of a surveillance team. The third person you don't see yet is the indicator to act on.

### What Follows You

Consider what patterns your life creates:
- Predictable routes (home → gym → work → coffee shop — always the same, always at the same time)
- Predictable timing
- Visible assets (vehicle make/model/plate, equipment, valuables)
- Digital footprint that maps to physical location (social media geotagging, public calendar)

The easiest target is the predictable one. Vary routes, vary timing, vary patterns in any domain where prediction creates vulnerability.

## Medical Emergencies: MARCH Protocol

MARCH is the Tactical Combat Casualty Care (TCCC) framework for managing life threats in order of lethality.

### M — Massive Hemorrhage

The leading preventable cause of death in trauma is exsanguination (bleeding out). Address first.

**Tourniquet application:**
- High and tight: apply 2–3 inches above the wound, as high on the limb as possible for extremity wounds
- Tighten until bleeding stops — not just reduced, but stopped
- Note the time of application (critical for the receiving medical team)
- Do not remove in the field once applied — removal can restart bleeding and dislodge clots
- CAT (Combat Application Tourniquet) or SOFTT-W are the standard training tools; know how to improvise with a belt, cloth, and rigid stick as windlass

**The 3-30-300 rule for bleeding:**
- Uncontrolled arterial bleeding: 3 minutes to unconsciousness
- Standard blood loss reserve: 30% of blood volume (approximately 1.5L in an adult) before hemodynamic compromise
- Time to irreversible organ failure from uncontrolled hemorrhage: approximately 300 seconds (5 minutes) in severe cases

Hemostatic gauze (QuikClot, Celox) for junctional wounds that cannot be tourniqueted (neck, groin, armpit): pack the wound firmly, maintain direct pressure for minimum 3 minutes. This is not gentle — you need to pack the gauze into the wound and hold pressure hard.

### A — Airway

Check for airway patency. In an unconscious patient: head-tilt chin-lift (for non-trauma), jaw thrust (if spinal injury is possible). Recovery position (lateral recumbent) for unconscious breathing patients to prevent aspiration.

Airway obstruction signs: stridor (high-pitched inspiratory sound), paradoxical breathing (chest moves opposite to normal), accessory muscle use (neck muscles straining on each breath), cyanosis.

A simple nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) is a field-deployable device that takes 5 minutes to learn and can maintain a patent airway in an unconscious patient. Include one in a comprehensive first aid kit.

### R — Respiration

Assess breathing quality and rate. Normal: 12–20 breaths per minute. Signs of respiratory distress:
- Rate >30 or <8
- Asymmetric chest rise (tension pneumothorax — needle decompression is the field intervention)
- Sucking chest wound: seal with an occlusive dressing (commercial or improvised — plastic, tape three sides to create a flutter valve)

**Needle decompression for tension pneumothorax** (requires training — do not improvise without it): 14-gauge needle, second intercostal space, mid-clavicular line. This is a skills course, not a field improvisation.

### C — Circulation

After massive hemorrhage control: assess perfusion. Capillary refill >2 seconds, pallor, confusion, and rapid weak pulse indicate shock.

Shock management in the field: lay flat, elevate legs if no spinal or lower extremity injury (improves venous return), keep warm (cold impairs clotting), maintain airway, do not give anything by mouth. IV access in the field is a trained skill; the interventions available without training are positional and environmental.

**Junctional hemorrhage** (wounds in groin, axilla, neck): junctional tourniquets (SAM-JT, JETT) or wound packing with hemostatic gauze and sustained manual pressure.

### H — Hypothermia

The trauma triad of death: hypothermia + acidosis + coagulopathy. Hypothermia impairs coagulation directly — a hypothermic patient bleeds more. Even in warm environments, trauma patients lose heat rapidly.

Field protocol: wet clothing off, insulate from the ground first (ground draws heat fastest), wrap in any available insulation, cover the head. The goal is prevent further heat loss, not warm the patient rapidly. Rapid rewarming of a hypothermic trauma patient can precipitate cardiac arrhythmia.

## Navigation

### Dead Reckoning

Dead reckoning: estimating current position based on a known starting point, direction of travel, speed, and elapsed time. No GPS required.

Components:
- **Known start point** — wherever you are when GPS or map access is lost
- **Direction** — compass bearing or sun/star navigation
- **Speed** — average walking speed is approximately 3 mph (5 km/h) on flat terrain; slower on inclines, in crowds, in darkness
- **Time** — elapsed time from last known position

**Accuracy degradation:** dead reckoning errors compound over distance. A 5° bearing error produces 87 meters of lateral error per kilometer traveled. Correct against a known landmark as soon as one becomes available.

### Terrain Association

Terrain association: navigating by matching the shape of the landscape to the map (or map to landscape). More robust than compass-only navigation in complex terrain.

Key features to read: ridgelines, valleys, drainage patterns, road junctions, distinct elevation features. When lost: find the highest ground possible, identify two prominent landscape features, triangulate your position.

**Water navigation principle:** in natural terrain, follow water downhill. Water leads to larger water, which leads to human habitation or infrastructure in most developed regions.

### Urban Navigation Without GPS

When entering an unfamiliar city, memorize before you need it:
- **The central axis:** one major north-south and one major east-west street (most cities have these; they anchor all other navigation)
- **The river or coast:** if present, it's a navigation reference that cannot be missed
- **Three landmarks** visible from height or distance (towers, bridges, distinctive buildings)
- **The direction of the nearest train station, hospital, and police station** from your accommodation

**Urban dead reckoning:** in a grid city, count blocks and track turns. "3 blocks north, 2 east, 1 north" can be mentally tracked to 4 blocks of accuracy. In non-grid cities, use the central landmarks as references and triangulate.

**The sun as compass:** in the northern hemisphere, the sun rises roughly east, transits roughly south at midday, sets roughly west. In the southern hemisphere, it transits north at midday. At 6 AM, face the sunrise — you're facing approximately east. These are useful orientation approximations, not precision tools.

**When completely lost in an urban environment:**
1. Find high ground or an elevated position — orientation requires a larger field of view
2. Identify one landmark you recognize from your memorized list
3. Move toward it
4. Once at the landmark, navigate from the known point

## Communications Under Duress

### Information Hierarchy and Brevity

Under stress, communication degrades: vocabulary shrinks, sentences run together, critical information is buried in detail, emotional content overwhelms factual content.

Counter this with pre-structured formats that require filling slots, not composition under pressure.

**SALUTE reporting** (military brevity format for enemy/threat reporting):
- **S — Size:** how many individuals? What vehicles?
- **A — Activity:** what are they doing?
- **L — Location:** where are they? Grid coordinates, address, or relative to a known landmark
- **U — Unit:** what organization or affiliation, if known?
- **T — Time:** when was this observed?
- **E — Equipment:** what are they carrying? What vehicles? What weapons?

SALUTE works for emergency communication, crime reporting, and any situation where you need to convey threat information quickly and clearly to a dispatcher or responding party.

**SITREP (Situation Report) format:**
- Who you are
- Where you are
- What happened
- Current status and immediate need
- Any ongoing threat

Practice brevity: say less, more clearly. The dispatcher, responder, or commander needs facts and your location — not a narrative. Lead with location. Always.

### Signal Discipline

Signal discipline: transmitting only what is necessary, when it is necessary, using minimum power/visibility. Originally a military radio principle; applies broadly.

**In emergency communication:**
- Give your location first (if anything prevents completion of the call, they know where to send help)
- One message, complete, not a conversation that can be interrupted
- Say when you've finished: "over" (radio) or simply stop after a complete message

**In personal information security:** the less you broadcast about your location, schedule, and assets, the fewer vectors exist for targeting. This applies to social media, casual conversation with strangers, and any context where information could be exploited.

**Code words / brevity codes:** for close-contact groups (family, small team), pre-establish simple code signals for "I need help" or "something is wrong here but I can't say it." Can be a text message (an out-of-context word), a phone call left unanswered, or a prearranged phrase. The code is useless if it's only established in the crisis.

## Improvised Tools

The principle: **any object can be a tool if you understand its properties.** Mass, edge, reach, hardness, heat resistance, electrical conductivity. Constraints force creativity. Function is primary; form is secondary.

**Utility under constraint:**
- A belt: tourniquet, restraint, carry strap, anchor point, makeshift rope, weapon
- A pen: lever, probe, pressure tool, window breaker (glass-breaker end), improvised weapon (kubaton function)
- Paracord / shoelaces: tie, hang, measure distance, trip, drag, improvised sling, clothesline
- A phone: flashlight, mirror/reflective surface, timer, noise maker, map (offline), recording device, level
- A jacket: carry bag (knotted arms), rope (arms linked), padding, signaling flag, splint padding, improvised stretcher component
- A credit card: shim, spacer, mixing surface, fire-starting scraper for ferro rod

**The MacGyver principle:** don't look for the right tool. Identify the required function, then find what in the environment can perform that function. Function → material, not material → function.

**Emergency improvisation rules:**
1. Work with what's present, not what you wish was present
2. Simple solutions first. Elegant later
3. Test before committing weight, force, or safety to improvised gear
4. The improvised solution only needs to last until a real solution is available — not forever

## Movement

**Don't telegraph intent.** Look where you're going, not where you're about to turn. Consistent pace. No sudden stops or sharp angle changes without a natural reason. Hesitation is visible information.

**Blend to the environment.** Move at the speed the environment dictates. In a crowded transit hub, move with the crowd. On a quiet street, match ambient pace. Deviation creates attention. When you need to move faster without flagging urgency, increase stride length before increasing pace.

**The threshold pause.** Before entering any new space, pause at the threshold for 2–3 seconds and scan. You're not stalling — you're collecting data that will be unavailable once you're inside and oriented differently.

**Hands free baseline.** Both hands available is the default readiness posture. Bags on the back, not in hand. This is both practical (grip available) and psychological (posture signals readiness).

**Cover vs. concealment:**
- **Concealment** hides you (a bush, a curtain, a dark corner)
- **Cover** stops projectiles (concrete, stone, engine block, earth berms, steel)
Concealment buys time — you can move without being seen. Cover saves lives. Most things that look solid provide only concealment. Know the difference. Interior walls are concealment, not cover. Vehicle doors are concealment. Engine blocks are cover.

**Movement between cover:** expose yourself for the minimum necessary time and distance between covered positions. Move low, move fast, stop behind cover before re-evaluating. Never remain stationary in concealment thinking it is cover.

**The 10-meter scan before entering any building:** locate exits, identify the person nearest each exit, note anything that doesn't fit the environment's baseline. This takes 15 seconds and is not paranoia — it's pattern recognition conducted proactively.

## Post-Incident

### The After-Action Review (AAR)

The AAR is a US Army-developed structured review used by elite military, emergency response, and high-performance organizations to convert experience into learning. The structure:

**1. What was the intent?**
What were you trying to accomplish? This is the standard against which performance is measured. Without a clear intent, there is nothing to evaluate — only narrative.

**2. What actually happened?**
Factual, sequential, no interpretation yet. Force specificity: "I moved to the left wall" not "I reacted." "He said X" not "he was hostile." The factual description is the foundation. Interpretation that sneaks into this step corrupts the analysis.

**3. What went well, and why?**
This step is not celebration — it's pattern extraction. If something worked, why did it work? Was it skill, preparation, positioning, decision quality, or luck? Only documented skill and preparation repeat reliably. Luck does not.

**4. What could have been better, and why?**
Same analysis, different direction. What decision or action produced a worse outcome than intended? Trace it back to the root cause: was it a skill deficit, an information deficit, a decision error, a planning failure, or an environmental factor outside your control? Accurate diagnosis is the only path to relevant improvement.

**5. What will you do differently next time?**
Actionable and specific. Not "be more aware" (unmeasurable) but "I will conduct a baseline scan before entering the next location" (observable and executable). Each lesson should produce a specific behavior change.

**AAR conduct rules:** no blame, no rank (in a team setting), no self-promotion. Truth-seeking only. The enemy of the AAR is social dynamics that make people protect their reputation at the expense of honest analysis.

### Managing Adrenaline Dump Recovery

The adrenaline dump (sympathetic nervous system activation) following a high-stress incident produces a predictable sequence: peak activation → action → comedown. The comedown is characterized by:

- Shaking (fine motor tremor as the muscles deplete stored glycogen)
- Nausea
- Emotional release (crying, laughter, anger — often disproportionate to the moment)
- Cognitive fog and difficulty sequencing thoughts
- Time distortion (incidents feel longer or shorter than they were)
- Memory fragmentation (the specific sequence of events may be unclear for hours)

**Tactical recovery protocol:**
1. Move to safety first — the recovery cannot happen in the hot zone
2. Tactical breathing to initiate parasympathetic response: 4-count in, 4-hold, 4-out, 4-hold. Four cycles minimum.
3. Sit or lie down if possible — prevents injury from the involuntary shaking
4. Eat something with simple carbohydrates if available — the shaking is partly glycogen depletion
5. Do not make significant decisions for 15–30 minutes if avoidable — cognition is degraded and emotional regulation is temporarily compromised

**Memory and documentation:** incident memory consolidates over the first 24–48 hours. Adrenaline impairs accurate encoding of sequence and detail. The window for accurate documentation is the first 2 hours; after that, memory begins to reconstruct rather than recall. Write a factual account as soon as you are safe and cognitively capable. Write facts, not interpretations. Date and time it.

### What to Document and When

If an incident involves any potential legal consequence — violence, crime, accident, injury to others — document immediately and thoroughly:

**What to capture:**
- Time, date, location (precise address or GPS coordinates)
- Sequence of events in chronological order with timestamps where known
- Who was present and what each person said or did, as precisely as possible
- Your own actions and the reason for each decision
- Physical evidence: photographs of scene, injuries, damage
- Witness names and contact information
- Any verbal threats, statements, or claims made by other parties
- Your physical and mental state (were you injured, were you in fear, what was your threat perception)

**Documentation format:** contemporaneous notes (written at the time) carry more legal weight than later reconstructions. Secure the document — email it to yourself, send it to counsel if applicable. Once it exists in a datable form, it is harder to challenge.

**What not to document:** speculation about others' motives, characterizations of others' mental states, your own guilt or uncertainty. Facts only. Interpretation happens later, in appropriate context, with counsel if necessary.

**Contact protocol after significant incidents:**
- Notify relevant parties (family, employer if relevant) on a need-to-know basis only
- Retain counsel before speaking to law enforcement in anything beyond an emergency reporting context — not because of guilt but because adrenaline-state statements can be inaccurate and legally consequential
- Follow up with your own physician for physical assessment within 24 hours, even if you feel fine — some injuries (concussion, internal) are not immediately apparent
