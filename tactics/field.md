# Field Tactics

Situational awareness, environmental intelligence, improvised problem-solving, performance under pressure.

---

## Situational Awareness

**Cooper's Color Code** — your mental readiness state:
- **White:** Unaware, relaxed, unprepared. Avoid this in unfamiliar environments.
- **Yellow:** Relaxed alertness. Aware of your surroundings without fixating on any one thing. Default state in public.
- **Orange:** Specific alert. You've identified something that needs attention. Focus narrows.
- **Red:** Action state. You're responding. Decisions made in white and yellow determine how well you perform in red.

Most people live in white. Operate in yellow.

**The OODA Loop** (Boyd): Observe → Orient → Decide → Act. Whoever completes this loop faster wins. Speed comes from pre-built mental models (orient faster) and remaining calm under load (decide faster). Train both.

**Baseline scanning:** Any environment has a baseline — the normal level of movement, noise, and activity. Deviations from the baseline are where threats and opportunities both emerge. Notice what's out of place.

## Positioning

- Always locate the exits before you need them. Two is one, one is none.
- In any room: back to the wall, eyes on the door. Habit.
- Never stand in a doorway or chokepoint longer than necessary.
- In public: avoid being the most conspicuous person in the space. Blend to the local baseline.
- High ground is always preferable. Literal and metaphorical.
- Create distance from potential threats before they materialize. Pre-positioning beats reactive positioning every time.

## Improvised Tools

The principle: **any object can be a tool if you understand its properties.** Mass, edge, reach, hardness, heat resistance, electrical conductivity. Constraints force creativity.

**Utility under constraint:**
- A belt: tourniquet, restraint, handle, strap, anchor
- A pen: lever, probe, marking tool, pressure point
- Paracord / shoelaces: tie, hang, measure, trip, drag
- A phone: flashlight, mirror (reflective surface), timer, noise maker, map
- A jacket: carry bag, rope (arms tied), padding, signal flag, heat retention

**The MacGyver principle:** Don't look for the right tool. Identify the required function, then find what in the environment can perform it. Function → material, not material → function.

**Emergency improvisation rules:**
1. Work with what's present, not what you wish was present.
2. Simple solutions first. Elegant later.
3. Test before committing weight, force, or safety to improvised gear.

## Movement

**Don't telegraph your intent.** Look where you're going, not where you're turning. Consistent pace. No sudden stops or sharp angle changes without a reason.

**Blend to the environment.** Move at the speed the environment dictates. In a crowded station, move with the crowd. In a quiet street, match the ambient pace. Deviation creates attention.

**The 10-meter scan.** Before entering any new space, pause at the threshold for 2–3 seconds and scan. You're not stalling — you're collecting data that will be unavailable once you're inside.

**Keep your hands free when possible.** Both hands available is a baseline readiness posture. Bags on the back, not in the hand.

**The cover vs. concealment distinction:**
- **Concealment** hides you (a bush, a curtain).
- **Cover** stops projectiles (concrete, engine block).
Concealment buys time. Cover saves lives. Know the difference before you need it.

## Performance Under Pressure

**Box breathing:** 4 counts in → 4 hold → 4 out → 4 hold. Repeat 4 cycles. Activates the parasympathetic nervous system, lowers heart rate, restores cognitive function. Works under extreme stress. Drill it so it's automatic.

**Cognitive narrowing:** Under high stress, peripheral attention collapses. You only see what's directly in front of you. Counter it: deliberately look peripherally, rotate your gaze, name what you see. This forces cortical engagement over pure amygdala response.

**The one-thing rule under load:** When overwhelmed, identify the single most important action in the next 30 seconds. Do only that. Expand the time horizon once the immediate is handled.

**After-action habit:** After any stressful or high-stakes event: what happened, what went well, what would you do differently. Brief, honest, no blame. This is how experience becomes skill.
