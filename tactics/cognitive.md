# Cognitive Tactics

How to think better, focus harder, and perform under pressure. The mind is trainable hardware. Upgrade it deliberately.

---

## Attention Architecture

### Spotlight vs. Lantern Attention

Psychologist Alison Gopnik's distinction: **spotlight attention** is narrow, focused, directed — the mode of expertise and task execution. **Lantern attention** is diffuse, open, ambient — the mode of children, creative insight, and environmental scanning.

Most adults default to spotlight almost exclusively and treat lantern as distraction. This is wrong. Both modes are essential and serve different functions:

- **Spotlight:** analytical work, writing, debugging, learning structured material, executing known procedures
- **Lantern:** creative problem-solving, finding unexpected connections, sensing what's off in a situation, pattern recognition at the edges of your knowledge

**The failure mode:** spending 8 hours in spotlight mode and calling the result "deep work" when you've actually been in shallow-focus task-switching the whole time.

**Deliberate mode-switching protocol:** Schedule lantern time explicitly — walks without headphones, shower thinking, sitting with a problem without engaging with it directly. The brain doesn't create connections on demand; it creates them when you step back from demand. See also: [flow-states.md](flow-states.md) and the incubation mechanism below.

### Controlled vs. Automatic Attention

Controlled (top-down) attention is effortful, directed by intention, metabolically expensive. Automatic (bottom-up) attention is triggered by stimuli — motion, novelty, loud sounds, faces, food — and is nearly irresistible.

**The attention design problem:** modern digital environments are engineered to hijack automatic attention constantly. Every notification, autoplay video, and algorithmic feed exploits bottom-up mechanisms. You cannot out-willpower this with top-down control — the systems targeting your automatic attention are better at it than your prefrontal cortex is at resisting it.

**The only reliable solution:** environmental design. Remove the stimuli. Not "I'll check my phone less" — phone in another room, notifications off, app deleted. The willpower solution will fail. The environment solution works without willpower.

**Attention residue** (Sophie Leroy): when you switch tasks, attention doesn't fully transfer to the new task. A residue of attention remains on the previous task, degrading performance on the current one. Every context-switch has a cognitive tax. The more context-switches in a day, the more residue, the lower the ceiling on any given task. Batch your work accordingly.

## Working Memory Management

### Miller's Law and Its Limits

George Miller's 1956 paper: working memory holds 7 ± 2 items simultaneously. Later revised by Nelson Cowan to approximately 4 items for distinct chunks. The practical point: your working memory is a very small whiteboard, and once it's full, new information forces old information off.

**Implications for your work setup:**
- Complex problems that exceed 4–7 variables should be externalized — written, diagrammed, or mapped — not held in your head. Your working memory is not a reliable scratch pad for multivariable analysis.
- Interruptions are expensive precisely because they wipe the working memory whiteboard. A 30-second interruption during deep work can cost 15–20 minutes of re-entry time.
- When you're stuck: don't try harder to hold it in your head. Write it out. Draw it. The act of externalization often produces the insight.

### Chunking

Chunking is the cognitive process of grouping items into meaningful units, effectively compressing information so more can fit in working memory. A chess grandmaster doesn't see 32 pieces — they see 6–8 meaningful patterns. A programmer doesn't see 40 tokens of syntax — they see a loop with a conditional.

**Building chunks:** chunks are built through deliberate practice with feedback, not passive exposure. You have to use the pattern under pressure, get feedback on your execution, and correct errors repeatedly. This is how expertise compresses complexity into manageable working memory loads.

**How to accelerate chunking:** deliberate practice (K. Anders Ericsson) — focused repetition at the edge of current ability with immediate feedback. Passive reading doesn't build chunks. Testing yourself, applying the material, getting corrective feedback — that builds chunks.

### Cognitive Load Theory Applied

Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory: all mental work imposes load on working memory. Three types:

**Intrinsic load:** the inherent complexity of the material. Fixed by the task — you can't reduce it without reducing what you're learning.

**Extraneous load:** cognitive burden from poor presentation, unnecessary complexity in the environment, confusing interfaces, or unclear instructions. This is waste. Eliminate it from your work setup: clear workspace, clean interface, good tools, organized notes. Extraneous load burns working memory on overhead instead of on the work.

**Germane load:** mental effort directed at building schemas (chunks). This is the useful load. Create conditions where germane load can dominate: reduce extraneous load first, then engage with intrinsic load fully.

**Practical application:** when a complex task feels overwhelming, ask whether the overwhelm is intrinsic (real complexity of the problem) or extraneous (environment, unclear goal, missing information, wrong tool). Fix the extraneous first.

## Deep Focus Protocols

### Newport's Four Depth Modes

Cal Newport (*Deep Work*) identifies four ways to schedule deep work:

**Monastic:** eliminate all shallow work, exist in near-total deep work mode. Realistic only for academic researchers or unusually structured independent workers. Not applicable for most.

**Bimodal:** divide time into deep and shallow periods — weeks of deep work alternating with weeks of availability. Works for some knowledge workers who can batch obligations.

**Rhythmic:** the most applicable for most people. Same time every day for deep work. 6:00–10:00 AM before the world activates. The routine eliminates the decision of when to go deep — it's automatic. Defend this block as inviolable.

**Journalistic:** deep work whenever you can steal time. Requires high capacity to shift into depth quickly. Hard to do if you lack practice, but trainable.

**The selection:** unless your schedule is genuinely chaotic, rhythmic is the move. Same block, same space, same ritual, every day. Four hours of uninterrupted deep work daily compounds faster than any other productivity improvement.

### The 90-Minute Ultradian Rhythm

Peretz Lavie and Nathaniel Kleitman: the brain operates on approximately 90-minute cycles of higher and lower neural arousal across the day (the Basic Rest-Activity Cycle, BRAC). These cycles govern sleep stages at night and, to a lesser extent, focus quality and alertness during the day.

**Practical application:** structure deep work in 90-minute blocks followed by 15–20 minutes of genuine recovery (not scrolling — movement, rest, eyes unfocused). Don't power through the recovery window trying to sustain intensity. The trough is not failure — it's the biological rhythm. Work with it.

**Two strong 90-minute blocks > one degraded 3-hour grind.**

### Entering Flow Deliberately

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's flow conditions: challenge slightly exceeds skill, clear goals and immediate feedback, full absorption. Flow is not a gift — it's an architecture problem. See [flow-states.md](flow-states.md) for the full protocol.

Critical preconditions:
1. **Eliminate interruption vectors** before starting — not during. Once you're in, any disruption restarts the entry process.
2. **Clear a defined, achievable goal for the session.** Vague goals produce wandering, not flow.
3. **Increase difficulty until it matches your current edge.** Flow doesn't happen on tasks that are too easy (boredom) or too hard (anxiety). Find the Goldilocks edge.
4. **The first 10–15 minutes are always rough.** Resistance at the start is not a signal to stop — it's the price of entry. Push through the first friction wall.

## Stress and Performance

### The Yerkes-Dodson Curve in Detail

Yerkes and Dodson (1908): there is an inverted-U relationship between arousal (stress/activation) and performance. Too little arousal = boredom, low engagement, poor performance. Too much = panic, cognitive narrowing, poor performance. Peak performance occurs at an optimal arousal level in the middle.

**Critical nuance:** the optimal arousal level varies by task type.
- **Complex cognitive tasks** (analysis, writing, strategy): optimal arousal is relatively low. High stress degrades performance on these tasks by consuming working memory and narrowing attention.
- **Simple physical tasks** (repeated manual labor, sprinting): optimal arousal is relatively high. Activation improves performance.
- **Moderately complex skill execution** (sport, surgery, presenting): intermediate arousal is optimal.

**Operational implication:** manage your arousal level to match the task. Before writing or strategic analysis: calm down, reduce stimulation. Before a presentation or physical performance: activate, prime. Wrong arousal level for the task type is a performance tax.

### Eustress vs. Distress: The Physiological Difference

Both eustress (productive stress) and distress (degrading stress) involve cortisol and adrenaline. The difference is in appraisal — how you interpret the arousal.

**The challenge state** (eustress):
- Cardiac output increases, vascular resistance decreases — more blood flow, better oxygenation
- DHEA (dehydroepiandrosterone) rises — a buffer hormone that improves resilience
- Focus sharpens, peripheral attention maintained
- Cognitively: *"I can handle this. My resources meet the demands."*

**The threat state** (distress):
- Cardiac output increases, vascular resistance also increases — the cardiovascular response to a threat (preparing for damage)
- Cortisol dominates — helpful short-term, toxic long-term
- Attention tunnel-narrows, working memory contracts
- Cognitively: *"This is going to hurt. The demands exceed my resources."*

**The reframe protocol (Jamieson et al., 2012):** telling yourself that your arousal is helpful — "My body is activating because this matters. That activation is preparing me to perform." — measurably shifts physiology from threat to challenge state. This is not positive thinking; it's a biologically validated intervention. Use it before high-stakes events.

### Deliberate Stress Inoculation

Stress inoculation training (Donald Meichenbaum): graduated, controlled exposure to stressors builds tolerance and response capacity. You cannot perform well under pressure you've never encountered in training.

**The exposure hierarchy:**
1. Conceptual rehearsal — mentally simulate the scenario in detail
2. Simulated stress — practice under conditions that approximate the real stressor (time pressure, observers, raised stakes)
3. Real exposure — controlled version of the actual stressor
4. Debrief each level before escalating

**Voluntary discomfort as baseline practice:** cold exposure, extended fasting, difficult conversations, physical training at or beyond your comfort threshold. These are not primarily physical disciplines — they train the relationship between discomfort and response. The discipline of continuing under discomfort generalizes across domains.

## Cognitive Biases as Operational Threats

Biases are not failures of intelligence — they're features of cognition operating in contexts they weren't optimized for. They cannot be eliminated; they can be detected and corrected.

### Anchoring

First information encountered disproportionately influences subsequent judgment, even when irrelevant. Tversky and Kahneman's wheel-of-fortune experiment: a random number (10 vs. 65) influenced estimates of the percentage of African nations in the UN.

**Operational threat:** in planning, the first estimate sets the range. In negotiation, the first number sets the anchor. In analysis, the initial hypothesis shapes what evidence you seek.

**Debiasing protocol:** before committing to an estimate or decision, explicitly generate at least one alternative anchor from the opposite direction. "What if the real answer is much higher? Much lower?" Then reconsider.

### Availability Heuristic

Probability judgments are distorted toward things that are easily called to mind — vivid, recent, emotionally charged, or widely publicized events feel more common than they are.

**Operational threat:** risk assessment calibrated to memorable failures (plane crashes) over statistically more significant ones (car accidents). Recent failures loom too large in planning. Dramatic scenarios displace realistic ones.

**Debiasing protocol:** base rate lookup. Before assessing probability, ask "What is the actual historical frequency of this?" Seek data, not memories.

### Confirmation Bias

The tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information that confirms existing beliefs while underweighting or ignoring contradictory evidence. Affects not just what you notice but how you interpret ambiguous data.

**Operational threat:** in planning, you design for your expected scenario and fail to stress-test it. In analysis, you stop searching when you find supporting evidence. In evaluating people, early impressions resist updating.

**Debiasing protocol:**
1. **Actively seek disconfirming evidence.** Ask: "What would have to be true for me to be wrong about this?"
2. **The steel man:** articulate the strongest version of the opposing view before deciding. Not the straw man — the steel man. If you can't articulate it, you don't understand the decision space yet.
3. **Pre-mortem:** assume the decision was a failure. Work backward. What went wrong? This forces you to inhabit the disconfirming scenario rather than rationalize it away.

### Sunk Cost Fallacy

Continuing a course of action because of already-invested resources (time, money, effort, identity) rather than on the basis of future expected value. The past investment is unrecoverable regardless of future decisions — it's economically irrelevant.

**Operational threat:** persisting in careers, relationships, projects, or beliefs because of how much has already been invested rather than what the future actually holds. "I've been doing this for 10 years" is not a reason to continue.

**Debiasing protocol:** evaluate decisions forward only. "If I were starting from zero today, would I choose this path?" If no, the sunk cost is distorting the judgment.

### Building Explicit Debiasing Habits

- **Decision journal:** record decisions, the reasoning behind them, and your confidence level. Review outcomes. The gap between reasoning quality and outcome is your calibration gap.
- **Reference class forecasting:** before estimating, identify the reference class (similar projects, similar decisions) and use their historical distribution as the prior. Then adjust for specifics.
- **The outside view:** ask what an outside observer who knew only the category of your decision (not your specific details) would predict. Often more accurate than the inside view.

## Mental Simulation

### Mental Rehearsal: The Olympic Model

Mental rehearsal is the cognitive simulation of a performance or scenario. Neuroscience basis: imagined actions activate similar neural pathways as executed actions — the brain doesn't perfectly distinguish vivid simulation from reality at the motor planning level. Used systematically by Olympic athletes, Special Forces operators, surgeons, and elite musicians.

**Process rehearsal vs. outcome rehearsal:**
- **Outcome rehearsal** (imagining crossing the finish line, making the sale, giving the perfect speech) feels good but has weak performance effects
- **Process rehearsal** (imagining the sequence of actions, the sensory detail of each step, including obstacles and recovery) has robust performance effects

**The protocol:** simulate in first-person, present tense, full sensory detail. Include not just the ideal scenario but the failure points — the moment the presentation technology fails, the moment you feel anxiety, the moment the counterpart pushes back — and rehearse your recovery response. The rehearsal is most valuable at the difficult moments, not the easy ones.

### Implementation Intentions

Peter Gollwitzer's research: vague intentions ("I will work on this") have low execution rates. **Implementation intentions** — "If X, then I will Y" — have dramatically higher execution rates because they pre-load the decision into automatic processing.

**Format:** "When [specific situation], I will [specific action]."

Examples:
- "When I sit down at my desk at 6:00 AM, I will open the document and write 200 words before checking any communication."
- "When I feel the urge to check my phone during deep work, I will write down what I was thinking about and return to the task."
- "When someone surprises me with a difficult request, I will pause 3 seconds before responding."

The if-then structure converts a desired behavior into a near-automatic response to a specific cue. The cue does the work instead of willpower.

## Emotional Regulation as Cognitive Tactic

Emotional states are not separate from cognitive performance — they are determinants of it. Regulation is not suppression. It's the deliberate modulation of emotional state in service of performance.

### The STOP Technique

A mindfulness-based interrupt for reactive situations:
- **S — Stop.** Pause the automatic response.
- **T — Take a breath.** Physiological regulation. Slow exhale specifically activates the vagus nerve and dampens sympathetic activation.
- **O — Observe.** What am I actually feeling? What's happening in my body? What am I thinking?
- **P — Proceed.** Continue with choice, not reaction.

The gap between stimulus and response is where character is expressed. STOP creates that gap when it would otherwise not exist.

### Emotional Granularity

Lisa Feldman Barrett's research: people with greater emotional granularity — the ability to precisely distinguish between similar emotional states — have better emotional regulation, better health outcomes, and better performance under stress.

"I'm stressed" is a blunt instrument. "I'm feeling anxious about an uncertain outcome that I care about but can't control" is a scalpel. The precise label activates prefrontal cortex regulation rather than simple limbic reactivity.

**Practice:** when you notice an emotional activation, don't stop at the first label. Ask: Is this anxiety or excitement? Is this frustration or disappointment? Is this anger or shame? The differentiation creates distance and regulatory capacity.

### Cognitive Defusion (ACT)

Steven Hayes's Acceptance and Commitment Therapy framework: we fuse with our thoughts, treating them as literal reality rather than as mental events passing through awareness. Defusion is the process of stepping back and observing thoughts rather than being them.

**Defusion techniques:**
- **Labeling the thought:** "I'm having the thought that I'm going to fail." This inserts a tiny gap between you and the content.
- **The news ticker:** imagine your anxious thoughts running as a ticker at the bottom of your vision. You're watching them, not living them.
- **Thank the mind:** "Thank you, mind, for the warning. I'll take it under advisement." Acknowledge without merging.

This is not positive thinking — you're not arguing that the thought is wrong. You're changing your relationship to it. The thought loses some of its behavioral force.

### Surfing Urges

For impulse control (checking phone, breaking from deep work, reactive messaging): the urge is a wave. It will peak and decline whether you act on it or not. Most urges peak within 20–90 seconds.

**Protocol:** when the urge arises, note it. Don't fight it, don't comply with it. Observe it as a physical sensation. Where is it in your body? What does it feel like? Curiosity about the urge is incompatible with being swept away by it. Within 60–90 seconds, it will diminish without being acted upon. This is trainable and compounds over time.

## Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

### Probabilistic Thinking

Most people think in categories (will happen / won't happen) when they should think in probabilities. The question is never "will X happen?" — it's "how likely is X, and what's the expected value of acting as if it will?"

**Convert to numbers.** "I think this is likely" → "I'd put this at 65%." The act of quantifying forces you to actually assess the uncertainty rather than handwave at it. Even rough numbers are better than vague qualifiers.

**The reference class again:** before assigning a probability, identify the reference class (similar situations, historical base rate) and start there. Adjust from the base rate with specific evidence. Don't start from your intuition and work outward — start from the base rate and adjust inward.

### Bayesian Updating in Real Time

Bayes' theorem formally: P(H|E) = P(E|H) × P(H) / P(E). In practice: your confidence in a hypothesis should update proportionally to the strength of new evidence, not dramatically with any single piece.

**Common violations:**
- Updating too much on a single dramatic piece of evidence (availability bias)
- Failing to update when evidence should lower confidence (confirmation bias)
- Updating to 100% or 0% (certainty) before the evidence warrants it

**The Bayesian habit:** maintain explicit probability estimates on important beliefs. When new evidence arrives, ask: "How likely would I have seen this evidence if my hypothesis is true? If it's false?" Update your estimate proportionally. Beliefs held loosely, updated with evidence, calibrated against outcomes — this is the operating system of good judgment.

### Pre-Decision Checklist

Before committing to a significant decision:
1. Have I explicitly sought disconfirming evidence?
2. What is the base rate for this type of decision?
3. What biases are most likely to be distorting my judgment here? (Anchoring, availability, confirmation, sunk cost)
4. Have I articulated the strongest case against my current preference?
5. What is my confidence level, numerically? Is it justified?
6. If I imagine looking back on this decision in 10 years, what do I wish I had thought about?

## Cognitive Recovery

### The Default Mode Network as Processing Engine

The brain's default mode network (DMN) — medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate, angular gyrus — activates during rest, mind-wandering, and self-referential thought. It was initially called the "default" mode because researchers thought it was the brain doing nothing. They were wrong.

The DMN is the brain's integration engine. It processes, consolidates, and connects information across domains. The insights that come in the shower, on a walk, or in the middle of the night are not random — they're the product of DMN processing that had been building while your conscious attention was elsewhere.

**The incubation mechanism** (Graham Wallas's four-stage model of creativity):
1. Preparation — active engagement with the problem
2. Incubation — conscious attention withdrawn; DMN processes subconsciously
3. Illumination — insight arrives, often suddenly
4. Verification — conscious testing of the insight

Incubation requires you to deliberately step away from the problem. Not to avoid it — to allow a different cognitive system to work on it. This is why the best ideas often come after you've "given up" for the day.

### Why Boredom Is Necessary

Constant stimulation suppresses DMN activity. When the attentional spotlight is always occupied — by podcasts, scrolling, entertainment, distraction — the DMN never gets uninterrupted processing time. The result: less insight, less creative connection, less meaningful rest.

**Boredom is not a problem to be solved. It is a cognitive resource to be cultivated.**

Structural boredom time: walks without audio, commutes without phones, waiting without distraction. These are not wastes — they are the substrate of integration and creative insight.

### Sleep as Cognitive Infrastructure

Matthew Walker (*Why We Sleep*): sleep is not rest — it's an active, essential cognitive process.

- **NREM deep sleep:** memory consolidation, synaptic pruning (removing weak connections), physical restoration
- **REM sleep:** emotional processing, creative connection-making, skill consolidation, emotional memory integration
- Missing REM specifically (alcohol, inconsistent sleep timing, shortened sleep) degrades emotional regulation and creative capacity more than most people recognize

**Performance half-life of sleep deprivation:** one night of 6 hours sleep is not acutely dangerous. After two weeks of 6-hour nights, performance is as degraded as after 24 hours of total sleep deprivation — and subjective perception doesn't track this. You feel more fine than you are.

**Sleep as the non-negotiable:** no cognitive optimization that occurs during waking hours compensates for degraded sleep. It is the infrastructure. Everything else is built on it.

### Active Recovery Techniques

- **NSDR (Non-Sleep Deep Rest) / Yoga Nidra:** 20-minute protocol of body scan and directed attention. Measurably restores dopamine levels in the striatum and improves afternoon cognitive performance. Use at lunch.
- **Physical movement:** walking specifically increases BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), improves mood and cognitive flexibility. Not the gym — low-intensity movement. The afternoon walk is not laziness; it is neurologically productive.
- **Structured downtime:** plan your rest the same way you plan your work. A recovery block with a defined start and end is psychologically and physiologically more restorative than passive half-attention to entertainment.
