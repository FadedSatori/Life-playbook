# Execution Systems

Most failure is not a failure of ambition. It is a failure of machinery. You knew what mattered, you meant to do it, and then the week happened. Intentions decay. Memory drops commitments. Urgent noise beats important signal every day it is allowed to compete.

An execution system is the machinery that turns intent into finished work without depending on mood, memory, or willpower. It has a few parts: a way to set direction, a way to capture and clarify every commitment, a way to limit what is in motion, a definition of done, a cadence of review, and a scoreboard.

This file is the machinery. [career/work-principles.md](career/work-principles.md) covers how to do the work itself (deep work, time blocking, the shutdown ritual). [time.md](time.md) covers what deserves your time at all. Build all three.

## Why Intentions Fail

Psychologists call it the **intention-behavior gap** (Paschal Sheeran's reviews are the standard reference): across many studies, a large share of people who form a sincere intention never act on it. The gap is not character. It is structure. As James Clear put it in *Atomic Habits*: "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems."

**The five failure points:**
1. **Storage.** Commitments held in your head get dropped, or they nag at the wrong moments. The head is a poor filing cabinet.
2. **Ambiguity.** "Sort out the taxes" is not doable. It is a cloud. Clouds don't get done; they get avoided.
3. **Overload.** Too many things in motion at once. Everything progresses slowly and nothing finishes.
4. **No feedback.** You don't know if you are on track until it is too late to correct.
5. **No review.** Without a regular look at the whole, the system goes stale and you stop trusting it.

**Implementation intentions** close part of the gap. Peter Gollwitzer's research on if-then plans ("When it is 7am on Monday, I will open the draft and write for 60 minutes") shows that deciding when, where, and how in advance raises follow-through. A 2006 meta-analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran, pooling 94 tests, found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment. It is the cheapest upgrade in this file.

## The Stack: From Direction to Action

Every working system has layers. Each answers a different question. Skip one and the layers above it float free.

| Layer | Question | Horizon | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose and principles | Why? | Life | [purpose.md](purpose.md), [principles.md](principles.md) |
| Vision | What does winning look like? | 3–5 years | [routines/annual-review.md](routines/annual-review.md) |
| Objectives | What matters most now? | Quarter to year | OKRs (below) |
| Projects | What outcomes am I committed to? | Weeks to months | Projects list |
| Next actions | What is the very next physical step? | Today | Action lists |
| Calendar | What must happen at a specific time? | Today | Calendar |

This mirrors David Allen's "horizons of focus" in *Getting Things Done*. Most people live entirely in the bottom two layers and wonder why they are busy but not going anywhere. A few live entirely in the top two and wonder why nothing changes. Execution is the discipline of connecting them.

## Setting Direction: OKRs

**Objectives and Key Results** came out of Intel, where Andy Grove adapted Peter Drucker's management by objectives and described the method in *High Output Management* (1983). John Doerr learned it from Grove at Intel, brought it to Google in 1999, and popularized it in *Measure What Matters* (2018).

**The structure:**
- **Objective:** qualitative, inspiring, directional. What you want.
- **Key results:** two to four measurable outcomes that prove you got there. How you'll know.
- Doerr's formula: *I will [objective] as measured by [key results].*

**Rules that keep OKRs honest:**
1. **Few.** Three objectives per quarter at most. Five objectives is a wish list.
2. **Key results are outcomes, not tasks.** "Publish 8 essays" is an output. "Grow the list from 400 to 1,000 readers" is an outcome. "Write more" is neither.
3. **Committed vs. aspirational.** Google distinguishes committed OKRs (expected to be fully met) from aspirational ones (stretch goals). For aspirational OKRs, landing around 60–70% is treated as success. Hitting 100% every time means you aimed too low.
4. **Score and review on a schedule.** An OKR set in January and opened again in December is a New Year's resolution with extra steps.

**A personal example.** *Objective:* become physically formidable again. *Key results:* deadlift 1.5x bodyweight by June 30; resting heart rate under 60 (monthly average); 40 of 48 planned training sessions completed this quarter. OKRs set direction. They don't tell you what to do on Tuesday. That is the job of the next layer.

## Capture and Clarify: Getting Things Done

David Allen's *Getting Things Done* (2001, revised 2015) is the most complete method for handling the flood of inputs at ground level. Its core claim: your mind is for having ideas, not holding them. Get every commitment out of your head and into a system you trust, and attention frees up for the work in front of you. (The research on why unfinished tasks nag is more modest than the folklore. See the note on plan-making in [career/work-principles.md](career/work-principles.md).)

**The five steps:**
1. **Capture.** Everything that has your attention goes into a small number of inboxes: a notebook, a notes app, a physical tray. Not your head.
2. **Clarify.** For each item: Is it actionable? If not, trash it, file it as reference, or park it on Someday/Maybe. If yes, what is the next action? If it takes under two minutes, do it now (Allen's two-minute rule).
3. **Organize.** Put clarified items where they belong: next actions (grouped by context, such as calls, computer, errands), the Projects list, Waiting For, the calendar, reference.
4. **Reflect.** Review the lists often enough to trust them. The weekly review is the linchpin; without it the system rots.
5. **Engage.** Choose what to do based on context, time available, energy, and priority. Then do it.

**Two definitions that change everything:**
- **Project:** any outcome that takes more than one action step and can be finished within about a year. "Renew passport" is a project. So is "Hire a contractor for the deck." Most people have 30 to 100 of them and track none.
- **Next action:** the next physical, visible step. Not "plan the party." "Text Sam to ask for the guest list." If you can't picture yourself doing it, it isn't a next action yet.

**The trusted system.** The test is simple: when something new arrives, do you capture it without thinking? When you sit down to work, do you look at your lists instead of your inbox? If not, the system isn't trusted, and you will fall back to memory and panic.

## Translating Goals Into Action

The weak joint in most systems is between the objective and the action list. Use this protocol for every new objective or project.

1. **State the outcome as finished.** "Garage converted into a usable gym, with flooring, rack, and lighting," not "Work on garage."
2. **Name the purpose.** One sentence on why. It decides trade-offs later.
3. **Brainstorm the pieces.** Dump every step, question, and dependency without ordering. Allen calls this sequence (purpose, outcome, brainstorm, organize, next actions) the natural planning model.
4. **Organize.** Sequence the pieces. Mark dependencies. Identify milestones.
5. **Define the very next action** for each part that can move now. Put each on the right list.
6. **Schedule the hard parts.** The work that requires depth gets a calendar block, not a list entry. See [career/work-principles.md](career/work-principles.md).
7. **Write the if-then.** "If it is Saturday at 9am, I am in the garage measuring the floor."

## The Planning Fallacy and Buffers

Kahneman and Tversky named the **planning fallacy**: people underestimate how long their own tasks will take, even when they know similar tasks ran late before. In a classic study by Buehler, Griffin, and Ross (1994), students predicted they would finish their theses in about 34 days on average. They took about 56. Only about 30% finished by their own predicted date.

Kahneman tells a story on himself in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. His team estimated about two years to write a curriculum textbook. When asked, a colleague with experience of similar teams admitted that roughly 40% never finished and the rest took seven to ten years. The team pressed on anyway. It took eight years.

At scale it gets worse. Bent Flyvbjerg's database of large projects, summarized in *How Big Things Get Done* (2023, with Dan Gardner), found that only about 8.5% came in on both budget and schedule. Hence Hofstadter's Law: "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law."

**The buffer protocol:**
1. **Use the outside view first.** How long did similar projects take, for you or anyone? Start from that base rate, then adjust. See [mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md](mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md).
2. **Know your personal ratio.** Log estimates and actuals for a month. If your work runs 1.5x your estimates, multiply by 1.5. Your data beats any rule of thumb.
3. **Buffer the project, not every task.** Eliyahu Goldratt's critical chain method pools safety margin at the end. Padding each task just invites Parkinson's Law ("Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion") and the student syndrome of starting late.
4. **Run a pre-mortem** before committing to a deadline. See [decision-journal.md](decision-journal.md).
5. **Promise the buffered date. Aim for the unbuffered one.**

## Work-in-Progress Limits

Kanban (Japanese for a signboard or card) comes from Taiichi Ohno's Toyota Production System, where it limited inventory on the factory floor. David J. Anderson adapted it for knowledge work, and Jim Benson and Tonianne DeMaria Barry's *Personal Kanban* (2011) scaled it down to one person.

**The math behind it.** Little's Law, from queueing theory: average time to finish an item = items in progress ÷ items finished per unit of time. With constant throughput, doubling what's in progress doubles how long each thing takes. Starting more things does not make you faster. It makes everything later.

**The personal board:**
- Four columns: **Backlog → Ready → Doing → Done.**
- Set a hard limit on **Doing**. For most people, one to three items.
- Nothing new enters Doing until something leaves it. Finished, or deliberately killed.
- When stuck, don't start something new. Swarm the blocker.

**The rule for projects:** keep a small number of active projects, maybe three to five depending on size. Everything else is parked, and parked honestly: "not now" rather than "sort of in progress." The cost of switching between too many threads is covered in [career/work-principles.md](career/work-principles.md).

**Find the bottleneck.** Goldratt's *The Goal* (1984): a system moves only as fast as its tightest constraint. Effort spent anywhere else is mostly wasted. Ask what is actually holding the work back (a decision, a person, a skill, energy) and put your effort there. See [leverage-points.md](leverage-points.md).

## Definition of Done

Agile software teams use a **definition of done**: a shared, written standard every piece of work must meet before it counts as finished. The idea transfers everywhere. Without it, "done" means "I stopped," and half-finished work piles up disguised as progress.

**Write one for every recurring type of work.** Examples:
- **An essay is done when:** the argument fits in one sentence, it is cut by 20% from the draft, read aloud once, sources are checked, and it is published.
- **A client deliverable is done when:** it meets the stated requirements, someone else has reviewed it, it is delivered, and receipt is confirmed.
- **A home repair is done when:** it works, it has been tested under load, tools are put away, and the receipt is filed.

The definition protects against two failures at once: shipping sloppy work and polishing forever. See [craftsmanship.md](craftsmanship.md).

## Project Kickoff Template

Use it for anything that will take more than a week or involve other people. Fifteen minutes. One page.

1. **Project name** (a finished outcome, stated as done)
2. **Purpose:** why this matters, in one sentence
3. **Success criteria:** how you'll know it worked (measurable where possible)
4. **Definition of done:** the specific conditions for calling it finished
5. **Scope:** what is in, and explicitly what is out
6. **Owner** (one person) and **stakeholders** (who needs to know or approve)
7. **Deadline** (the buffered promise) and **target date** (the unbuffered aim)
8. **Milestones:** three to five checkpoints with dates
9. **Risks:** the pre-mortem list. It failed; why?
10. **Resources:** money, time blocks, tools, help required
11. **First next action:** the very next physical step, and when it happens
12. **Review cadence:** when you'll check progress (weekly by default)
13. **Kill criteria:** what would make you stop, and you'll honor it. This guards against sunk cost. See [mental-models/decision-making.md](mental-models/decision-making.md).

## Cadence

A system without a rhythm decays within weeks. The cadence is the system.

| Rhythm | Execution job | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Daily | Check the calendar, pick the most important task, work from next-action lists, capture everything | [routines/daily.md](routines/daily.md) |
| Weekly | Empty the inboxes, review every project, update lists, plan the week | [routines/weekly-review.md](routines/weekly-review.md) |
| Monthly | Score lead measures, check OKR progress, cut or add projects | 30 minutes, end of month |
| Quarterly | Score OKRs, set the next quarter's, archive finished projects | 2 hours |
| Annually | Revisit vision and purpose, set the year's direction | [routines/annual-review.md](routines/annual-review.md) |

Allen's summary of the weekly review is worth keeping: **get clear, get current, get creative.** If you only keep one rhythm, keep the weekly one. It is what makes everything else trustworthy.

## Tracking: Lead and Lag Measures

*The 4 Disciplines of Execution* (McChesney, Covey, and Huling, 2012) makes the distinction that fixes most goal tracking.

- **Lag measures** track the result: weight lost, revenue earned, book finished. They tell you if you won, but only after the fact, when it is too late to change.
- **Lead measures** track the behaviors that drive the result. They are **predictive** (moving them moves the lag) and **influenceable** (you control them directly): training sessions completed, sales calls made, words written per day.

**The protocol:**
1. Frame each goal as **from X to Y by when.** "From 210 to 190 pounds by September 1."
2. Pick one or two lead measures you believe drive it. "Protein target hit 6 days a week; 4 training sessions a week."
3. Keep a **simple visible scoreboard.** You should be able to tell at a glance whether you're winning.
4. Hold a **weekly accountability moment**: what did I commit to, did I do it, what's the commitment for next week.
5. Check monthly that the lead measures are actually moving the lag. If not, you picked the wrong levers.

**Goodhart's Law** (in Marilyn Strathern's phrasing): "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Track a few things, rotate them when they get gamed, and never confuse the number with the goal.

**On habits:** the "21 days" figure has no solid basis. In a study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues (2010), the median time for a new daily behavior to become automatic was about 66 days, with enormous variation between people and habits. Track longer than you think you need to.

## The System Trap

The productivity system is the most seductive form of procrastination. Designing it feels like work. Comparing apps feels like work. Rebuilding your tags feels like work. None of it is the work.

**Signs you are in the trap:**
- You have changed tools more than once in the last year.
- Your system takes more than about 10% of your working time to maintain. (This is a rule of thumb, not research.)
- You spend more time reading about productivity than producing.
- Your lists are beautiful and your projects are stalled.

**Gall's Law** (John Gall, *Systemantics*): "A complex system that works is invariably found to have evolved from a simple system that worked." Start with a notebook, a calendar, and a weekly review. Add complexity only when a specific, repeated failure demands it.

**The rules:**
1. Change the system only during the weekly or quarterly review, never mid-work.
2. Each change must fix a named, recurring problem.
3. Boring and used beats elegant and abandoned.
4. When in doubt, close the app and do the next action.

**Quick diagnosis:** things fall through the cracks, so capture is incomplete. Lists feel overwhelming, so items aren't clarified into next actions. Busy but nothing finishes, so too much is in progress. The system was abandoned, so the weekly review stopped. Productive at the wrong things, so the direction layer is missing.
