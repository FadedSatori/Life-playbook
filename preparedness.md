# Preparedness

A capable person is not someone who expects catastrophe. It is someone who has done the small, boring work so that ordinary emergencies stay ordinary. Power outages, car accidents, medical events, storms, floods, job loss, a lost wallet in a foreign country: these happen to nearly everyone eventually. The prepared person handles them with a clear head. The unprepared person improvises under stress.

This is [antifragility.md](antifragility.md) at the practical level and the Navy SEAL maxim made concrete: you don't rise to the occasion, you fall to the level of your preparation. It is also the Stoic *premeditatio malorum* turned into a checklist ([stoic-practice.md](stoic-practice.md)).

Scope: realistic, legal, proportionate preparedness for the events that actually happen. Not doomsday fantasy.

---

## The Mindset

- **Prepare for likely events first,** not dramatic ones. Your odds of a house fire, car trouble, a medical emergency, or a job loss exceed your odds of any apocalypse.
- **Proportion.** Preparedness is insurance. Spend a modest, one-time effort and a small annual review, not your life.
- **Skills over gear.** Gear can be lost or fail. Knowledge and trained responses go with you. Training beats equipment.
- **Redundancy for what matters.** Two is one, one is none, applied to critical needs: water, light, communication, copies of documents.
- **Calm is a skill.** Panic kills more than the original event. Rehearsal builds calm. See [tactics/cognitive.md](tactics/cognitive.md), [tactics/field.md](tactics/field.md).

## Rule of Threes (for prioritizing)

A widely used survival heuristic for how long you can go without, in harsh conditions:

- **3 minutes** without air (or in icy water, or with severe bleeding)
- **3 hours** without shelter in extreme heat or cold
- **3 days** without water
- **3 weeks** without food

Use it as a priority order: airway and bleeding first, then temperature/shelter, then water, then food. They're rough guides, not guarantees; individuals and conditions vary.

## Skills to Learn (in priority order)

Take a certified, hands-on course in each. Reading is not enough for physical skills.

### 1. Emergency medical response
Most useful single skill category, since it applies in cars, homes, gyms, and travel.
- **CPR and AED use.** Hands-only CPR for an adult who collapses: call emergency services, push hard and fast in the center of the chest (about 100–120 compressions per minute, around 5–6 cm deep for adults), and use an AED as soon as one is available. Get certified (Red Cross, AHA, or local equivalent).
- **Severe bleeding control.** "Stop the Bleed" training: direct pressure, packing a wound, and applying a tourniquet properly for life-threatening limb bleeding. Severe bleeding is a leading cause of preventable death after injury and can kill in minutes.
- **Choking response** and recovery position.
- **Wilderness first aid** (a multi-day course) if you spend time outdoors or far from help: fractures, hypothermia, heat illness, anaphylaxis.
- **Recognize emergencies:** stroke (FAST: face, arms, speech, time), heart attack, anaphylaxis, severe allergic reaction, overdose. Know what to do and call early.

### 2. Fire and home safety
- Working smoke and carbon monoxide detectors (test monthly; replace per manufacturer schedule).
- Fire extinguisher where you cook and know how to use it (PASS: pull, aim, squeeze, sweep).
- Two exits from every sleeping area and a rehearsed family plan with a meeting point.
- Know where the water, gas, and electrical shut-offs are.

### 3. Navigation and self-reliance outdoors
- Read a map, use a compass, and carry a charged phone with offline maps and a backup power bank.
- Tell someone your route and return time.
- Basic shelter, fire, and layering knowledge for your climate.
- Know your limits and turn around early.

### 4. Vehicle readiness
- Maintain it. Most breakdowns are preventable.
- Keep fuel above a quarter tank.
- Know how to change a tire, jump-start, check fluids.
- Car kit: water, snacks, jumper cables or a jump pack, flashlight, first aid kit, blanket, phone charger, basic tools, reflective triangle, and seasonal items (traction aids, warmer layers).

### 5. Physical capability
The best preparedness is a fit, strong, mobile body. Being able to carry a load, run when needed, climb, swim, and fight if necessary solves many problems. See [health/fitness.md](health/fitness.md), [martial-arts.md](martial-arts.md). Learn to swim well.

### 6. Situational awareness and personal safety
- Notice your surroundings; avoid distraction in unfamiliar settings. The cheapest self-defense is not being there.
- Jeff Cooper's color code as a mindset model: relaxed awareness in daily life, heightened alertness when something is off, decisive action when needed.
- Trust the discomfort signal and leave. See [tactics/field.md](tactics/field.md) and the gut signal in [dark-psychology.md](dark-psychology.md).
- Know exits, avoid isolated risky situations, share your location with trusted people when traveling.
- If you consider carrying any tool for self-defense, learn the law where you live and get proper training. Legal, trained, and responsible, or not at all.

## The Home Kit (72 Hours and Beyond)

Aim for at least 72 hours of self-sufficiency; many agencies suggest two weeks for some hazards. Build it once, check twice a year.

**Water:** About 4 liters (1 gallon) per person per day for drinking and basic hygiene. Store at least 3 days. Know how to purify (boiling, filters, chemical tablets).

**Food:** Shelf-stable food you'd actually eat, a manual can opener, and a way to heat it safely. Rotate stock.

**Light and power:** Flashlights and headlamps (spare batteries), a battery or hand-crank radio, power banks. Don't rely on candles.

**Medical:** A real first aid kit including tourniquet and trauma supplies if you're trained; a week or more of essential prescription medications if your health allows and your provider agrees; copies of prescriptions.

**Warmth and shelter:** Blankets, sleeping bags, warm layers, and a plan for heating or cooling during outages. Never run generators, grills, or camp stoves indoors (carbon monoxide kills).

**Sanitation:** Trash bags, wipes, hygiene supplies.

**Tools and utilities:** Multi-tool, duct tape, work gloves, whistle, fire extinguisher, dust masks.

**Documents and cash:** Copies (physical and encrypted digital) of IDs, insurance, deeds, medical info, and account details, plus small bills in cash. ATMs and card readers fail in outages.

**Communication:** An out-of-area emergency contact everyone knows, written numbers for key people (phones die), and a family meeting plan.

**People with special needs:** Infants, elderly, pets, medical devices: plan specifically.

**Know your local hazards.** Earthquake, flood, wildfire, hurricane, tornado, winter storms, and industrial risks differ. Check your local emergency management agency for guidance, evacuation routes, and alerts, and sign up for local alerts.

## Go-Bag (Get-Home or Evacuation)

A compact pack ready to leave quickly: water and a filter, snacks, a layer and rain shell, a headlamp, a first aid kit, a power bank and cables, copies of documents, cash, medications, a paper map, a multi-tool, and comfortable shoes (keep a pair at work). Check and refresh seasonally.

## Financial and Digital Resilience

Most emergencies that touch ordinary lives are financial or administrative.

- **Emergency fund:** 3–6 months of essential expenses. See [career/finances.md](career/finances.md). It is the single most useful preparedness item.
- **Insurance:** health, disability, home/renters, auto, adequate liability. See the insurance section in [career/finances.md](career/finances.md).
- **Income resilience:** a second income stream and marketable skills. See [wealth-building.md](wealth-building.md), [networks.md](networks.md).
- **Estate basics:** will, powers of attorney, healthcare directive, beneficiaries current.
- **Digital continuity:** password manager with emergency access for a trusted person, 3-2-1 backups, two-factor authentication with recovery codes stored safely. See [technology-leverage.md](technology-leverage.md).
- **Identity protection:** credit freezes, monitored accounts, unique passwords.

## Community Is the Real Safety Net

Individuals survive disasters mainly through other people. Neighbors help each other in the first hours; official help arrives later. Know your neighbors, skills in your circle, and who has what (medical, tools, trades). Build relationships before the crisis. See [community.md](community.md), [friendship.md](friendship.md), [networks.md](networks.md). Being the person others can count on is both a duty and a protection.

## Maintenance Routine

Add to [routines/annual-review.md](routines/annual-review.md) or the biannual clock change:

- Test smoke and CO detectors; replace batteries.
- Rotate water and food, check expiration dates.
- Check first aid kit and medications.
- Update documents, contacts, and the family plan.
- Refresh go-bag clothing for the season.
- Service the car; refresh its kit.
- Refresh skills: re-certify CPR, practice a fire drill.
- Review insurance and emergency fund.

Do a family drill once a year. Rehearsal is what turns knowledge into reflex.

---

## What Not To Do

- Don't buy gear you haven't learned to use.
- Don't let preparation become anxiety or a lifestyle of fear. Aim for competent calm.
- Don't hoard excessively or ignore the law.
- Don't neglect the likely for the dramatic.
- Don't do it alone. Include your household and neighbors.
