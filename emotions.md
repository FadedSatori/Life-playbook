# Emotions

Emotions are not the opposite of reason. They are your body's fast assessment of what matters right now and what to do about it. The man who ignores them isn't being rational. He is flying with half his instruments taped over.

Emotional intelligence is not niceness and it is not softness. Peter Salovey and John Mayer coined the term in 1990; Daniel Goleman popularized it in 1995, along with some claims (such as emotional intelligence mattering more than IQ) that outran the evidence. Strip away the hype and what remains is a set of trainable skills: perceive what you feel, name it precisely, regulate it deliberately, and read it in others.

This file is the general system. Specific emotions have their own files: [anger.md](anger.md) and [grief-and-loss.md](grief-and-loss.md). The disowned material lives in [shadow-work.md](shadow-work.md). Recovery from hard hits lives in [resilience.md](resilience.md).

## What Emotions Are For

The functional view: emotions are fast appraisals that prepare action.

- **Fear** mobilizes you to escape or avoid a threat.
- **Anger** prepares you to remove an obstacle or defend a boundary.
- **Sadness** registers loss, slows you down, and draws support.
- **Disgust** pushes you away from contamination, physical or moral.
- **Shame** protects your standing in the group. **Guilt** pushes you to repair a specific wrong.
- **Joy** says continue, approach, bond. **Interest** says explore.

Every emotion is a message plus a push. Your job is to read the message accurately and decide whether to follow the push.

**The science is not settled on what emotions are.** Two main camps:
- **Basic emotion views** (Paul Ekman and others): a small set of universal emotions, commonly listed as happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, and disgust, each with characteristic expressions and physiology.
- **The theory of constructed emotion** (Lisa Feldman Barrett, *How Emotions Are Made*, 2017): there is no dedicated circuit or fingerprint for each emotion. The brain constructs emotional experience from core affect (how pleasant or unpleasant, how calm or activated you feel) and the concepts you've learned for making sense of it in context.

A major 2019 review led by Barrett ("Emotional Expressions Reconsidered") concluded that facial movements don't map reliably one-to-one onto specific emotions. The debate continues. The practical upshot works under either view: emotions are information you can learn to read with more precision, and the concepts you have shape what you can recognize and regulate.

**Emotion vs. mood.** An emotion is brief (seconds to minutes), usually has an object (angry *at* something), and prepares a specific action. A mood lasts hours or days, is diffuse, has no clear object, and tints everything. You can reason with an emotion. A mood responds better to inputs: sleep, food, movement, daylight, contact with people. If you've been irritable for three days, check your body before you re-litigate every slight. See [health/sleep.md](health/sleep.md).

## Naming: Affect Labeling

Matthew Lieberman and colleagues (2007) had people view emotional faces while in a brain scanner. Choosing a word for the emotion ("angry," "scared") reduced amygdala response compared with other ways of processing the same images, while increasing activity in a region of the right prefrontal cortex. Later studies suggest labeling can modestly reduce reported distress too, and that people tend not to expect it to help, which is why it's underused.

**How to do it:** brief, specific, without judgment. "Anxiety. About Thursday's meeting." One clean label, not a twenty-minute monologue. Labeling is not venting and not rumination. Writing it down works as well as saying it.

## Emotional Granularity

Barrett's term for how finely you distinguish your emotions. Low granularity: "I feel bad." High granularity: "I'm disappointed and a little embarrassed, not angry." Correlational research reviewed by Todd Kashdan, Barrett, and Patrick McKnight (2015) links higher granularity, especially for negative emotions, to more flexible regulation and less reliance on unhealthy coping such as heavy drinking or lashing out under stress.

[tactics/cognitive.md](tactics/cognitive.md) covers the basic practice of pushing past the first label. The bottleneck for most people is vocabulary. Build it.

## The Feelings Vocabulary

Use this list during check-ins until the words come on their own.

- **Anger family:** irritated, annoyed, frustrated, impatient, resentful, indignant, bitter, furious
- **Fear family:** uneasy, nervous, apprehensive, worried, anxious, insecure, alarmed, panicked, dread
- **Sadness family:** disappointed, discouraged, lonely, hurt, wistful, melancholy, grieving, hopeless
- **Shame and guilt:** embarrassed, self-conscious, inadequate, exposed, humiliated, guilty, remorseful
- **Aversion:** distaste, disgust, revulsion, contempt, disdain
- **Joy family:** content, relieved, glad, satisfied, playful, delighted, elated
- **Connection:** affectionate, grateful, tender, trusting, warm, belonging, compassionate
- **Strength:** confident, determined, proud, capable, steady, fierce, free
- **Interest:** curious, engaged, absorbed, fascinated, eager, anticipatory
- **Low energy:** tired, flat, numb, bored, restless, empty, depleted
- **Mixed:** bittersweet, ambivalent, nostalgic, torn, envious, jealous

**Precision notes:**
- **Envy vs. jealousy.** Envy is wanting what someone else has. Jealousy is fearing you'll lose what you have to a rival.
- **Guilt vs. shame.** Guilt says "I did a bad thing." Shame says "I am bad." Guilt motivates repair; shame tends to motivate hiding.
- **Thoughts disguised as feelings.** "I feel that you're wrong" is a judgment. Marshall Rosenberg's *Nonviolent Communication* flags words like "abandoned," "ignored," and "betrayed": they blend a feeling with an accusation about what someone did. Underneath "betrayed" might be hurt, shocked, and scared. Name those.

## Reading the Body

Emotions often show up in the body before they reach words. Lauri Nummenmaa and colleagues (2014) asked people to color in where they felt different emotions; the resulting body maps were distinct for different emotions and fairly consistent across participants.

Common signals (they vary between people, so learn yours):
- **Jaw, fists, shoulders tight; heat in face or chest:** anger, frustration
- **Tight chest, shallow breath, churning stomach:** anxiety, fear
- **Heaviness, lump in throat, pressure behind the eyes:** sadness
- **Hot face, urge to hide, eyes down:** shame, embarrassment
- **Open chest, lightness, energy:** joy, pride, excitement
- **Recoil, nausea, curled lip:** disgust, contempt

**HALT** (from recovery communities): before you conclude an emotion is about the world, check whether you're Hungry, Angry, Lonely, or Tired. A surprising share of "this situation is intolerable" is low blood sugar and sleep debt.

For deeper interoceptive training (body scans, meditation, breathwork), see [consciousness-technologies.md](consciousness-technologies.md).

## Regulation: Gross's Process Model

James Gross's process model sorts regulation strategies by *when* they act in the life of an emotion. Five families, earliest to latest:

1. **Situation selection.** Choose which situations you enter. Don't go to the bar the week you quit drinking. Schedule the hard conversation for when you're rested. Underrated because it requires knowing yourself in advance. Caution: overused, it becomes avoidance, and avoidance feeds anxiety.
2. **Situation modification.** Change the situation you're in. Move the argument from text to face to face. Bring an agenda. Take a break.
3. **Attentional deployment.** Direct your attention: toward a different aspect, or briefly away. Rumination is this strategy running in reverse.
4. **Cognitive change.** Change what the situation means. "He's not attacking me; he's under pressure from his boss." "This pounding heart is my body getting ready to perform." This is reappraisal.
5. **Response modulation.** Act on the response once it's here: slow breathing, exercise, suppressing the outward expression, or (badly) drinking.

**Earlier is cheaper.** Strategies that act before the emotion fully builds generally cost less than those that fight it once it's running.

**Reappraisal vs. suppression.** Gross's research found that suppression (keeping a straight face while it burns inside) reduces visible expression but not the felt emotion, can raise physiological arousal, and can impair memory for what happened. In conversations, suppressors tend to come across as less engaged, and partners feel less connected. People who habitually reappraise report more positive emotion and better well-being than habitual suppressors (Gross and Oliver John, 2003).

**Three caveats:**
- **Reappraisal is not denial.** You're looking for a more accurate or more useful frame, not a false one.
- **If you can fix it, fix it.** Reappraisal fits best when you can't change the situation. Reappraising your way into tolerating a fixable problem is a trap.
- **Suppression is a tool, not a default.** At the negotiating table, in an emergency, in front of your kids during a crisis, holding your face is right. George Bonanno's work on regulatory flexibility suggests resilient people match the strategy to the situation. The cost comes from suppressing everything, always.

The warrior standard was never absence of emotion. It is command of response. See [stoic-practice.md](stoic-practice.md).

## Self-Compassion Is a Strength Tool

Kristin Neff (2003) defined self-compassion by three components, each with an opposite:

1. **Self-kindness vs. self-judgment.** Treat yourself in failure the way you'd treat a respected teammate: honest about the error, without contempt.
2. **Common humanity vs. isolation.** Failure and suffering are part of being human, not proof that you are uniquely defective.
3. **Mindfulness vs. over-identification.** Hold the pain in clear awareness: not suppressing it, not drowning in it.

**It is not softness.** Juliana Breines and Serena Chen (2012) ran four experiments in which a self-compassionate frame after failure or wrongdoing increased motivation to improve compared with a self-esteem boost or no intervention. People studied longer after failing a test and were more motivated to make amends after a transgression. The effects were modest and the samples mostly students, but the direction is clear: contempt for yourself breeds avoidance; self-compassion keeps you in the fight. It isn't self-pity, and it doesn't lower the standard.

The best coaches are demanding and on your side. Be that coach for yourself.

**After a failure, say three sentences:** "This hurts and I screwed up." (mindfulness) "Everyone who attempts hard things fails like this." (common humanity) "What do I need right now to get up and fix it?" (kindness, aimed at action)

## Men and Emotional Expression

No stereotypes: many men are emotionally fluent and many women aren't. But a few patterns are worth knowing.

**Alexithymia** is a personality trait, not a diagnosis: difficulty identifying and describing your own feelings, plus a thinking style turned outward to external facts. The term was coined by Peter Sifneos in the 1970s, and it is measured on a continuum, most commonly with the Toronto Alexithymia Scale. A 2009 meta-analysis led by Ronald Levant found men score slightly higher on average. It's a small difference with huge overlap between the sexes. Levant's "normative male alexithymia" hypothesis proposes that part of it comes from socialization: boys trained to hide vulnerable feelings, with anger left as the one sanctioned outlet. That's a hypothesis, not a settled finding.

**The cost of unnamed emotion.** What you can't name tends to come out through the body (tension, poor sleep), through behavior (drinking, overwork, numbing), or through anger. The U.S. National Institute of Mental Health notes that depression in men may show up as irritability, anger, or risk-taking rather than obvious sadness.

**The reframe.** Reading your internal state accurately is a performance skill, the same way reading terrain is. Operators, surgeons, fighters, and negotiators perform better when they can tell fear from fatigue from anger. See [masculinity.md](masculinity.md).

## The Daily Check-In Protocol

Two to five minutes, twice a day. Anchor it to existing habits: midday and evening. See [routines/daily.md](routines/daily.md).

1. **Stop and breathe.** Three slow breaths, long exhales.
2. **Scan the body.** Head to feet. Where is tension, heat, heaviness, energy?
3. **Rate core affect.** Pleasantness from -5 to +5. Energy from 1 to 10.
4. **Name it precisely.** Two or three words from the vocabulary list. "Fine," "stressed," and "tired" don't count.
5. **Find the source.** An event, a thought, a memory, a person, or a body state (HALT)?
6. **Read the message.** What is this telling me matters? What is it pushing me to do?
7. **Choose a response.** Act on it, change the situation, reappraise it, or let it pass. Respond; don't react.
8. **Log one line.** Date, time, words, intensity, source.

In your [weekly review](routines/weekly-review.md), read the log. Look for patterns: times of day, people, situations, sleep, training. The patterns are self-knowledge you can't get any other way.

## When to Get Professional Help

Emotional skill doesn't replace treatment. Using the right tool is strength. See a doctor or a licensed mental health professional if you notice:

- **Low mood, emptiness, or loss of interest** in most things, most days, for two weeks or more, especially with changes in sleep, appetite, energy, or concentration, or feelings of worthlessness. These are core signs of depression.
- **Worry or anxiety that is hard to control,** lasts for months, and interferes with work, sleep, or relationships; panic attacks; avoidance that keeps shrinking your life.
- **New or escalating irritability, rage, or recklessness.**
- **Using a substance or behavior to manage feelings** and being unable to stop. See [addiction-and-vices.md](addiction-and-vices.md).
- **Trauma symptoms** (intrusive memories, nightmares, hypervigilance, numbness) that persist more than about a month after an event.
- **Grief that isn't loosening** over many months.
- **Any thoughts of suicide or self-harm.**

**In a crisis:** in the US, call or text **988** (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline). If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. Outside the US, contact local emergency services or a local crisis line.

Effective treatments exist, including cognitive behavioral therapy, other evidence-based therapies, and medication where appropriate. Getting help early is cheaper than getting it late.
