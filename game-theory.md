# Game Theory

Most of life is a game in the technical sense: several parties, each choosing actions, where each outcome depends on what the others do. The people who see the structure of the game they are in make better moves than the people who only see the moves.

Game theory is the discipline of seeing structure. It explains why decent people produce bad outcomes together, why cooperation survives, why threats work or fail, and why the way a situation is set up matters more than the character of those inside it.

---

## First Question: What Game Is This?

Before choosing a move, identify the game.

- **Zero-sum or positive-sum?** Zero-sum: what one gains, another loses (a single prize, a lawsuit over a fixed sum). Positive-sum: cooperation creates value (trade, collaboration, most careers). Most real situations are mixed, and treating a positive-sum situation as zero-sum is among the most expensive errors in negotiation and relationships. See [negotiation.md](negotiation.md): the orange example is the whole lesson.
- **One-shot or repeated?** Behavior in a single encounter with a stranger differs from behavior when you will meet again.
- **Simultaneous or sequential?** Do players move at the same time, or can you see the other's move first?
- **Complete or incomplete information?** Does each side know the payoffs, the types, the history?
- **Who are the players and what do they actually want?** Not what they say.

Misidentifying the game is the root of most strategic error.

## The Prisoner's Dilemma

Two players each choose to cooperate or defect. Whatever the other does, defecting pays better for the individual, yet mutual defection leaves both worse off than mutual cooperation. In a single round, the rational individual move is defection; the collective result is poor.

This structure appears everywhere: arms races, price wars, overfishing and pollution (tragedy of the commons), doping in sport, two coworkers each hoarding credit, a couple each withholding first.

**The way out is repetition.** When the game repeats and the future matters (what Axelrod called "the shadow of the future"), cooperation can become the rational strategy.

### Axelrod's Tournaments
In computer tournaments run by Robert Axelrod around 1980 (*The Evolution of Cooperation*, 1984), game theorists submitted strategies for the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma. The simplest entry won both rounds: **Tit for Tat**, from Anatol Rapoport. It cooperates first, then copies the opponent's previous move.

The traits Axelrod found in the successful strategies:
1. **Nice:** never defect first.
2. **Retaliatory:** punish defection immediately, so you can't be exploited.
3. **Forgiving:** return to cooperation once the other does.
4. **Clear:** be predictable enough that others can learn to cooperate with you.

Later work adds a refinement: in noisy environments where signals can be misread, pure Tit for Tat can spiral into mutual punishment, and slightly more forgiving strategies (generous variants, or "win-stay, lose-shift") do better.

**Life translation:** Begin in good faith. Respond to betrayal proportionately and visibly. Forgive quickly when behavior changes. Be consistent so people can read you. This is a strategic restatement of the ethic in [warrior-codes.md](warrior-codes.md): be dangerous enough to be respected, decent enough to be trusted.

## Commitment, Credibility, and Threats

A threat works only if it is credible, and a credible threat is often one you would not rationally choose to carry out once the moment arrives, unless you have bound yourself.

- **Commitment devices** make your future behavior non-negotiable: burning the boats (Cortés is the legend, the principle is real), financial stakes, public promises, contracts. The power comes from removing your own option to back down. See pre-commitment in [reality-engineering.md](reality-engineering.md).
- **Reputation as a commitment device:** The person known to always follow through is believed without having to prove it each time. This is why consistency compounds ([networks.md](networks.md)).
- **Empty threats get called.** Never threaten what you won't do. Make fewer, real ones.
- **Leave the other side a way out.** A cornered opponent with nothing to lose fights harder than the payoffs predict. Offering a face-saving exit is often the strongest move. Sun Tzu: leave a surrounded enemy an outlet ([warfare.md](warfare.md)).
- **Brinkmanship / the game of Chicken:** Two parties on a collision course; the one who visibly cannot swerve wins if the other does. The danger is real: when both commit, both lose. Use rarely and with an exit.

## Equilibrium and Focal Points

**Nash equilibrium:** A set of strategies where no player can do better by changing their own strategy alone. Equilibria can be bad (the Prisoner's Dilemma's mutual defection). Seeing the equilibrium tells you where a system will settle if nobody changes the rules.

**Schelling points (focal points):** When people must coordinate without communicating, they converge on the option that stands out as natural or salient (Thomas Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict*, 1960). Meet someone in a big city with no prior agreement? You'd both think of the most obvious landmark at noon.
- **Use:** In ambiguous negotiations and coordination, propose the obvious, simple, fair-looking option; it attracts agreement. Round numbers, midpoints, precedents.
- **Defense:** Know that anchors and "obvious" splits shape outcomes. See anchoring in [negotiation.md](negotiation.md).

## Signaling

When people can't verify quality directly, they use signals. A signal is credible only if it is costly to fake (Michael Spence).

- A degree signals ability and persistence, partly independent of what it taught.
- A long track record is hard to fake.
- Costly sacrifice, skin in the game, and showing your work all signal sincerity.
- Cheap talk ("trust me") carries little weight.

**Applications:**
- **Build signals that are expensive to counterfeit:** results, public work, reliability over years. See [networks.md](networks.md).
- **Read signals correctly:** discount cheap talk, weight costly action. Behavior over promises. See future-faking in [dark-psychology.md](dark-psychology.md).
- **Beware signal inflation:** when everyone adopts a signal, it loses information and the cost of keeping up rises. Choose signals where you hold a real edge.
- **Avoid signaling traps:** much conspicuous consumption is a signaling contest nobody wins. See [wealth-building.md](wealth-building.md).

## Human Behavior Isn't Purely "Rational"

Experimental game theory shows people depart from the textbook model in predictable ways.

- **Ultimatum game:** One player proposes a split; the other can accept or reject (rejection leaves both with nothing). Pure rationality says accept any positive offer. In practice, offers below roughly 20–30% are often rejected. People pay to punish unfairness. Implication: a lopsided but "rational" offer can blow up a deal. Fairness matters and is predictable.
- **Reciprocity and altruistic punishment:** People will incur costs to punish cheaters, which sustains cooperation in groups.
- **Emotions as commitment devices:** Anger and a reputation for retaliation can deter exploitation; the same anger, uncontrolled, destroys value. See [anger.md](anger.md).
- **Bounded rationality:** Real people use heuristics, and under pressure revert to habit. Model the actual person, not the ideal one.

## Practical Playbook

1. **Identify the game and the players' real incentives.** Draw the payoff matrix if the situation is important. Ask what each side gains and loses from each pair of moves.
2. **Look for positive-sum moves first.** Expand the pie before dividing it.
3. **Make repeated games explicit.** Remind both sides the relationship continues. Lengthen the shadow of the future with ongoing commitments and visible reputations.
4. **Change the payoffs, not the people.** If incentives produce bad behavior, redesign them rather than lecturing. This is the "rules" lever in [leverage-points.md](leverage-points.md).
5. **Be nice, retaliatory, forgiving, clear.** The Axelrod profile.
6. **Make credible commitments** on what matters, and keep few.
7. **Preserve the other side's exit.** Avoid cornering.
8. **Read costly signals, discount cheap ones.**
9. **Watch for the other side's model of you.** What they believe you will do shapes what they do. Manage your reputation accordingly.
10. **Don't play games that are rigged against you.** Sometimes the best move is to leave the game or change the board. See "exit as power" in [counter-strategies.md](counter-strategies.md).

---

## Limits

Game theory simplifies. Real situations have fuzzy payoffs, hidden players, emotion, culture, and ethics. Use it as a lens to see structure, not as a calculator that dictates action. And remember what the model leaves out: the person across the table is not just a payoff function. Relationships, honor, and trust are not "irrational"; they are what let humans escape the Prison in the first place.
