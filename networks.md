# Networks and Reputation

Opportunity travels through people. Jobs, deals, introductions, funding, collaborators, and the best information rarely arrive through public channels. They arrive through someone who knows you, trusts you, and thinks of you at the right moment.

Your network and reputation are assets that compound like capital, and like capital they are built before they are needed. The person who starts building a network when they need a favor is already late.

This file covers the professional and reputational layer. The deep personal layer is [friendship.md](friendship.md) and [community.md](community.md). The two are different and both matter. Do not confuse them.

---

## What a Network Actually Is

A network is not a contact list. It is a set of relationships in which trust, knowledge of what you do, and willingness to act on your behalf exist.

Three properties define its strength:
1. **Trust:** They believe you are competent and reliable.
2. **Awareness:** They know what you do and what you are looking for.
3. **Recall:** They think of you when the relevant situation arises.

Most contacts have none of the three. A small number of people who have all three outperform thousands who have none.

### Weak ties and the surprising power of distance
Mark Granovetter's research ("The Strength of Weak Ties," 1973) found that people more often found jobs through acquaintances than close friends. The reason: close friends move in the same circles and know the same things. Acquaintances connect you to different clusters and new information.

**Implication:** Your closest circle is for depth, trust, and support. Your wider, looser network is for novelty, opportunity, and information. Cultivate both, deliberately, for different reasons.

### Structural holes
Ronald Burt's work: people who bridge otherwise disconnected groups hold unusual advantage, because they see and carry information that others in each group lack. Value flows to the connector. Seek to be a bridge between fields, communities, and cultures. This is the social version of [cross-domain thinking](cross-domain-thinking.md).

## Luck Surface Area

Opportunity is partly luck, but luck can be engineered. The formula:

**Luck surface area = (what you do) × (how many people know you do it)**

Do valuable, visible work, and tell people about it. The two multiply. Excellent work nobody sees produces little. Loud mediocre work builds a hollow reputation that collapses on contact with reality. Do excellent work *and* make it findable: writing, projects, talks, contributions, a clear public record. See [writing.md](writing.md) and [tactics/public-speaking.md](tactics/public-speaking.md).

## Give First

The most reliable way to build a network is to be useful to people with no immediate expectation of return.

**Practical forms of giving:**
- Introductions between two people who should know each other (the double opt-in introduction: ask each person whether they want the intro before making it).
- Sharing useful information, an article, a contact, a lead.
- Genuine, specific feedback or help on someone's work.
- Public credit and endorsement when deserved.
- Your time and attention, fully present.

**Why it works:** Reciprocity is a strong human norm (Cialdini; see [power-dynamics.md](power-dynamics.md)), but the bigger effect is reputation. Over years, you become known as someone who adds value, and people want to help you.

**The distinction from manipulation:** Giving in order to create a debt is a transaction in disguise, and people feel it. Give because you want the person and the field to be better off, and treat any return as a bonus. The ethical line is the same one drawn in [negotiation.md](negotiation.md).

## Reputation

Reputation is the accumulated, remembered record of how you behave, particularly when it is costly. It is what people say about you when you are not in the room. It is built slowly through consistent behavior and destroyed quickly by a single serious breach.

**The components:**
- **Competence:** You deliver quality.
- **Reliability:** You do what you say, on time, every time. The cheapest and rarest differentiator.
- **Integrity:** You are the same in private and public, and you tell the truth when it costs you.
- **Generosity:** You make others better off.
- **Judgment:** You are discreet, you read situations well, and you do not waste others' trust.

**Rules of reputation management:**
1. **Under-promise, over-deliver.** Small, repeated reliability compounds into a name.
2. **Return calls and emails; keep time.** Basic professionalism is the biggest bottleneck for most people. Doing the basics consistently puts you in the top tier.
3. **Never burn bridges needlessly.** The world is small and memories long. Leave every ending clean.
4. **Fix mistakes fast and openly.** How you handle a failure often defines your reputation more than the failure itself. See [failure.md](failure.md).
5. **Protect others' confidences absolutely.** Discretion is the foundation of being trusted with more.
6. **Do not gossip.** Whatever you say to someone about others tells them what you will say about them. Gossip is a sign of how you will treat the listener when they are the subject.
7. **Defend your name.** Greene's Law 5 is correct on the point: reputation is the foundation of power. Guard it with your behavior, not through noise.

## Building the Network: The Protocol

### 1. Map what you have
List the people in your professional world across three rings:
- **Core (5–15):** Trusted, regular contact, would help unprompted. See Dunbar layers in [community.md](community.md).
- **Active (50–150):** Know you, respect you, you are in contact a few times a year.
- **Latent:** Past colleagues, classmates, acquaintances you could reconnect with.

### 2. Decide what you are building toward
A network without direction is noise. Name the fields, roles, and kinds of people that matter for your [purpose](purpose.md) and next stage. Aim for density in two or three areas rather than a thin spread.

### 3. Seek mentors and peers deliberately
- **Mentors:** One or two people significantly ahead. Approach with respect for their time: do your homework, ask specific questions, show you acted on prior advice, and make it easy for them. The best way to get a mentor is to be a visibly serious student of the thing they have mastered. See [reality-engineering.md](reality-engineering.md) on the social environment.
- **Peers:** A small group of ambitious equals, regular contact, honest feedback, shared growth. These relationships often matter most over a long career.
- **Proteges:** Mentor others coming up behind. Teaching clarifies your own understanding, builds loyalty, and extends your reach.

### 4. Create contexts, not just contacts
Relationships form around shared activity. Join or build groups where repeated, meaningful interaction happens: a mastermind, a training group, a reading circle, a project team, a professional association with real engagement. Hosting is a high-leverage move. The person who convenes the dinner becomes the node. See the curation role in [community.md](community.md).

### 5. Maintain with low-cost, high-signal contact
Relationships decay without contact. Make maintenance a system:
- A simple log of key relationships, last contact, and what matters to them.
- A quarterly pass through the active ring: a short note, a useful article, a congratulation on news, a direct "thinking of you."
- Show up at key moments: promotions, losses, launches, births. Presence at these is remembered.
- Reconnect latent ties with a specific, sincere message, not a generic ping.

### 6. Ask well
When you ask for help, make it easy to say yes: specific request, clear context, minimal time, and an easy out. "Could you spare 15 minutes on X? Here is what I've already tried" beats "Can I pick your brain?" Say thank you, then close the loop afterwards and report what happened. People help those who make their help count.

## Online and Public Presence

Public presence extends reach. Treat it as a long-term asset, not a performance.

- **Say something real.** Share what you are learning and building. Specific and honest beats polished and generic.
- **Be consistent.** A steady cadence over years builds more than bursts.
- **Own the platform.** A personal site and email list outlast any social network. See [technology-leverage.md](technology-leverage.md).
- **Mind the permanence.** Assume everything is searchable and screenshot-able forever. Do not post in anger.
- **Do not chase approval metrics.** Likes measure attention, not value. Beware the validation loop. See [attention.md](attention.md).

## Protecting the Network From Poison

Not every connection adds value. Some drain or endanger.

- **Audit.** Which relationships energize you, expand you, and reflect your values? Which consistently take, undermine, or pull you down? Adjust time accordingly.
- **Recognize predatory patterns.** Flattery that arrives with an ask, rapid intimacy, pressure, inconsistent stories. See [dark-psychology.md](dark-psychology.md) and [counter-strategies.md](counter-strategies.md).
- **Set boundaries early.** Easier to hold a line from the start than to retrofit one.
- **Associate with people who raise your standard.** The average of the people closest to you sets your baseline. See [reality-engineering.md](reality-engineering.md).

---

## The Long Game

Networks and reputations pay off over decades, and the people who understand this behave differently:

- They treat every interaction as a deposit into a long relationship.
- They keep their word when it is inconvenient.
- They are generous without scorekeeping.
- They stay in touch when they do not need anything.

Ten years of this, and doors open that no application, resume, or cold email could.
