# Personal Security

Personal security is the discipline of keeping yourself and the people you are responsible for safe, without turning your life into a bunker. Its primary tools are awareness, avoidance, and preparation. Physical response is the last and least reliable tool, not the first.

Most men overinvest in the dramatic scenario (the stranger with a weapon) and underinvest in the common ones: the partner or ex who becomes dangerous, the acquaintance who fixates, the scam, the unlocked door, the drunk at closing time. Put effort where the risk actually is.

Everything here is defensive and proportionate. It describes recognition, documentation, reporting, and safety planning. It does not describe following, confronting, or investigating anyone. Laws vary by place; check yours and get qualified local advice.

---

## The Realistic Threat Picture

Public data from agencies such as the US Bureau of Justice Statistics and the CDC consistently shows the same broad pattern:

- **Violence and harassment usually come from someone the victim knows:** a current or former partner, a relative, an acquaintance, a coworker. Stranger attacks are a minority.
- **Stalking and intimate-partner abuse are widespread,** affecting many people at some point in their lives, and they are among the more predictable precursors of serious harm. Separation is a high-risk period.
- **Fraud and scams** touch far more people than violence does, and cause large financial and emotional damage. See [opsec-and-privacy.md](opsec-and-privacy.md).
- **Opportunistic crime** (theft from cars, burglary of unlocked or visibly empty homes, phone snatching) is the most common physical threat and the most preventable.
- **Situational risk** rises with alcohol, late hours, isolation, and unfamiliar places.

The implication: invest in your relationships and environment first. Choose partners and associates carefully, notice early warning signs, keep your home hard to enter, and avoid the settings where trouble concentrates. See [relationships/principles.md](relationships/principles.md) and [boundaries.md](boundaries.md).

## Awareness Without Paranoia

Awareness is a relaxed, wide-aperture habit, not constant vigilance. Hypervigilance burns you out and makes you worse at noticing real anomalies.

- **Learn the baseline.** Know what normal looks like in your neighborhood, workplace, and routes. Anomalies only show up against a baseline.
- **Trust the signal, check the story.** A gut feeling that something is off is information. You do not need to prove it before acting on it by leaving, moving, or calling someone.
- **Compensate when depleted.** Awareness drops when you are hungry, angry, lonely, or tired.
- **Phone down in transit.** Heads-down walking is a gift to thieves.

The Cooper color code, the HALT condition, and practical situational awareness are in [tactics/field.md](tactics/field.md). Do not repeat that work here; use it. For the cognitive trap of seeing threats everywhere, see [cognitive-biases.md](cognitive-biases.md).

## Pattern-of-Life Exposure

Anyone who wants to harm, rob, or harass you benefits from predictability. Your pattern of life is the set of places, times, and routines that repeat.

**What exposes it:**
- The same route, time, and parking spot every day.
- Real-time location sharing with a wide circle, or with anyone you would not trust with your schedule.
- Posting travel as it happens, rather than after you are home.
- Public check-ins, fitness-app routes, and tagged photos.
- Children's activities, schedules, and school details posted openly.
- Out-of-office messages and social posts announcing an empty home.

**Sensible countermeasures:**
- Vary routine where it is cheap: different routes, times, and parking occasionally. This is a low-cost habit, not a lifestyle.
- Share live location only with a few trusted people, and review the list after any breakup or falling-out.
- Post trips afterward. Keep addresses and school names out of public posts.
- Keep lights, mail, and deliveries managed when away, via a neighbor or a trusted person.

Scale to risk. A normal person needs a few habits. Someone dealing with an obsessive ex needs more, and should work with an advocate.

## Home Security Basics

Think in layers. No single measure stops a determined intruder, but layers push opportunistic ones to an easier house.

1. **Deterrence.** Make the home look occupied, watched, and not worth the effort: visible lighting, trimmed shrubs, clear sightlines, a dog, a sign or camera.
2. **Delay.** Slow entry. Solid exterior doors, quality deadbolts, reinforced strike plates with long screws, secure window locks, a locked garage, and a bar or pin on sliding doors. Most burglars use doors, so start there.
3. **Detection.** Know something has happened: smoke and carbon monoxide detectors first (see [preparedness.md](preparedness.md)), then door and window sensors, motion lighting, and cameras if you want them.
4. **Response.** Decide in advance what you do: a family plan, phone charged and within reach, a known safe room if you have one, and a call to emergency services as the first step rather than the last.

**Practical points:**
- **Lock up every time.** The most common weakness is an unlocked door or an open garage.
- **Do not hide keys** under mats or pots. Use a keypad lock or trusted neighbor.
- **Rekey or change codes** after a breakup, a tenant leaving, or a lost key.
- **Cameras and alarms:** useful as deterrence and for documentation. Choose reputable products, check current independent reviews, use unique passwords and updates on the devices, and keep cameras pointed at your own property. Insecure cameras can become a way for others to watch you.
- **Know your neighbors.** A neighbor who notices your door open and calls you is worth more than a gadget. See [community.md](community.md).
- **Do not advertise absences or valuables:** boxes from new electronics at the curb, packages piling up on the porch, full-car shopping left visible.
- **Vehicle:** lock it, take valuables with you, and park in lit, visible places.

## Recognizing Surveillance or Stalking (Defensive Only)

The aim is to recognize when you are being monitored or targeted so you can get help, not to investigate or counter-surveil anyone.

**Stalking, broadly, is a pattern of unwanted attention that makes a reasonable person fear for their safety.** Legal definitions vary by state and country, but the pattern is what matters.

**Possible signs:**
- Someone shows up repeatedly where you are, without a reason you can explain.
- A person seems to know your plans, location, or private conversations that you did not share with them.
- Repeated unwanted calls, texts, messages, gifts, or contact through friends, family, or new accounts after you asked them to stop.
- Your accounts show logins you do not recognize, or passwords and settings change.
- Your phone behaves strangely: fast battery drain, unexpected data use, unfamiliar apps or settings. These can have innocent causes, so treat them as prompts to check, not proof.
- A vehicle you do not recognize repeatedly nearby, or someone appearing to photograph you.
- Your location is known when you have not shared it.

**Technical side.**
- **Stalkerware** is software, often installed by someone with prior physical access to your phone, that secretly reports your location, messages, and activity. The Coalition Against Stalkerware publishes guidance on warning signs and where to find help.
- **Be careful removing it.** Deleting stalkerware or changing settings can alert the person and may destroy evidence. If you think a partner may be monitoring you, get advice from a domestic-violence advocate first.
- **Use a safe device.** If your own phone may be compromised, look for help on a device the person has never touched, such as a library computer or a trusted friend's phone.
- **Unknown trackers.** Modern phones include features that alert you to an unknown tracking tag traveling with you. Make sure those alerts are enabled and read your platform's current guidance.

## What to Do If You Think You Are Being Followed, Watched, or Stalked

**Do not confront the person. Do not try to follow or catch them. Do not retaliate.** Your job is to get safe and build a record.

1. **Move toward people.** Go to a public place, a staffed business, or a police station. Do not go home if you believe you are being followed there. Call emergency services (911 in the US) if you feel in immediate danger.
2. **Document.** Keep a log with dates, times, places, and what happened. Save messages, voicemails, emails, screenshots, and photos. Back copies up somewhere the person cannot reach. Note witnesses.
3. **Say it once, clearly, if safe.** Some advocates advise a single clear "do not contact me" message, then no response at all, since replies can feed the behavior. Ask an advocate whether this fits your situation, especially with an abusive partner.
4. **Tell trusted people:** family, friends, employer, building security, and school if children are involved. Give them a description and a plan to call you or the authorities.
5. **Report.** File a police report and ask for a case number. Reports help even if immediate action is limited.
6. **Consider legal protection.** Many jurisdictions offer protective, restraining, or no-contact orders. A court clerk, legal aid office, or an advocate can explain the local process.
7. **Safety plan with an advocate.** In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) offers 24-hour confidential support and safety planning, including for people who are not sure their situation qualifies. Confirm current contact options at thehotline.org. Outside the US, search for your country's equivalent service.
8. **Lock down your digital life:** change passwords from a safe device, turn on strong MFA, review linked accounts, location sharing, and shared devices. See [opsec-and-privacy.md](opsec-and-privacy.md).
9. **Expect it to take time.** Keep documenting until it stops.

## Online Harassment and Doxxing Response

Doxxing is the publication of your private information, such as your home address, workplace, or family details, to enable harassment.

1. **Document first.** Screenshot posts, usernames, URLs, and dates before they are deleted. Keep a dated log.
2. **Report to the platform** using its harassment and privacy-violation tools. Report each instance and keep the confirmations.
3. **Lock down accounts:** unique passwords, strong MFA, private profiles, review who can contact or tag you, and remove or trim exposed personal details where you can.
4. **Reduce what is findable.** Use data broker opt-outs and review public records. See [opsec-and-privacy.md](opsec-and-privacy.md).
5. **Alert the people around you:** employer, family, neighbors, and your children's school, so no one gives out information to a caller.
6. **Escalate threats.** Credible threats of violence belong with police. In the US you can also report to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3). Laws about harassment, stalking, and publishing private information vary by state and country; a lawyer or legal aid can explain your options.
7. **Do not engage.** Replying rarely ends it and often escalates. Do not retaliate in kind. Dox no one back.
8. **Get support.** Harassment wears people down. Talk to friends and, if it is heavy, a counselor. See [resilience.md](resilience.md).

## Traveling Safely

The general guide is in [travel.md](travel.md). Add these personal-security angles:

- **Hotel rooms:** avoid ground-floor rooms by easily reached windows if you can; use the door's secondary lock; do not open the door to anyone you did not call without verifying with the front desk. Do not announce your room number. Keep valuables in the safe or on you.
- **Rideshares:** match the plate, car, and driver to the app before you get in; ask the driver to say your name rather than offering it; share your trip with someone; sit where you can exit easily.
- **Solo travel:** share your itinerary with someone you trust and check in on a schedule. Know where the nearest hospital and embassy or consulate are. Keep local emergency numbers saved.
- **Nightlife:** go with people you trust, agree on a plan to leave together, watch your drink from pour to finish, pace your drinking, and leave when the vibe turns. Alcohol is a recurring factor in assaults and robberies. See [health/nutrition.md](health/nutrition.md) for alcohol and recovery.
- **Valuables:** carry little, split cash and cards, and keep copies of key documents stored securely online.
- **Post afterward,** not live.

## De-escalation and Avoidance First

The best fight is the one that never starts. Skill in this order:

1. **Awareness and positioning:** notice trouble early and arrange your life to avoid it.
2. **Avoidance:** leave early, take the other route, skip the venue, end the evening. Pride is cheap to lose; injuries are not.
3. **De-escalation:** lower your voice, slow down, keep your hands visible and open, give the other person a way out without losing face, and do not argue about who is right. Ego is the usual cause of avoidable violence. See [anger.md](anger.md), [communication.md](communication.md), and [boundaries.md](boundaries.md).
4. **Escape:** get out and get help, rather than staying to prove a point.
5. **Physical defense:** the last resort, when escape is not possible and you are in real danger.

**Law, in general terms.** Self-defense law in most places allows reasonable and proportionate force to protect yourself or others from imminent unlawful harm, and typically expects you to stop when the threat ends. Specifics (duty to retreat, defense of property, use of weapons, how "reasonable" is judged) differ widely by jurisdiction, and getting them wrong can end in prison. Learn your local law from a qualified source, ideally a reputable instructor or an attorney.

**Training.** Good instruction improves composure, fitness, and judgment as well as technique. Look for schools that train under realistic resistance and emphasize avoidance and legal awareness. See [martial-arts.md](martial-arts.md) and [health/fitness.md](health/fitness.md).

**No vigilantism.** Do not take the law into your own hands. Call the authorities, document, and let the system work, imperfect as it is. See [ethics.md](ethics.md) and [warrior-codes.md](warrior-codes.md).

## Protecting Family and Kids

- **Make home a place where kids can tell you anything.** Most protection depends on children being willing to tell you when something feels wrong. React calmly, thank them for telling, and never punish the messenger.
- **Teach body safety in age-appropriate terms:** correct names for body parts, that no adult should ask them to keep secrets from parents, and that they can say no and tell you. Use reputable child-safety organizations for age-appropriate scripts.
- **Know the people in their lives:** coaches, tutors, babysitters, friends' homes. Prefer group settings, and ask how adults handle one-on-one time.
- **Online safety:** keep devices in shared spaces for younger kids, use parental controls appropriate to age, talk about what to do if a stranger messages them, and talk about sending images. See [parenting.md](parenting.md).
- **Pickup rules:** a family password for unplanned pickups, and a plan if they are lost.
- **Family plan:** emergency contacts, a meeting place, and who to call. See [preparedness.md](preparedness.md).
- **Partners:** share the plan, and agree on what to do if either of you feels unsafe. See [relationships/intimate-partnership.md](relationships/intimate-partnership.md).

## Threat Self-Assessment

Answer honestly. Any yes deserves action.

1. Is there anyone who has threatened, stalked, or harassed me or my family?
2. Is there a person (current or former partner, coworker, neighbor) with a pattern of control, intimidation, or fixation?
3. Does anyone know my accounts, passwords, or home layout who should not?
4. Is my address, workplace, or children's school easy to find?
5. Do I have a public role, wealth, or conflict that raises my profile?
6. Are my routines predictable and my location widely shared?
7. Could someone enter my home easily?
8. Do my family and I know what to do in an emergency?

**Scoring.** No yes answers: maintain baseline habits. A few yes answers on exposure and home: fix those this month. Any yes on threats or a controlling person: contact an advocate, document, and plan now.

## Quarterly Review (30 Minutes)

1. Re-run the self-assessment. What changed?
2. Walk the property: locks, lights, doors, windows, sightlines.
3. Review who has keys, codes, and location access. Remove anyone who should not.
4. Review privacy and location settings on phones and apps.
5. Check emergency supplies, contacts, and the family plan. See [preparedness.md](preparedness.md).
6. Check your own state: sleep, stress, and drinking. See [health/sleep.md](health/sleep.md).
7. Reread your log of incidents, if you have one, for patterns.
8. Adjust one thing. Not twelve.
