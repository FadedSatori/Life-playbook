# Purpose

The question is not "what do you want?" That is a question for a consumer. The question is "what are you for?" That is a question for a person.

Viktor Frankl survived Auschwitz and three other Nazi concentration camps. His observation from that extreme: the men who survived were not the physically strongest. They were the men who had something they were living for — a person to return to, a work to complete, a meaning they could not yet abandon. The men who lost their sense of purpose died first. He called this "the last of the human freedoms" — the freedom to choose one's response to any circumstance, including the worst imaginable. That freedom, he observed, requires something to choose it *for*.

His framework: **logotherapy** — from *logos*, the Greek for meaning. The thesis: the primary human drive is not pleasure (Freud) or power (Adler), but meaning. A person who has a clear why can endure almost any how.

This file is about the why.

---

## Purpose vs. Goals vs. Mission

These three are frequently confused. They operate at different levels:

**Goals** are specific outcomes you are working toward. Measurable, time-bounded, achievable. They live inside a time horizon. Goals serve purpose; they are not purpose.

**Mission** is the focused expression of purpose in a particular domain or context. Your mission at work, your mission in a relationship, your mission in a community. Mission is directional — it points at a specific field where the purpose is expressed.

**Purpose** is the deepest level — the orientation of your existence, the answer to "what are you ultimately for?" Purpose does not expire with the achievement of a goal. It is not bound to a job title or a role. It is the animating force that makes particular goals and missions worth pursuing.

The person who only has goals has a map with destinations but no compass. When one destination is reached, they must invent a new one without knowing why. The person who has purpose generates goals naturally — because they know what they are building toward and why.

## The Question Frankl Could Not Escape

Frankl's central therapeutic insight: meaning cannot be given or prescribed. It must be found or constructed by the individual. The therapist cannot tell you what your life means. The priest cannot either. The meaning is yours to discover.

But it is discoverable. And the search is not arbitrary.

He identified three paths:
1. **Creating or producing something** — the work you bring into the world
2. **Experiencing something or encountering someone** — love, truth, beauty, or another human being encountered fully
3. **The attitude we take toward unavoidable suffering** — how we choose to bear what cannot be changed

Most people, when asked about their purpose, reach immediately for path one — the creative/productive path. This makes sense. But Frankl's insight is that paths two and three are equally valid. The person who loves deeply and is loved deeply has found meaning. The person who faces suffering without bitterness and uses it to develop compassion has found meaning. Purpose is not only about what you produce.

## Ikigai: The Japanese Framework

The Okinawan concept of *ikigai* (生き甲斐) — roughly translated as "a reason for being" — is often visualized as the intersection of four circles:

1. **What you love** — what you would do for its own sake, without external reward
2. **What the world needs** — what creates genuine value for others
3. **What you are good at** — your skills, capacities, and earned expertise
4. **What you can be paid for** — what has economic viability in the world

The intersections:
- Love + Good at (but not needed or paid for) = Delight and fullness, but no wealth
- Good at + Paid for (but not loved or needed) = Comfortable but empty — a "profession" without soul
- Needed + Paid for (but not loved or good at) = Useful but unfulfilling — a grind
- Love + Needed (but not good at or paid for) = Excitement and contribution, but unsustainable

**The center** — where all four overlap — is ikigai. Not all four need to be maximized equally. But the person who is entirely in one circle and ignoring the others is building on one leg.

The practical move: audit each circle honestly. Where are you strong? Where are you weak? The gaps are development targets.

## Bronnie Ware's Deathbed Research

Bronnie Ware, an Australian palliative care nurse, spent years recording the final reflections of the dying. Her book *The Top Five Regrets of the Dying* catalogs what people regretted when they could no longer change it.

The five regrets, in order:
1. **"I wish I'd had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected of me."** The most common. The person who spent 40 years building someone else's version of their life.
2. **"I wish I hadn't worked so hard."** Most men, specifically. The missed time with children, with partners, with the texture of daily life — exchanged for the abstraction of a career.
3. **"I wish I'd had the courage to express my feelings."** The unexpressed grief, gratitude, love, and conflict that accumulated into regret.
4. **"I wish I had stayed in touch with my friends."** The friends lost to time, busyness, and the assumption that they would still be there.
5. **"I wish I had let myself be happier."** The person who never noticed they were performing misery rather than living through it.

None of the five regrets are about failing to achieve goals. The regrets are about failing to live from authentic purpose — failing to know what they were for and then actually pursue it.

**The mortality test:** Project yourself to that final accounting. What would your version of these regrets be? What would the 85-year-old version of you wish you had done differently in the year ahead? This is not morbidity — it is the sharpest clarity tool available.

## Discovered vs. Constructed Purpose

There are two traditions on how purpose is found:

**The discovery tradition:** purpose is already there, waiting. Your task is to find it — in your nature, your calling, your God-given capacity. In this view, purpose is something real that exists prior to your search for it, and the work is to tune into what is already true.

**The construction tradition:** purpose is made, not found. You choose what to stand for, what to invest your life in, what to build. In this view, no purpose exists until you create it through commitment and action.

The practical answer is that both are true at different stages. Early in life, you experiment and explore until something generates genuine resonance — patterns that feel more like discovery than invention. You "find" what you care about by exposure to the world. Later, you construct — you choose to deepen and develop what you found, to commit to a direction even when the initial charge of discovery has faded.

The error of the discovery-only view: paralysis. Waiting for a calling that announces itself dramatically before committing to anything. Most callings are quiet and cumulative; they announce themselves through patterns across years, not one thunderclap.

The error of the construction-only view: the construction is arbitrary. You can construct a commitment to anything. Without some reference to what you actually are — your nature, your gifts, the things that generate genuine energy — you are building on sand.

**The working synthesis:** Explore broadly until patterns emerge. Then choose. Then live as though the choice matters permanently — because it does.

## Purpose and Identity

James Clear's formulation: identity is the anchor for behavior change. The person who identifies as a runner runs. The identity precedes the behavior.

Apply this to purpose: the person who has integrated their purpose into their identity does not ask themselves each morning whether to pursue it. It is not a goal they choose — it is who they are. The choice was already made, at a level deeper than daily decision.

The difference between purpose as a mission statement and purpose as an identity:
- **Mission statement:** "I want to build technology that makes healthcare accessible." (External target)
- **Identity:** "I am someone who uses technology as a lever to reduce unnecessary suffering." (Internal orientation)

The identity form survives the failure of a specific mission. When the company fails, the career shifts, the context changes — the person with the identity-level purpose reorients to the new terrain without losing the thread. The person with only a mission statement has to start over.

## The Purpose Statement

A practical output of the work in this file: a single sentence that answers the question "what are you for?"

Not a career goal. Not a mission statement. A statement of fundamental orientation.

**The format:** "I exist to [verb — the core action] [for whom or what] [toward what end]."

**Examples of the form (not the content — yours will be different):**
- "I exist to develop and transmit the ideas and practices that allow men to become more fully themselves."
- "I exist to build systems that reduce the friction between human capability and human expression."
- "I exist to demonstrate through my own life that a person can be both rigorously excellent and genuinely loving."

The test of the statement: does it generalize across contexts? Does it survive the loss of any particular role? Does it still apply in your relationships, in your private practice, in how you handle difficulty — not only in your career?

If it only applies at work, it is a mission statement, not a purpose.

**The revision process:** Purpose statements are not written once. They are drafted and refined over years as you learn more about what you are actually for. Write the current best version. Test it against your actual behavior. Revise when the behavior reveals a gap.

## The Daily Practice of Purpose

Purpose is not a document. It is a practice.

**Morning orientation:** Before engaging with the day's tasks, return to the fundamental question: what am I for? One sentence, once a day, as a compass calibration. This is not self-help journaling. It is navigation.

**The purposeful decision filter:** When facing a significant decision, ask: which option is more in line with what I am for? Not what is easier, what produces more money, or what others expect. What is more aligned with the purpose?

**Purpose under pressure:** The test of whether purpose has been genuinely integrated is what happens under duress. When things go wrong, when the path is hard, when the cost is real — what remains? The person whose purpose is still there under pressure has something real. The person whose purpose evaporates when it becomes inconvenient had a decoration, not an anchor.

**The annual reassessment:** Once per year — see [routines/annual-review.md](routines/annual-review.md) — return to the purpose statement. Is it still true? What have you learned about what you are for that you didn't know a year ago? What parts of it have been tested and proven? What parts revealed themselves as performance?

## The Relationship Between Purpose and Suffering

Frankl's deepest observation: suffering that has meaning is bearable. Suffering that has no meaning destroys.

This is not the same as saying suffering is good, or that you should seek it out. It is an observation about the psychological structure of human endurance. The person who can answer "why am I going through this?" — who has a context large enough to contain the difficulty — can survive what would destroy the person without that context.

Purpose is that context. Not a theological explanation, necessarily. Not a guarantee that suffering will end. Simply: a frame large enough to hold the difficulty without being reduced to it.

The practical implication: invest in purpose before the crisis. The person who discovers the question of meaning during the crisis is working at the hardest possible moment. The person who has already built their answer can access it when they need it most.
