# Decision Making

## The Core Problem

Most bad decisions are made in one of three states: under time pressure, under emotional influence, or with incomplete framing. The goal is to slow down and reframe before committing.

---

## Frameworks

### 10/10/10 Rule
How will you feel about this decision in:
- 10 minutes?
- 10 months?
- 10 years?

Useful for: decisions driven by short-term emotion (fear, excitement, anger).

---

### Regret Minimization
Imagine yourself at 80, looking back. Which choice would you regret more — doing it, or not doing it?

Useful for: big life decisions, risky bets, things you've been putting off.

---

### Reversibility Filter
Is this decision reversible or irreversible?

- **Reversible:** Decide fast and iterate. Overthinking is expensive.
- **Irreversible:** Slow down. Apply more scrutiny. Get more data.

Most decisions feel more permanent than they are.

---

### Pre-mortem
Assume the decision fails spectacularly. What caused it?

Working backward from failure surfaces risks that optimism blinds you to.

---

### The 2-Way Door
Bezos's framework: is this a one-way door (hard to undo) or a two-way door (easy to reverse)?

Two-way doors should be made quickly, at the lowest level possible. One-way doors deserve more time and input.

---

## Decision Rules

1. **Never make a major decision when angry, exhausted, or euphoric.** Sleep on it.
2. **Beware of decisions made under social pressure.** "Everyone else is doing it" is not a reason.
3. **If you can't explain why you're doing something, that's a signal.**
4. **A good process with a bad outcome is still a good process.** Don't let results bias your evaluation of how you decided.
5. **When genuinely torn between two options, flip a coin — then notice how you feel about the result.** Your gut has an answer.

## Sunk Cost Fallacy

**The pattern:** Continuing a course of action because of resources already invested (time, money, effort, identity) rather than because of its future value.

**The mechanism:** Daniel Kahneman's loss aversion — losses feel approximately twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good. The sunk cost fallacy is loss aversion applied to past investments: we continue to protect the "investment" to avoid experiencing the loss of admitting it was wrong.

**Identity entanglement makes it worse:** When a decision was made publicly, or when identity became attached to it (the person who bought the startup, the person who chose that career), the sunk cost protection is not just economic — it is existential. Reversing the decision feels like admitting you were wrong in a way that threatens self-concept.

**The test:** Strip away the history. If you arrived at this situation fresh today — with no prior investment, no public commitment, no identity attached — what would you choose? That is the decision.

Apply this especially to: long-running projects that have lost their rationale, relationships held together by history rather than present value, positions defended by the fact of having taken them publicly.

## Base Rate Neglect (The Outside View)

**The pattern:** When evaluating a plan or project, focusing on its specific features (the inside view) while ignoring how projects like this typically perform (the outside view).

**The mechanism:** Kahneman and Lovallo's research on planning fallacy. When asked to forecast outcomes for their own projects, people systematically produce optimistic estimates that ignore the base rate for comparable projects. The entrepreneur who believes their restaurant will succeed despite 60% industry failure rates in year one is committing base rate neglect.

**The fix — reference class forecasting:**
1. Identify the reference class: all projects of this type, in comparable conditions, attempted by comparable people
2. Find the base rate: what percentage succeed? What does success typically look like at 1 year, 3 years, 5 years?
3. Start with the base rate as your prior
4. Adjust for the specific differentiating features of your case — but adjust modestly, because your project probably isn't as exceptional as it feels from the inside

**The outside view is uncomfortable** — it strips the narrative away. Use it precisely because it's uncomfortable. Your inside view is already optimistic by default.

## The Pre-Parade

A complement to the pre-mortem. Where the pre-mortem asks "assume this failed — what caused it?", the pre-parade asks "assume this succeeded spectacularly — what made it work?"

**How to use it:**
1. Imagine the decision has produced its best possible outcome, 3–5 years from now
2. What conditions were present that enabled it?
3. What decisions were made well along the way?
4. What did you do in the first 90 days that mattered most?

The pre-parade is not about optimism — it is about success architecture. It surfaces the conditions and early actions that maximize probability of the good outcome. Pair it with the pre-mortem: know both what to avoid and what to build toward.

## Second-Order Thinking

**The principle:** Most people stop at first-order effects — the direct, immediate consequence of a decision. Second-order thinking asks: what is the consequence of that consequence?

Howard Marks (investor): *"First-level thinking is simplistic and superficial... Second-level thinking is deep, complex and convoluted."*

**Example:**
- First order: "Raise the price" → revenue per unit increases
- Second order: "At higher prices, some customers leave" → volume decreases
- Third order: "Lower volume weakens market position and network effects" → competitive moat erodes

**How to use it:**
Before any significant decision, explicitly write:
- **First-order effect:** What happens directly and immediately?
- **Second-order effect:** What happens as a result of that?
- **Third-order effect:** What happens as a result of that?

Second and third-order effects are where decisions differentiate. Most people in most competitive contexts are operating at first-order. The person consistently thinking one level deeper has a durable advantage — in investing, strategy, relationships, and life design.

*"The problem with most people's thinking is that they consider only the first-order consequences."* — Howard Marks
