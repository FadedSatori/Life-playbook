# Work Principles

How you work determines what you produce. Most people treat work structure as secondary — they focus on what to work on and assume the how will take care of itself. The how is not secondary. The how is where the gap between potential and output lives.

---

## The Foundation: Deep Work

**Deep work** (Cal Newport's term): cognitively demanding work performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that pushes the limits of your ability and produces irreplaceable value.

This is the main event. Everything else — meetings, messages, admin, coordination — is overhead in service of it. Protect it accordingly.

**Why it's rare and valuable:**
- The modern attention economy is specifically designed to fragment it
- Most people have not developed the capacity to sustain it for more than 90 minutes at a stretch
- The value of deep work compounds: insights connect, skills sharpen, output accelerates
- Rarity + value = leverage. The person who can produce four hours of genuine deep work daily outperforms most teams

**The attention residue problem** (Sophie Leroy's research): When you switch from one task to another, cognitive resources tied to the first task don't immediately transfer. A residue of attention remains on the previous task, impairing performance on the new one. The implication: every interruption — even a brief one — does not cost only the time of the interruption. It costs the transition cost on each side. A 30-second check of email in the middle of deep work can cost 20 minutes of re-engagement. The shallow work doesn't just interrupt — it degrades.

## The Four Depth Modes

Not every life configuration allows the same depth architecture. Choose the mode that fits your current situation — and build toward the one that would give you the most output.

**Monastic:** Eliminate nearly all shallow obligations. Reserve the working hours for deep work only. Contact and availability are minimal and structured. Best for: writers, researchers, solo creators with maximum autonomy. Requires: sustainable income model that doesn't demand constant availability.

**Bimodal:** Divide time into clearly defined deep and shallow periods — alternating between stretches of monk-mode focus (days, weeks, or seasons) and connected, available periods. Best for: people with variable obligations, academic schedules, project-based work.

**Rhythmic:** Build a regular daily deep work habit at a fixed time. Make depth as automatic as breakfast. Best for: most people in organizational roles who need predictable structure. The key: the schedule must be honored in advance, not decided day-of.

**Journalistic:** Seize depth whenever gaps appear — any 90-minute window is a potential deep work session. Best for: experienced practitioners with strong attentional control. Not a beginner mode. Requires the ability to switch into depth quickly and protect it from interruption even without structural help.

**The critical insight across all modes:** depth sessions must be scheduled weeks in advance, not decided in the morning. The work will fill whatever container you give it. Give it the first and largest container.

## Time Blocking: The Architecture

A to-do list cannot produce depth. A list of tasks is structurally incapable of protecting the time to do them. A task list tells you what to do; a time-blocked schedule tells you when.

**The system:**

1. **Before the week begins:** Time-block every hour of the workday into task categories. Deep work blocks come first. Shallow work (email, admin, meetings) fills remaining space.

2. **Fixed-schedule productivity:** Set your shutdown time first. Let the constraint compress the work to fit. An 8-hour day with no end time will consume itself with Parkinson's Law. A 6-hour day with a hard stop produces the same or more output through forced prioritization.

3. **The shutdown ritual:** At the end of every workday, complete a shutdown ritual that signals the brain that work is done for the day. This is not optional — the brain generates intrusive work-related thoughts during off hours specifically because open loops feel unresolved. The Zeigarnik effect: the brain actively maintains incomplete tasks in working memory, generating intrusive thoughts until they're closed. The ritual closes the loops. Components: review open tasks, set tomorrow's first deep work task, capture everything in a trusted system (not memory), say the phrase "shutdown complete" (or your equivalent). This prevents work thoughts from colonizing recovery time.

**Replanning is not failure.** When the plan changes during the day (and it will), reblock the remaining time on paper. You haven't lost the system — you've updated the map.

**The depth metric:** Track your daily deep work hours. The target for most knowledge workers: 4 hours of genuine deep work is an elite day. 3 hours is strong. Fewer than 2 hours most days means the architecture needs work. Don't count time at a desk — count time in uninterrupted, cognitively taxing concentration.

## Communication Protocol

Ambient communication is the enemy of depth. The goal is not to minimize communication but to batch it in a way that removes its ability to fragment concentrated work.

**The batching system:**
- Check and respond to messages 2–3 times per day at defined windows (morning, midday, end of day)
- Outside those windows, notifications are off
- Exceptions: pre-agreed urgent channels with a small number of people who understand what "urgent" means

**Message quality rules:**
- The one-sentence response rule: if a message can be replied to in one sentence, do it immediately during a batching window
- Question consolidation: never send a message that could be combined with the next 3 questions you need to ask the same person. Batch your questions; reduce their interruptions
- Be specific in requests: "Can we talk?" is worse than "15 minutes Thursday to discuss the launch date — does 2pm work?" The former creates an open loop; the latter closes it
- The process-centric approach: instead of rapid back-and-forth, identify the minimal number of exchanges needed to close a loop and design a single message that contains the context needed for a definitive response

**Meeting hygiene:**
- Default meeting duration: 25 minutes (not 30 or 60 — the odd number signals intentionality)
- Every meeting requires a stated purpose and desired outcome, written in the invite. If you can't state the purpose in one sentence, the meeting is not ready to happen
- Default to no. Meeting requests that could be handled async should be handled async
- If you must be in a meeting: come prepared, leave with written action items and owners
- The standing meeting default: most recurring meetings should be reviewed quarterly. Most are no longer necessary at their current frequency

**Async first:** Real-time communication produces the feeling of productivity while disrupting the conditions for actual productivity. Use it only when async doesn't work — when real-time feedback loops are the actual point (creative collaboration, relationship-building, sensitive conflict resolution).

## Environment Design for Depth

The environment is not neutral. It either supports deep work or undermines it. Design it deliberately.

**Physical space:**
- Deep work requires an environment your brain associates with depth. If you do shallow work in the same location, your brain will route to shallow-work mode. Create a separate space for deep work if possible — or at minimum, consistent rituals that signal the transition
- Remove all phones from the room during deep work sessions. Not silenced — removed. The mere presence of a smartphone within reach (even face-down, even off) measurably reduces available cognitive capacity (Ward et al., 2017 study)
- Noise policy: ambient background noise at café levels (~70dB) has modest benefits for creative work (see [creativity.md](../creativity.md)). For analytical deep work, silence is superior. Match the environment to the task type

**Digital environment:**
- Use a site and app blocker (Freedom, Cold Turkey, Focus) during deep work sessions. Willpower against the internet is a losing battle; infrastructure beats intention
- Notifications: default all apps to no notification. Turn on only what you deliberately choose. The default should be silence, not noise
- The one-browser-window rule: during deep work, have only one browser window open, relevant to the task. Closing all other tabs removes the temptation and the attention residue

## Output vs. Consumption

The ratio of time in creation mode vs. consumption mode is the most reliable predictor of productive output. Track it, if only approximately.

**Creation:** writing, building, designing, solving, analyzing, producing anything that didn't exist before the session
**Consumption:** reading, meetings, reviewing, replying, browsing, absorbing

Most knowledge workers spend the majority of their time in consumption mode and wonder why they produce so little. The reframe: **consumption serves creation.** Reading a book is consumption. But if the reading directly enables better creation, it's justified. If it's absorbing information with no clear application, it's probably avoidance.

**Default state = creation.** When no specific task is active, default to: reading deliberately (see [reading-system.md](../learning/reading-system.md)), writing (the forcing function for clear thinking), or building a skill. Not browsing. Not passive consumption.

## Career Capital Theory

Most people approach career with the passion mindset: find what you're passionate about, then do that. This is backward and it produces fragile careers.

The craftsman mindset: focus on getting so good they can't ignore you. Get excellent at something rare and valuable. Career capital — rare skills that the market values — is what gives you leverage, options, and eventually the ability to pursue a meaningful mission.

**The sequence:**
1. Build rare and valuable skills through deliberate practice at the edge of your ability (see [skill-building.md](../learning/skill-building.md))
2. Career capital creates leverage: autonomy, mission, creative control
3. Passion tends to follow mastery, not precede it — you become passionate about things you're good at

**Deliberate practice applied to career work:** Not just putting in hours but working at the edge of current capability, seeking immediate feedback, isolating the specific weaknesses, and practicing those specifically. The person who has been doing a job for ten years using comfortable routines has less than one year of deliberate practice. The person who has been pushing their limits for three years has three years of compounding growth.

**Build skills that compound.** Rare and valuable > common and safe. A skill that multiplies the effectiveness of other skills is worth more than a specialized skill with no transfer value. Writing, clear thinking, and strategic communication multiply everything. Deep technical expertise in a valued domain creates defensible leverage. Accumulate both.

**The career capital audit:** Quarterly, identify: What skills do I have that are genuinely rare and valuable? What am I building toward that will be more valuable in 3 years than it is today? What am I practicing in comfort zone that is producing no new skill? The last category needs to shrink.

## Working on the Right Things

Depth applied to the wrong things is wasted. Before asking "how do I do this well?" ask "is this worth doing at all?"

**The MIT (Most Important Task) discipline:** Every day has one task that, if completed, would make the day successful regardless of everything else. Identify it before the day begins. Do it first, before the world's noise accumulates. Do not move to secondary tasks until the MIT is complete.

**The 80/20 project audit:** In any project portfolio, a small fraction of projects produce the majority of value. Before adding a new project or commitment, audit the existing ones: which 20% are producing 80% of meaningful output? Direct disproportionate energy there. Ruthlessly deprioritize or eliminate the rest.

**Good enough to ship:** Perfect is an infinite standard. "Good enough to put your name on" is not. The quality bar is not "could this be better?" — everything could always be better. The bar is: "Would I be proud to show this to the person whose opinion I respect most?" If yes, ship it. The next iteration will be better because of what you learn from shipping.

**Rework is expensive:** Think clearly before starting, not while approaching the deadline. A slow, careful decision before committing to a direction costs far less than a fast start in the wrong direction.

## The Work-Life Boundary

Recovery is not wasted time. It is the other half of the system.

The brain cannot sustain deep concentration indefinitely. The research on elite performers (musicians, athletes, chess players) consistently shows that the highest performers practice for 4–5 focused hours per day — not 12 hours. Beyond the sustainable concentration ceiling, additional time produces diminishing returns, then negative returns (fatigue compounds, insight degrades, errors increase).

**Protect the off-switch:** When work ends for the day, it ends. No checking messages during dinner. No "just one more thing" after shutdown ritual. The shutdown ritual exists specifically to make this psychologically possible. The brain that doesn't fully recover doesn't fully perform.

**The weekend rule:** At minimum one full day per week with no professional communication — no email, no Slack, no work-related reading. This is not laziness. It is the maintenance cycle for the system. Every elite performance domain builds in this structure. Knowledge work pretends it doesn't need it. It's wrong.

---

## Principles (compressed)

- Deep work is the main event. Protect it before anything else.
- Do the hard thing first. The MIT gets the first hours.
- Fewer commitments, done better. Every yes is a no to something else.
- Finish what you start. Completion bias over starting-new-things bias.
- Build skills that compound. Rare and valuable > common and safe.
- Reputation is built slowly and lost quickly. Act accordingly.
- Do work that would matter even if no one was watching.
- The depth metric doesn't lie. Hours of genuine deep work per day is the number that matters.
