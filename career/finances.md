# Finances

Money buys time and options. That is what it is for. Not status, not security theater, not lifestyle inflation. Time and options — the ability to say no to things that don't serve you and yes to things that do.

Build toward that, systematically.

---

## Rules

1. **Pay yourself first.** Savings and investments are automated before lifestyle spending is possible. If it's not automatic, it won't happen consistently.
2. **Live below your means, not at them.** The gap between income and spending is the source of all financial freedom. Guard it.
3. **Time in market beats timing the market.** Invest consistently; don't try to be clever.
4. **Debt is a tool, not a lifestyle.** Only use leverage for assets that appreciate or generate income.
5. **Know your numbers.** Review spending monthly. What gets measured gets managed.

---

## The Wealth Formula

Wealth = Income − Spending + (Investments × Time × Returns)

The three levers: income, spending, and the compounding factor. Most people try to pull only the income lever. The spending lever is faster to move and more reliable. The compounding factor is the multiplier that rewards both.

**The savings rate is the variable that matters most early.** At a 5% savings rate, you need approximately 66 working years to retire. At a 50% savings rate, you need 17 years. At a 75% savings rate, 7 years. These numbers come from the math of financial independence, not from optimism — they follow directly from what your savings can replace in spending.

**Net worth tracking:** Calculate once per month: (assets) − (liabilities) = net worth. Track the direction, not just the number. A rising number — even slowly — means the system is working. A flat or declining number with rising income means spending is consuming the gains. The number reveals what the story cannot.

## The Wealth Sequence

Follow this order. Do not skip steps, do not optimize out of sequence. The sequence is optimized for tax efficiency, risk management, and compound growth.

**Step 1: Emergency fund**
3–6 months of essential living expenses in a high-yield savings account. Accessible within 24–48 hours. Not in the market. This fund is insurance against the situations that would otherwise force you into high-interest debt or premature investment liquidation. Build this before investing anything beyond employer match.

**Step 2: Employer 401(k) match**
If your employer matches 401(k) contributions, contribute at minimum the amount that captures the full match. This is a 50–100% guaranteed, immediate return on investment — there is no better return available. Skipping it is leaving free money on the table.

**Step 3: High-interest debt payoff**
Any debt above approximately 6–7% annualized interest rate should be paid off before additional investment. The guaranteed return from eliminating 20% credit card debt exceeds the expected market return. Below 5–6% interest (mortgages, many student loans), the math favors investing rather than accelerated payoff.

**Debt payoff method:** Two approaches — the avalanche (pay off highest interest rate first: mathematically optimal) and the snowball (pay off smallest balance first: psychologically optimal). The avalanche saves more money. The snowball produces faster wins that sustain motivation. If you need motivation to continue, use the snowball. If your systems are solid, use the avalanche.

**Step 4: Health Savings Account (HSA) — if available**
The HSA is the most tax-advantaged account available in the US: contributions are pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Triple tax advantage. After age 65, withdrawals for any purpose are taxed as ordinary income — making it effectively a second traditional IRA. Max it. Let the balance grow invested; pay current medical expenses out of pocket when possible to let the account compound.

**Step 5: Roth IRA (or Traditional IRA)**
$7,000/year (2024 limit; increases with inflation). Roth contributions are post-tax; growth and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. See the Roth vs. Traditional decision framework below.

**Step 6: Max the 401(k)**
$23,000/year (2024 limit). Pre-tax contributions reduce current taxable income and grow tax-deferred. The compound interest runs on the pre-tax dollar — a meaningful advantage over time.

**Step 7: Taxable brokerage account**
Once tax-advantaged accounts are maxed, invest additional savings in a taxable brokerage account. Use the same index fund approach; be aware of tax-loss harvesting opportunities (selling positions at a loss to offset capital gains).

## Asset Classes: The Evidence

**Index funds over stock-picking:**
- Approximately 90% of active fund managers underperform their benchmark index over 20-year periods, after fees
- The few who outperform are difficult to identify in advance and change over time
- Index funds provide diversification, low cost (expense ratios of 0.03–0.10% vs. 0.5–1.5%+ for active funds), and tax efficiency

**The three-fund portfolio:**
1. **Total US stock market** (e.g., VTI): ownership of approximately 4,000 US companies across all market caps
2. **Total international stock market** (e.g., VXUS): ownership of international developed and emerging markets
3. **Bond index** (e.g., BND): government and corporate bonds; stabilizes the portfolio and provides rebalancing opportunity

Typical allocations: 60/30/10 (US/International/Bonds) for aggressive; 50/30/20 for moderate; 40/30/30 for conservative. Age toward more bonds as retirement approaches. Adjust to your actual risk tolerance, not the theoretical one.

**The rebalancing rule:** Once per year (or when any asset class drifts more than 5% from target), rebalance back to target allocation by selling the overweight assets and buying the underweight ones. This mechanically enforces "buy low, sell high" and maintains risk exposure at the intended level.

**Real estate:**
Makes sense when: you can stay in an area for 7+ years (long enough to recoup transaction costs), the rent-vs.-buy math favors buying at local price levels, and you have adequate liquidity after the down payment. Does not automatically build wealth — it is a leveraged investment with liquidity constraints, maintenance costs, and concentration risk. Run the numbers; don't assume.

## Tax Strategy

**Roth vs. Traditional:**
- Pay taxes now (Roth) if you expect to be in a higher tax bracket at withdrawal than you are today
- Defer taxes (Traditional) if you expect to be in a lower bracket at withdrawal
- As a general rule: Roth is favored when you're in a low-to-mid bracket (under $100k single, under $200k married); Traditional is favored in high-income years

**Tax-loss harvesting:**
Selling a position at a loss to realize a capital loss, which offsets capital gains and reduces taxable income by up to $3,000/year against ordinary income. The key: buy a similar (not identical) position within 30 days to maintain market exposure without triggering wash-sale rules. Most automated investing platforms (Betterment, Wealthfront) do this automatically.

**Tax-advantaged account sequencing:** Where you hold each investment matters, not just what you hold. The sequence:
- Hold bonds and dividend-paying funds in tax-advantaged accounts (tax-deferred or tax-free)
- Hold growth stocks and broad index funds in taxable accounts (long-term capital gains treatment)
- REITs and other high-distribution assets belong in tax-advantaged accounts

**Business structure:**
If self-employed, a single-member LLC provides liability protection with minimal additional tax complexity. An S-Corp election (typically at ~$80k+ net self-employment income) can reduce self-employment tax by allowing reasonable salary + distribution structure. A solo 401(k) allows contributions up to $66,000/year (2024) as both employee and employer — far exceeding IRA limits. Consult a tax professional before implementing; the leverage is real.

## Insurance: Asymmetric Protection

Insurance is not an investment. It is ruin-condition prevention — the application of [antifragility.md](../antifragility.md) to finances. The purpose is not to win or even to break even on premiums. It is to prevent low-probability, high-magnitude events from permanently impairing your financial position.

**Disability insurance:** The most underinsured category. Your income is your largest financial asset — vastly exceeding the value of most people's investment portfolios, particularly early in a career. A 30-year-old who becomes disabled without disability coverage loses 30+ years of income. Long-term disability insurance replaces 60–70% of income if you become unable to work. If your employer provides it, understand the coverage. If not, get it. Look for: "own-occupation" definition of disability (not "any occupation"), 90-day elimination period, benefits to age 65.

**Health insurance:** The most common source of catastrophic personal financial events in the US. Never go uninsured for health, regardless of perceived youth or health.

**Life insurance:** Term life only if others depend on your income. 10–20x annual income in coverage for the period during which dependents rely on you. Whole life and other permanent insurance products are almost never the optimal financial tool — buy term and invest the difference. Exception: certain high-net-worth estate planning strategies using life insurance, which require professional guidance.

**Umbrella insurance:** Inexpensive ($200–400/year) coverage for liability claims that exceed home/auto policy limits. Becomes relevant once you have meaningful assets worth protecting.

**What not to insure:** Extended warranties on consumer electronics, flight insurance, credit card protection insurance. Any insurance where the maximum loss is manageable — insure against ruin, not inconvenience.

## Building Income

The wealth sequence assumes income. Growing income is as important as managing it.

**The negotiation leverage principle:** Your negotiating position is strongest at the moment of offer, weakest once you've accepted and started. Negotiate at the offer stage. Counteroffers are expected; not countering is leaving money on the table. Research market rates (Glassdoor, levels.fyi, LinkedIn Salary) before negotiating — know the market before making your case.

**The 70% rule for career moves:** If an opportunity pays 50% more but aligns 70% with your values and builds career capital, take it. If it pays 30% more but aligns 40% with values and builds no new capital, decline it. Money is not the only scarce resource in career decisions.

**Multiple income streams:** The vulnerability of single-income dependency is a financial risk. A second income stream — consulting, a side business, intellectual property, investment income — reduces the consequences of any single disruption. Start building the second stream while the first is stable; don't wait for necessity.

**The business asset threshold:** A business that requires your constant physical presence is a job you own. The financial leverage of entrepreneurship comes from building systems that generate revenue without requiring your presence. If you're self-employed, the question to keep asking: "What would need to be true for this to run without me?" The answer is the path to genuine financial leverage.

## The Psychology of Wealth

**The hedonic treadmill:** Human beings adapt to improved circumstances and return to approximately the same level of satisfaction. The house, the car, the raise — these produce a temporary increase in wellbeing before normalizing. The person chasing satisfaction through acquisition is on a treadmill that never stops. The solution is not to stop earning but to stop expecting consumption to produce lasting wellbeing.

**Lifestyle inflation:** The automatic expansion of spending to match income increases. This is the mechanism by which most income growth fails to produce proportional wealth growth. The default is to treat income increases as permission for lifestyle increases. The deliberate choice is to treat them as acceleration of the wealth-building sequence.

**The "enough" number:** Most people have never explicitly calculated how much wealth would provide genuine financial freedom — the point at which investment income covers essential living expenses without earned income. This is the Financial Independence (FI) number: (annual essential expenses) × 25 = the portfolio needed to sustainably fund those expenses at a 4% withdrawal rate. Calculate this number. It is almost certainly smaller than you imagine. Having a defined target changes the relationship to saving — from abstract virtue to concrete project.

**Loss aversion and investing:** Kahneman's research: losses feel approximately twice as painful as equivalent gains feel pleasurable. This cognitive bias causes investors to sell during downturns and buy during peaks — the exact opposite of sound investing. The antidote: automate contributions so emotions aren't in the loop during market volatility. Check your portfolio quarterly, not daily. The investor who checks daily is measurably more likely to make fear-based decisions than the investor who checks quarterly.

**Money buys time and options. That's what it's for.**
Financial freedom is not a dollar amount. It is the point at which your time is no longer primarily allocated by financial necessity — when you choose your work because it's meaningful, your location because it serves your life, your commitments because they reflect your values. Build toward that, not toward a number for its own sake.
