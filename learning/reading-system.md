# Reading System

Reading without retention is an expensive hobby. The goal is not to finish books. It is to change — to acquire mental models, frameworks, and insights that alter behavior and compounding thinking for years after the book is closed.

---

## Principles

1. **Read to change behavior, not to finish books.** A book that changes one thing permanently is worth more than ten books consumed and forgotten.
2. **It's okay to quit a book.** The sunk cost fallacy applies to reading. If a book is not delivering value by the halfway point, stop.
3. **Take notes in your own words.** Rephrasing forces understanding. Highlighting alone is passive indexing — it produces the feeling of engagement without the substance.
4. **The goal is permanent notes, not reference notes.** The measure of a reading practice is not the size of your notes folder. It is how often past reading shows up as insight in present thinking.

---

## The Forgetting Problem

Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve: without any active review, approximately 70% of new information is forgotten within 24 hours. Within a month, up to 80% is gone. This is the baseline condition for any reading practice that doesn't include deliberate retrieval.

This is not a memory failure — it is normal neural housekeeping. The brain retains information it encounters repeatedly and forgets information it encounters once. The reading system exists to defeat this default.

Everything that follows is the engineering solution to Ebbinghaus.

---

## Spaced Repetition

Retrieval at increasing intervals defeats the forgetting curve. The intervals:
- **24 hours** after reading: first review
- **1 week** later: second review
- **1 month** later: third review
- **6 months** later: fourth review

After four retrievals at these intervals, information moves into long-term memory with high retention probability.

**Implementation options:**
- **Anki** (free software): the most systematic spaced repetition tool. Ideal for specific facts, formulas, quotes, and definitions that need to be precisely remembered. The algorithm manages the review intervals automatically.
- **Manual queue:** a simple list of books read with review dates. On review, skim your notes and attempt to recall the core ideas before reading the notes. Less precise than Anki but effective.

**What goes into spaced repetition:** specific, quotable insights, framework names and their mechanics, counterintuitive findings that challenged your priors. Not summaries — specific pieces of knowledge.

## Active Recall During Reading

The biggest leverage point is during reading, not after.

**The pause-and-recite technique:** At the end of every chapter (or every major section), close the book. Without looking at the text, recite aloud or in writing what you just read — the main arguments, the key evidence, the central insight. Then open the book and check what you missed. The recitation attempt — even the failed one — is learning.

**Recognition vs. recall:** When you re-read a passage, your brain says "yes, I've seen this." That is recognition — the lowest form of retention. You can recognize something you cannot recall. Most reading produces recognition only. Deliberate recall practice converts recognition into genuine retention.

**Flashcard-while-reading:** Instead of highlighting, write questions on an index card or in a digital system: "What does Kahneman say about the difference between System 1 and System 2?" — not "Kahneman's dual-process theory" (that's a note, not a question). Questions force recall. Notes allow passive recognition.

---

## During Reading

- **Highlight sparingly** — only what you would actually quote to someone or act on. If you highlight half a book, you've highlighted nothing.
- **Write a one-line note in the margin when something connects to your life** — the connection between the book and your current situation is where the real value is. That connection is what makes the insight sticky.
- **Mark sections worth revisiting** with a distinct symbol (dog-ear, specific bookmark, star annotation). On finishing the book, go back to these before writing your synthesis.

---

## After Finishing: The Synthesis Note

Within 48 hours of finishing a book, write a synthesis note. This is not a summary — it is a dialogue between you and the book.

**The synthesis note template:**

1. **What does this book argue?** (One paragraph — force yourself to be this concise)
2. **What is the single most important idea?** (One sentence)
3. **Where do you agree?** (What has been confirmed or expanded)
4. **Where do you disagree or find the argument incomplete?** (Intellectual honesty about limits)
5. **How does it connect to the last three things you've read or thought about?** (The cross-domain connection is where insight lives)
6. **What changes in your operating system?** (One specific behavioral or conceptual update you will implement)
7. **What should you read next that this book points to?** (Most great books point to their intellectual ancestors in their bibliography)

The synthesis note has one function: to produce permanent notes, not reference notes.

## The Personal Knowledge System

The reading system is the mechanism by which external ideas become internal capability. Over time, the notes from your reading should not live in separate book-specific files — they should live in the playbook itself, integrated into the relevant section.

**The Zettelkasten distinction:**
- **Reference note:** "Kahneman distinguishes System 1 (fast, automatic, intuitive) from System 2 (slow, deliberate, effortful)." — This is what the book says.
- **Permanent note:** "My snap judgments in negotiation are System 1 — fast but pattern-matched to surface features. The counterpart has the same System 1 running. Both of us are more rational when we slow the process down deliberately, which is why silence and explicit pauses are high-leverage in hard conversations." — This is what I think.

The permanent note is the one that belongs in the playbook. The reference note is search-engine material. Build the permanent notes.

Over time, the playbook is not a collection of frameworks someone else developed. It is the crystallization of your own thinking, developed in dialogue with the best thinkers you've encountered. That is the actual goal of the reading practice.

## What to Read

**Primary:** Books that build durable mental models. Not news, not trending takes, not content that will be irrelevant in two years.

**Mix:** ~60% nonfiction, ~40% fiction. Fiction is not a leisure indulgence — it builds the capacity for inhabiting perspectives other than your own. The reader of literary fiction consistently outperforms non-readers on theory of mind tests and empathic accuracy.

**Bias toward classics and books >5 years old.** The filter of time is ruthless. A book that has been read and discussed for 20 years has survived because it contains something real. A book published last month has not been tested. Trust the time filter.

**The three-stack method:**
- **Reading stack:** What you are actively reading (one to three books at most)
- **Up-next stack:** Books queued for immediate next (researched and decided)
- **Someday stack:** Everything else — aspirational but not yet committed

**Source-of-recommendation hierarchy:**
1. Great books recommend great books in their bibliography — work backward through the intellectual genealogy of ideas you care about
2. Trusted people in your specific domain > generic bestseller lists
3. Bestseller lists select for marketing and zeitgeist more than enduring quality

**The "one deep read leads to three" principle:** Every serious book, if engaged with fully, points to at least three books worth reading more. The reading practice is self-expanding.

---

## Retention Review

- **Re-read great books.** The second read after living more life is a qualitatively different experience — you'll catch everything you weren't yet ready to receive the first time. Schedule deliberate re-reads of your highest-value books.
- **Discuss books with someone.** Explaining your synthesis forces gaps to surface. Gaps are where learning happens.
- **Return to your notes before starting a new book in the same subject area.** You are not starting fresh — you are building on the existing structure. The connection between what you already know and what you're about to read is the prime territory for insight.
