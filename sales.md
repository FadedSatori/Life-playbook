# Sales

Every outcome that depends on another person saying yes runs through sales. A job offer, a funded project, a partnership, a hire, a donation, a date, a team that follows your plan. If you cannot move people toward a decision that serves them and you, your ideas remain private.

Sales has a bad reputation because of how it is done badly: pressure, tricks, and deception. Done well, it is the discipline of understanding a problem deeply, offering a real solution, and helping someone decide with clarity. It is service with structure. The ethical frame is not optional. See the line drawn in [negotiation.md](negotiation.md) and [power-dynamics.md](power-dynamics.md).

---

## The Core Principle

**People buy to solve a problem or reach a goal, not to acquire your product.**

The product is the vehicle. The change in their situation is the destination. The salesperson who talks about features sells a vehicle. The one who understands the destination sells the trip.

Everything below follows from that: find the problem, size it, connect your solution to it, and let the person reach the decision themselves.

## The Sequence

### 1. Qualify: Is this a real fit?
Time is finite. Spend it where a real problem, real authority, and real ability to act exist. The classic qualification questions:
- **Need:** Is there a genuine problem or goal here?
- **Authority:** Does this person decide, or influence the decision? Who else is involved?
- **Budget/means:** Can they act on it?
- **Timing:** Is there a reason to move now, or at all?

A polite "no fit" early saves both sides weeks. Qualifying out is a skill, not a failure.

### 2. Discover: Ask before you tell
Discovery is where sales is won. Spend most of the first conversation asking and listening.

**The shape of good discovery:**
- **Situation:** How do things work today?
- **Problem:** Where does it break, cost, or frustrate?
- **Implication:** What does that problem cost them: in money, time, risk, or stress? What happens if it continues?
- **Need-payoff:** What would solving it be worth? Let *them* say it.

This is the SPIN structure (Neil Rackham's research on large-sale effectiveness). The point of implication questions is not to apply pressure. It is to make the real cost of inaction visible, so the decision is made on the true stakes.

Use the tools from [negotiation.md](negotiation.md): mirroring to draw out more, labeling to name emotion, calibrated "how" and "what" questions to guide without pushing. Listening is the lever. See [communication.md](communication.md).

**Rule:** If you are talking more than 40% of the time in discovery, you are pitching, not discovering.

### 3. Present: Connect, don't recite
Tie your solution directly to what they told you. Use their words. Lead with the outcome, then how you deliver it. Show evidence: results from similar clients, concrete numbers, a demonstration. Prove it, do not just claim it.

Use story to carry the point (see the narrative arc in [tactics/public-speaking.md](tactics/public-speaking.md)): a customer like them, a problem like theirs, the change that followed.

### 4. Handle objections: Treat them as information
An objection is not an attack. It is the buyer showing you what stands between them and yes. Welcome it.

**The protocol:**
1. **Listen fully.** Do not interrupt or rebut early.
2. **Acknowledge.** "That's a fair concern."
3. **Clarify.** "Help me understand what's behind that." The stated objection is often not the real one.
4. **Isolate.** "If we solved that, is there anything else holding you back?" Surfaces hidden objections.
5. **Respond with substance.** Evidence, a reframe, or an honest concession.
6. **Confirm.** "Does that address it?"

**Common real objections beneath the stated ones:**
- *"Too expensive"* often means unclear value, no budget authority, or a fear of being wrong.
- *"Need to think about it"* often means an unresolved concern they have not voiced. Ask what, specifically, they want to think through.
- *"Not the right time"* may be true, or may mean the problem is not painful enough yet. Return to implication.

### 5. Close: Ask, then be quiet
Most deals die from not being asked. Closing is a natural next step in a conversation that has already earned it, not a trick.

- **Direct ask:** "Based on what we've covered, does it make sense to move forward?"
- **Next-step close:** Agree on a specific, dated next action. Never leave "I'll get back to you" with no date.
- **Assumptive framing, honestly used:** "The next step is X. Does Tuesday work to start?" only when the buyer has signaled readiness.

After the ask, stop talking. The silence tool from [negotiation.md](negotiation.md) applies fully. The person who speaks first usually concedes.

**Accept a clear no gracefully.** A clean "no" is useful information and preserves the relationship. A pushed "no" burns it. Ask what would have to be true for a yes, and leave the door open.

### 6. Follow through: The sale starts at yes
Deliver more than you promised. The compound effect of reliability is referrals, repeat business, and reputation, which is the cheapest and strongest sales engine there is. See [networks.md](networks.md).

## The Psychology Underneath

- **Reduce risk, not just add value.** Loss aversion (Kahneman and Tversky) means people weigh potential loss more heavily than equal gain. Guarantees, pilots, references, and clear scope reduce the felt risk of saying yes.
- **Make the decision easy.** Fewer options, a clear recommendation, a simple next step. Choice overload stalls action.
- **Trust precedes everything.** People buy from those they trust. Trust = credibility + reliability + intimacy (candor) − self-interest (Maister's trust equation). The fastest way to lower perceived self-interest is to be willing to tell a buyer when you are *not* the right fit.
- **Cialdini's principles** (see [power-dynamics.md](power-dynamics.md)) are real forces. Use them honestly: genuine social proof, real scarcity, authentic reciprocity. Manufactured versions erode trust and invite regret, refunds, and reputation damage.

## Selling Ideas, Selling Yourself

The same sequence runs everywhere:

- **Job interviews:** Discovery (what problem does this team have?), then connect your record to *their* problem. Not a list of your accomplishments.
- **Fundraising and pitching:** Qualify the investor, make the stakes of the problem vivid, show traction as proof, ask clearly.
- **Leading a team:** You are selling a direction. Understand what each person cares about, connect the mission to it, handle the concerns honestly. See [leadership.md](leadership.md).
- **Relationships and friendship:** The healthy version is simply honest, curious, direct communication. The unhealthy version is manipulation. See [dark-psychology.md](dark-psychology.md) for where the line breaks.

## Building the Skill

Sales is learned by volume with feedback, like any skill.

1. **Make the asks.** Set a weekly quota of real conversations. Fear shrinks with exposure. See [courage.md](courage.md). Rejection is training load. Each "no" is a rep.
2. **Record and review.** Listen to your own calls or review notes. Find where you talked too much, skipped discovery, or flinched at the ask.
3. **Study the best.** Rackham (*SPIN Selling*), Voss (*Never Split the Difference*), Cialdini (*Influence*), Maister (*The Trusted Advisor*), Ziglar and Hopkins for foundations. Read, then practice what you read within 48 hours.
4. **Keep a pipeline.** Track every opportunity, its stage, and next action. What gets measured gets managed.
5. **Debrief every loss.** Why did it fall through? Was it fit, timing, price, trust, or my execution? Use the debrief protocol from [failure.md](failure.md).

## The Ethical Line

Sell only what you believe serves the buyer. If you do not believe in it, the work is deception with extra steps and the long-game math turns against you. Do not manufacture urgency, hide material terms, exploit fear, or pressure someone past their own judgment. The reputation you build compounds for decades. The one you burn does not return.

Persuasion that survives full transparency, where the buyer would still agree if they saw exactly what you were doing and why, is the standard.
