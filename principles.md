# Core Principles

The foundation everything else is built on. When in doubt, return here. These are not aspirations. They are load-bearing walls. Remove one and the structure collapses.

---

## Values

1. **Integrity over comfort.** Do what you said you'd do, especially when it's inconvenient.
2. **Long game thinking.** Optimize for who you're becoming, not how you feel right now.
3. **Ownership.** No excuses, no blame. You are the author of your life.
4. **Depth over breadth.** A few things done with full commitment beat many things done halfway.
5. **Presence.** Where you are, be all there.

---

## Full Unpacking of Each Value

### 1. Integrity Over Comfort

**What it means in practice.** Your word is your bond before it costs you anything. The test is never when it's convenient — it's when keeping your word means losing sleep, losing money, or losing face. Every time you honor a commitment under pressure, you become more trustworthy to the most important audience: yourself. Every time you fold, you train yourself that your word is negotiable.

**What it costs.** Real integrity costs comfort constantly. You will miss parties because you said you'd train. You will deliver hard truths that people don't want to hear. You will walk away from deals that feel good but smell wrong. You will be seen as rigid by people who have made peace with their own compromises. This is the price. Pay it.

**What it looks like under pressure.** The moment arrives and every rationalization is available. "This once." "No one will know." "The circumstances changed." Integrity is the muscle that refuses those stories. It is not the absence of temptation — it is action in spite of it.

**Edge cases.** Integrity does not mean rigidity. If you made a commitment with incomplete information and honoring it would cause serious harm, you renegotiate openly — not silently, not by disappearing. The renegotiation itself must be done with integrity: early, direct, accountable. "I said I'd do X. I can't. Here's why. Here's what I can do instead."

**What it excludes.** Performative virtue. Signaling without substance. Telling people what they want to hear to avoid conflict. Agreeing to things you already know you won't do. Making promises in the heat of enthusiasm with no plan to keep them.

**Violation signals.**
- You've been avoiding a specific person or conversation.
- There is a gap between what you told someone and what you're actually doing.
- You catch yourself constructing elaborate explanations for why you can't do what you said you'd do.
- You feel relief when someone releases you from a commitment you should have honored.

**Historical embodiment.** Cato the Younger (95–46 BC) refused to bend to Caesar even when surrender would have saved his life. He died by his own hand rather than live in a Rome that had abandoned the Republic. His integrity was total and it cost him everything. The lesson is not to die for principle — it is to understand that principle, fully held, has a price. Know the price before you claim the principle.

### 2. Long Game Thinking

**What it means in practice.** Every choice is a vote for the person you are becoming. The question is never "what do I want right now?" — it is "what do I want to have chosen in ten years?" Present you is a steward of future you. Treat that stewardship seriously.

**What it costs.** Delayed gratification, constantly. Watching others take shortcuts that appear to work. Investing in skills and relationships with no near-term payoff. Accepting that compounding is invisible until it is suddenly overwhelming. The cost is not suffering — it is patience, which is harder.

**What it looks like under pressure.** Short-term urgency is always more visceral than long-term importance. Under pressure, the long game thinker asks: will this matter in five years? If yes, how should I act now to get the outcome I want then? If no, why am I letting it consume me?

**Edge cases.** Long game thinking is not an excuse to defer everything forever. There are time-sensitive moves. The question is not "is this urgent?" but "which urgency is real?" Some windows close. The long game thinker must distinguish between tactical urgency (real) and emotional urgency (usually manufactured).

**What it excludes.** Chasing what is trending. Making decisions based on how you'll look this month. Sacrificing compounding relationships for transactional wins. Burning your reputation for a quick score.

**Violation signals.**
- You are making decisions to manage how you feel right now rather than to build what you want.
- You are choosing the path of least resistance and calling it strategy.
- Your short-term choices are narrowing your future options rather than expanding them.
- You are prioritizing being liked over being respected.

**Historical embodiment.** Jeff Bezos built Amazon for a decade before it was profitable, absorbing ridicule and investor pressure because he could see the compounding clearly. Warren Buffett turned down deal after deal through the dot-com bubble — not because he was afraid, but because he understood that the game being played around him was not the game he was playing. Long game thinking requires the psychological security to be wrong in the short term. Most people can't hold that position.

### 3. Ownership

**What it means in practice.** You are the cause, not the effect. Every outcome in your life has your fingerprints on it — your decisions, your inactions, your tolerances, your blind spots. This is not about blame. It is about power. The person who owns their outcomes can change them. The person who assigns blame to circumstance cannot.

**What it costs.** It costs the comfort of victimhood. Blame is a painkiller — it explains suffering without requiring action. Ownership removes that escape. It also costs ego: admitting that most of your problems are self-generated is humbling. The upside is total: agency is the most valuable thing you can have.

**What it looks like under pressure.** The pressure creates perfect conditions for blame: the market, the team, the bad luck. The owner scans for what was in their control and failed, what they could have known earlier, what they could do differently now. Then they act on that scan. The blame story is not forbidden — it's just irrelevant to getting out.

**Edge cases.** Ownership does not mean absorbing guilt for things genuinely outside your control. It means owning your response to those things. The accident wasn't your fault. How you recover from it is yours entirely.

**What it excludes.** Learned helplessness. Waiting to be rescued. Building narratives about why you can't. Finding sophisticated explanations for stasis. Outsourcing your development to other people's approval.

**Violation signals.**
- Your explanations for failures always center on external factors.
- You are waiting for someone else to give you permission to move.
- You are collecting evidence that the system is stacked against you rather than looking for the move.
- You talk about problems without ever reaching action.

**Historical embodiment.** Viktor Frankl survived Auschwitz by discovering that even in conditions of total external control, the inner response remained his. He wrote *Man's Search for Meaning* to document the insight: the last human freedom is the choice of attitude in any given circumstance. Ownership is not optimism. It is the refusal to surrender authorship of your inner life regardless of external conditions.

### 4. Depth Over Breadth

**What it means in practice.** Mastery requires repetition at a level that feels unreasonable to dilettantes. You will not be exceptional at many things. You can be exceptional at a few. Choose what you will go deep on and protect those choices from the constant pull of novelty and optionality.

**What it costs.** It costs options. Going deep on something means not going deep on something else. This creates genuine anxiety in a culture that celebrates versatility and "keeping doors open." The depth practitioner closes doors deliberately. This is not loss — it is focus made visible.

**What it looks like under pressure.** Under pressure, breadth feels like security — more skills, more options, more fallbacks. The depth practitioner resists this. A few genuinely excellent capabilities produce more actual security than a wide shallow portfolio of average ones.

**Edge cases.** Some breadth is required. You need functional literacy across many domains to navigate modern life. The principle is not "ignore everything except one thing" — it is "don't let functional breadth crowd out exceptional depth in the things that matter most to you."

**What it excludes.** Dabbling. Collecting hobbies without advancing in any of them. Switching focus the moment difficulty appears, which is usually right before the breakthrough. Status games that require visible versatility. The compulsion to be interesting rather than excellent.

**Violation signals.**
- You are starting many things and finishing few.
- Your attention to your primary craft has been diluted by secondary projects that excite you but don't advance the mission.
- You are consuming content about a skill rather than practicing it.
- You have been at the same plateau for months with no deliberate practice to break it.

**Historical embodiment.** Bobby Fischer gave up everything — social development, formal education, conventional adolescence — to go so deep into chess that the game itself was transformed. His dedication looked pathological from outside. From inside it was the only rational response to having found the thing worth mastering. You do not need his extremity. But you need his clarity about what is worth going deep on.

### 5. Presence

**What it means in practice.** The present moment is the only place where anything actually happens. Planning occurs in the present. Action occurs in the present. Connection occurs in the present. The habit of being elsewhere — in memory, anticipation, distraction — is the habit of missing your life while technically attending it.

**What it costs.** It costs the avoidance function of mental absence. Presence requires sitting with discomfort rather than escaping into plans or regrets. It requires actually feeling what is happening rather than processing it from a safe narrative distance. This is harder than it sounds.

**What it looks like under pressure.** Under threat, the mind wants to race — to rehearse every possible disaster, to replay every mistake. Presence is not the suppression of that response. It is the return, again and again, to what is actually happening right now, what information is actually available, what action is actually possible.

**Edge cases.** Presence does not mean refusing to plan or refusing to learn from the past. It means that planning and reflection happen deliberately, in their time, rather than as background noise that drowns out the present.

**What it excludes.** Compulsive phone checking. Half-attention in conversations. Planning dinner during a meeting. Being physically in one place while mentally somewhere else and calling it multitasking.

**Violation signals.**
- You are in a conversation and composing your response rather than listening.
- You pick up your phone without knowing why.
- You finish a meal and don't remember tasting it.
- The people in the room with you consistently have to repeat themselves.

**Historical embodiment.** Jim Harrison, the novelist, described watching friends die having spent their lives rushing through experiences to get to the next ones. The meditative traditions all converge on the same observation: most human suffering is generated not by present circumstances but by mental absence from them. Marcus Aurelius returned to this point obsessively in the *Meditations* — "nowhere can man find a quieter or more untroubled retreat than in his own soul." The retreat is available right now, not later.

## The Hierarchy When Values Conflict

Values conflict. The pretense that they don't is naive. When they do, the hierarchy below resolves it.

**Integrity beats all others.** If long game thinking suggests a compromise of integrity ("just this once, it will pay off"), integrity wins. The long game that requires dishonesty is already lost. No value built on a broken foundation holds.

**Ownership beats presence.** If presence in this moment means avoiding an uncomfortable conversation you own, ownership wins. Being here without being accountable is just comfortable hiding.

**Depth beats presence in the short term.** Presence is real-time. Depth requires the ability to defer immediate experience for future mastery — practicing when you don't want to, staying with a problem past the point where it's enjoyable. Depth wins the scheduling conflict.

**Long game beats depth when the whole game is at stake.** If pursuing depth in one area is destroying your health, primary relationships, or financial stability, the long game perspective overrides the focus imperative. Mastery requires a viable player.

The practical rule: **Integrity > Ownership > Long Game > Depth > Presence.** This hierarchy is not permanent — it applies to direct conflicts. In normal operation, all five run in parallel.

## Operating Code

Ten behavioral rules that flow directly from the values. These are not suggestions.

1. **Never make a commitment you don't intend to keep.** Better to say no upfront than yes with no follow-through. Your yes must mean yes.

2. **Finish what you start.** Not every project — but the things you've told yourself or others you'll complete. Before starting something new, ask what's unfinished.

3. **Be the same person in every room.** What you say about someone behind their back, say to their face. How you act when being observed, act when no one sees.

4. **Do the hard conversation early.** Every difficult conversation deferred costs twice what it would have cost immediately. Do it now.

5. **Protect your primary craft from everything.** Block the time. Guard the hours. Let your best energy go to the work that matters most before the world gets to it.

6. **Audit your inputs quarterly.** What you consume — news, content, conversation — shapes your thinking. Every 90 days, cut what is not advancing your understanding or capability.

7. **Never optimize for looking good in the short term at the expense of being good in the long term.** The two paths diverge constantly. Choose the path that builds the asset.

8. **Own the mistake immediately.** When you are wrong, say so quickly and clearly. The coverup always costs more than the error.

9. **Do what you said you'd do before doing what you feel like doing.** Commitments have priority. Moods are not scheduling systems.

10. **Go where you are most useful, not most comfortable.** Comfort seeking is the slow erosion of capability. Find the edge of your competence and work there.

11. **Give your full attention or be absent.** Partial presence is disrespect with plausible deniability. Show up fully or don't show up.

12. **Let your results speak longer than your words.** Demonstrate before claiming. Ship before announcing. Build before promoting.

13. **Keep a short list of things you're going deep on. Review it weekly.** If something not on the list is consuming your best energy, either add it or stop it.

14. **When you're wrong about a person, update fast.** The belief rule applies to yourself too. Don't preserve a story about someone past the evidence.

15. **Never spend an hour that you couldn't defend.** Not every hour must be productive. Rest is productive. The test is intentionality — can you say what you were choosing and why?

## The Foundation Under Everything

Why these five values and not others?

These five protect against the most reliable forms of self-destruction.

**Integrity** protects against the erosion of trust — including self-trust. The person who has trained themselves that their word is conditional becomes unreliable to themselves. They cannot rely on their own commitments, cannot build toward long-horizon goals, cannot be counted on by others. Integrity is the prerequisite for every other value functioning.

**Long game thinking** protects against impulsivity — the single most reliable destroyer of accumulated advantage. Fortunes, reputations, relationships, and bodies are most commonly ruined not by catastrophe but by a thousand short-term decisions that felt rational in the moment. The long game lens is the corrective.

**Ownership** protects against helplessness. The external locus of control is the most comfortable prison. Everything is someone else's fault; therefore nothing can be changed. Ownership is the only exit. It is not comfortable. It is the only path.

**Depth over breadth** protects against the dilettante trap — doing many things well enough to seem capable while achieving nothing at a level that actually matters. In a world of superficial generalists, depth is the scarcest and most valuable resource. It also protects against the particular modern pathology of consuming rather than producing, curating rather than creating.

**Presence** protects against the lived experience of never actually being where you are — the peculiar modern condition of physical attendance with mental absence. A life of presence is a life actually experienced, not processed at a remove.

Taken together, these five make a person reliable, strategic, accountable, excellent, and actually alive to their experience. The alternative is a version of yourself that drifts, reacts, blames, skims, and sleepwalks. That version is always available. These principles are the active resistance to it.

## Decision Filter

Before committing to anything significant, ask:
- Does this align with my values?
- Would I be proud of this in 10 years?
- Am I saying yes out of genuine desire, or out of fear/guilt/ego?

If two of three answers are no, decline.

**Extended filter for major decisions** (career moves, relationships, large financial commitments):

- What does the best version of this outcome look like in five years?
- What does the worst version look like, and can I survive it?
- Who else will be affected, and have I actually considered them?
- What am I not seeing because I want this to be true?
- If I had to explain this decision to my most respected mentor, could I do it without feeling the need to justify rather than explain?
- Is this expanding my future options or collapsing them?

**The reversal test.** If you're considering adding something, ask: if this thing already existed in my life and someone proposed removing it, would I fight to keep it? If no, don't add it.

## Non-Negotiables

Things that are not up for debate regardless of circumstance:
- Sleep 7–8 hours
- Move every day
- Keep your word
- Protect deep work time

**The logic of non-negotiables.** The negotiable thing is the thing that gets negotiated away. Every exception creates the template for the next exception. The non-negotiable removes the decision from the arena entirely. You are not deciding each day whether to sleep enough or move — those are closed questions. The cognitive load drops to zero. The energy goes elsewhere.

**What to do when you fail a non-negotiable.** Note it. Don't dramatize it. Return to the standard immediately. One missed day is a miss; three missed days is a new pattern. Patterns are what you're managing.

**Adding non-negotiables.** Before making something non-negotiable, test it for 30 days as a strong preference. If you can identify the mechanism by which it improves your life, and if skipping it produces measurable degradation, promote it. Don't inflate the non-negotiables list — the power comes from its shortness.
