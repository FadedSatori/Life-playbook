# Mentorship

Every tradition that produced masters ran on mentorship. The guild had apprentice, journeyman, and master. The dojo had sensei and student. The legion had centurions who trained recruits. Special operations units pass skill through instructors who have done the work. The Stoics wrote to each other. Socrates taught Plato, Plato taught Aristotle, and Aristotle taught Alexander.

Books transmit information. Mentors transmit judgment, standards, shortcuts, and a model of who you can become. A mentor compresses years of error into a conversation. Few things in this playbook have a higher return.

Mentorship has two sides: getting one and becoming one. A complete person does both, at the same time, throughout life.

---

## Why Mentors Matter

- **They shorten the learning curve.** Anders Ericsson's research on expert performance found that deliberate practice with expert guidance and feedback is a defining feature of how experts develop. A good coach sees what you cannot see about yourself and designs the next rung. See [learning/skill-building.md](learning/skill-building.md).
- **They calibrate you.** A person ahead of you shows what "good" looks like, resets your sense of what's possible, and tells you the truth about your work.
- **They open doors.** Reputation transfer and introductions. See [networks.md](networks.md).
- **They model character.** You absorb how someone handles pressure, failure, money, and people. This often matters more than any single piece of advice.
- **Bloom's 2 sigma finding** (1984) reported that one-on-one tutoring produced learning outcomes about two standard deviations above classroom instruction. Later research suggests the true effect is smaller, but the direction is consistent: personalized, feedback-rich instruction beats broadcast teaching.

## Mentors, Sponsors, Coaches, and Peers

They differ. You want a mix.

- **Mentor:** Advises and shares experience. Longer-term, relational. Teaches you how to think about the path.
- **Coach:** Improves a specific performance or skill through feedback and practice.
- **Sponsor:** Uses their influence to advocate for you and open opportunities (Sylvia Ann Hewlett's distinction). Mentors give advice; sponsors put their reputation behind you. Sponsorship is earned by performance and trust.
- **Peer mentors:** People at your level who exchange honest feedback and support. Often the most durable relationships.
- **Teacher/author at a distance:** Someone you learn from through their work. Valuable, not a substitute for a relationship.

Build a **personal board of advisors**: a few people with different strengths, such as a craft master, a strategist, a financial or business mind, a person of character and wisdom, and a few peers. No one person can be everything.

## Finding a Mentor

**1. Become worth mentoring first.** Mentors choose students, not the reverse. Do real work. Have specific questions. Show initiative and evidence of effort. Before asking anyone for time, make yourself someone whose success would reflect well on them.

**2. Know what you need.** "Will you be my mentor?" is a poor opening. Better: "I'm working on X. You've done it. Could I ask you three questions?" Clarity lowers the cost of saying yes.

**3. Look near before far.** Teachers, bosses, senior colleagues, coaches, community and faith leaders, family friends, alumni. The best mentors often sit just a few steps ahead of you and are accessible.

**4. Start small and earn the relationship.** One thoughtful question. A specific request for 15–20 minutes. Show up prepared, respect the time, and follow up.

**5. Give value.** Share a relevant article, a connection, a useful observation, or help with a project. Think about what you can offer a more experienced person: energy, a fresh view, labor on something they care about, genuine gratitude, and results.

**6. Close the loop.** The single most reliable way to keep a mentor: do something with the advice and report back. "You suggested X. I tried it. Here's what happened." Mentors are motivated by seeing their advice take root. Nothing discourages them more than advice ignored without word.

**7. Let it grow organically.** Formal asking can come after a relationship exists. Many of the best mentoring relationships are never named.

**Approach etiquette:**
- Be brief and specific in writing. Show you've done your homework.
- Make a "yes" easy: concise ask, flexible timing, an easy way out.
- Don't expect free ongoing labor from a busy person; pay in appreciation, results, and reciprocity, or pay in money for coaching where appropriate.
- Respect "no" or silence; try again later with something new, or move on.
- Never drain or flatter; be sincere.

## Being a Good Mentee

1. **Prepare.** Arrive with questions, context, and a clear topic. Send a short agenda.
2. **Be coachable.** Ask for the truth and be able to hear it. If you argue with every critique, you'll stop receiving it. Defensiveness is the end of feedback. See [failure.md](failure.md).
3. **Do the work between conversations.** Action is the currency.
4. **Take notes and review them.** See [memory-systems.md](memory-systems.md).
5. **Own your decisions.** A mentor gives input; you decide and bear the consequences. Don't outsource your judgment or blame the mentor.
6. **Be discreet.** Honor confidences.
7. **Express gratitude** specifically. Tell them what changed because of them.
8. **Take multiple perspectives.** Different mentors may disagree. That's useful. Weigh their reasoning rather than defer to one.
9. **Outgrow gracefully.** Relationships evolve. Move from student toward peer, and keep the friendship.
10. **Pass it on.** Teach what you've learned to those behind you. This is the obligation attached to every gift.

## Becoming a Mentor

You do not need to be at the top to help someone a few steps behind. You need to have walked part of the road and be willing to tell the truth.

### Principles
1. **Listen first.** Understand their situation, goals, and constraints before advising. Most bad mentoring is advice given to a problem not yet understood.
2. **Ask more than you tell.** Questions help people find their own answers, which stick better. "What have you tried? What do you think is going on? What would you do if you weren't afraid?" See [negotiation.md](negotiation.md) and [rhetoric.md](rhetoric.md) on Socratic questions.
3. **Share your failures, not only your wins.** Vulnerability makes the guidance credible and humane. Your mistakes are the most useful part of your experience.
4. **Tell the truth, kindly.** Praise what is good and specific; name what needs work plainly. Use the Situation-Behavior-Impact structure from [leadership.md](leadership.md). Flattery is a disservice.
5. **Hold a high standard.** Believe they can reach it. Expecting more than they expect of themselves is a form of respect. This is the "wise feedback" idea in research on feedback: communicating high standards together with assurance that the person can meet them.
6. **Challenge and support.** Support without challenge breeds comfort; challenge without support breeds fear. Balance both.
7. **Respect their autonomy.** Offer perspective; don't dictate. Their life, their decisions.
8. **Give opportunity.** Stretch assignments, introductions, a seat at the table, public credit.
9. **Set expectations.** How often you'll meet, what's expected, how long, how to disagree. Clarity prevents drift and disappointment.
10. **Keep confidences.** Trust is the substrate.
11. **Know your limits.** Refer them to others when you lack expertise. Mentors who bluff do harm.
12. **Know when to end.** Good mentorships often evolve into friendships. Some run their course. Close with gratitude.

### Common mentor mistakes
- Cloning: pushing your path as the only path.
- Advising from outdated experience in a changed landscape. Say so.
- Taking over the problem rather than building the person.
- Using the mentee for status or labor.
- Withholding hard truths to be liked.
- Becoming the hero of every story.

### Mentoring frameworks
**GROW:** Goal (what do you want?), Reality (where are you now?), Options (what could you do?), Will (what will you do, by when?). A simple coaching conversation structure.

**Shu-Ha-Ri** (from Japanese martial arts): *Shu* (obey): learn the form exactly as taught. *Ha* (break): once mastered, depart from and adapt the form. *Ri* (leave): transcend the form and create your own. Good mentors teach according to the stage: strict guidance for the beginner, more questions and freedom as they advance. See [martial-arts.md](martial-arts.md), [warrior-codes.md](warrior-codes.md).

## Formal Apprenticeship Models Worth Borrowing

- **The guild model:** Long structured apprenticeship with graduated responsibility, real work, a masterpiece to prove readiness. The principle: learning by doing real work under a master's eye.
- **Special operations selection and training:** Mentorship through high standards and shared hardship, with senior operators teaching junior ones. Standards are not lowered; support is high.
- **The residency model in medicine:** "See one, do one, teach one." Observe, perform with supervision, then teach. Teaching consolidates mastery.
- **Pairs and triads:** Regular small groups of peers. Masterminds. Accountability and diversity of view.

## Reverse Mentoring and Learning From Everyone

The young know things you don't (new tools, culture, a fresh view), and everyone you meet knows something you don't. Ask. A humble posture toward learning keeps the mind young. Emerson's line from "Greatness" (1876), "Every man I meet is my master in some point, and in that I learn of him," is a good working rule. Pair this with [technology-leverage.md](technology-leverage.md): younger colleagues often lead on new tools.

## A Mentorship Plan

1. **Define your next two years' goals.** What do you most need to learn? See [purpose.md](purpose.md).
2. **List the 3–5 people** who have what you want to learn: craft, business, character, life.
3. **Identify what you can offer** each of them.
4. **Make a specific, small first contact** with one of them this month.
5. **Keep a log:** date, what was discussed, what you did, outcome. Review it quarterly.
6. **Identify one person you can mentor** now. Start.
7. **Review annually:** who has helped, who has drifted, who needs a thank-you, who needs you. See [routines/annual-review.md](routines/annual-review.md).
