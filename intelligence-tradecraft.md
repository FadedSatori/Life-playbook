# Intelligence Tradecraft

Intelligence is the discipline of knowing what is true, with calibrated confidence, in conditions where information is incomplete, contested, and sometimes deliberately false. That is nearly every important situation: a hire, an investment, a counterparty, a diagnosis, a rumor, a headline.

Professional intelligence services spent decades learning how smart people fool themselves and developed methods to reduce it. Those methods transfer directly to civilian life. This file extracts the ones that matter.

Everything here is about analyzing information you can legitimately obtain: public sources, your own observations, and what people tell you. It is a research and judgment skill, not a surveillance one. Respect privacy, law, and the line drawn in [warrior-codes.md](warrior-codes.md).

---

## Sun Tzu's Last Chapter

Sun Tzu closes *The Art of War* with spies: the greatest cost in war is ignorance, and the commander who knows the enemy's state while the enemy doesn't know his holds a decisive advantage. See [warfare.md](warfare.md). Boyd's OODA loop is an intelligence problem: whoever orients more accurately, faster, decides better.

The same advantage exists in business, negotiation, and personal decisions. Better information, better interpreted, is the cheapest edge there is.

## The Intelligence Cycle

The classic framing for turning questions into knowledge:

1. **Direction:** What decision is this for? What exactly do I need to know? A vague question yields vague research. Write the question and the decision it supports.
2. **Collection:** Gather information from multiple, independent sources.
3. **Processing:** Organize, translate, check, and record it.
4. **Analysis:** Judge what it means, how confident you are, and what's missing.
5. **Dissemination / action:** Communicate the conclusion clearly and use it, then feed what you learn back into the next question.

Most people skip step 1 and over-invest in step 2, collecting an ocean of data with no decision attached. Start from the decision.

## Evaluating Sources

Not all information is equal. Rate the source and the information separately.

**The Admiralty (NATO) system** rates:
- **Source reliability:** A (completely reliable) to F (cannot be judged).
- **Information credibility:** 1 (confirmed by other sources) to 6 (cannot be judged).

So "B2" is a usually reliable source delivering information that is probably true. The point is the habit: asking *how* you know something.

**Questions to ask of any source:**
- **Access:** Did they actually witness this, or are they repeating it? How many hands has it passed through?
- **Competence:** Do they have the expertise to judge it?
- **Motive:** What does the source gain if I believe this? Follow the incentives.
- **Track record:** Have they been right before? About this kind of thing?
- **Corroboration:** Do independent sources agree? "Independent" means they didn't derive from each other. Ten articles citing one report are one source.
- **Format of claim:** Specific and checkable, or vague and unfalsifiable?

**Lateral reading.** Research by Sam Wineburg and colleagues at Stanford found that professional fact-checkers outperformed historians and students by leaving a site quickly to check what others say about it, rather than reading deeply within it. Don't judge a source by its own About page and design. Open new tabs and see what independent parties say about who is behind it. Do this before investing time in the content.

**Primary over secondary.** Go to the original document, dataset, filing, or study rather than a summary of it. Summaries drop caveats and add spin.

**Check dates and context.** Real images and quotes get recirculated in false contexts. Verify when and where.

See [epistemic-self-defense.md](epistemic-self-defense.md) for the bias and manipulation side of this.

## Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH)

Richards Heuer, a veteran CIA analyst, wrote *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis* (1999), a short book that is also one of the best on thinking in general. His core finding: analysts, like everyone, form a favorite explanation early and then collect evidence that supports it. Confirmation bias is the default.

His method, ACH, forces you to fight it:

1. **List all plausible hypotheses,** including unlikely ones and the "boring" one. At least three.
2. **List the evidence and assumptions** relevant to the question.
3. **Build a matrix:** hypotheses across the top, evidence down the side. For each cell, ask: is this evidence consistent, inconsistent, or neutral relative to this hypothesis?
4. **Focus on diagnostic evidence:** the items that distinguish between hypotheses. Evidence that fits all of them (like "the suspect was in the city") tells you nothing.
5. **Work to disprove, not to prove.** The hypothesis with the *least* inconsistent evidence is more likely correct than the one with the most supporting evidence. A hypothesis is rarely killed by supportive evidence piling up; it is killed by one solid contradiction.
6. **Check sensitivity:** Which items of evidence is the conclusion most dependent on? What if one of those is wrong or planted?
7. **Report conclusions with confidence levels,** and list what new evidence would change your assessment.

**Everyday uses:** Why did the deal fall through? Is this person trustworthy? What is causing this symptom? Is this investment sound? What is actually going on with this team? The same discipline in every case: more than one explanation, evidence that discriminates, and a hunt for disconfirmation.

## Structured Techniques for Fighting Your Own Mind

- **Key assumptions check:** List what you're taking for granted. Rank by how critical and how uncertain. Verify the load-bearing ones.
- **Devil's advocate / red team:** Someone's job is to argue the opposite, or to think like the adversary. Red-teaming your plan from the opponent's seat reveals vulnerabilities. See [warfare.md](warfare.md).
- **Pre-mortem:** Assume the plan failed; explain why. See [decision-journal.md](decision-journal.md).
- **"What would have to be true?"** For a claim you doubt, ask what the world would look like if it were true, then check those implications.
- **Outside view / base rates:** Before the details of your case, ask how cases like this typically turn out. See [mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md](mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md).
- **Separate facts, inferences, and assumptions.** Label each. Most "facts" in conversation are inferences.
- **Estimative language:** Use calibrated words and numbers. "Likely" should mean something specific to you and your readers (the US intelligence standard, ICD 203, defines it as 55–80%), not a mood.
- **Chronology and link analysis:** Lay events in order; map who is connected to whom and how. Patterns that are invisible in prose appear on a timeline or diagram.

## Open-Source Research Basics

Public information is vast and underused. Typical sources for personal and professional diligence:

- **Public records and filings:** company registrations, court dockets, property records, regulatory filings, patents, licenses (varies by jurisdiction).
- **Professional footprint:** official bios, conference talks, publications, past employers, public interviews.
- **Archives:** web archives for deleted or changed pages.
- **Reviews and complaints:** pattern across many, not one.
- **Direct references:** talk to people who have worked with the person or organization, especially people the subject didn't nominate.
- **Search craft:** use quotation marks, site: filters, date ranges, and different spellings; search the name plus "lawsuit," "complaint," "fraud," and "review."

**Practical diligence checklists:**
- *Before a business partnership or investment:* verify identity and registration, check litigation and regulatory history, speak to former partners and customers, check claims against independent evidence, understand how they make money.
- *Before a major hire:* structured interviews, work samples, and reference checks beyond the provided list.
- *Before a high-stakes purchase or contract:* read the actual terms; research the counterparty's history of disputes.
- *Before sharing a startling claim:* verify through lateral reading first. See [epistemic-self-defense.md](epistemic-self-defense.md).

**Limits:** Don't misuse personal data, stalk, harass, deceive to obtain information, or intrude on private life. Aggregation of individually public facts can become surveillance. Use proportion and purpose: research to make a decision, not to dominate or exploit a person.

## Reading People as Sources

People are also sources, and the signals of reliability apply.

- **Baseline first.** You can't spot deviation without knowing normal behavior. Observe before judging.
- **Look for clusters, not single cues.** There is no reliable "tell" for lying. Research finds that human lie detection by cues like eye contact is near chance (meta-analyses by Bond and DePaulo put accuracy around 54%). Better signals: inconsistency over time, missing detail where detail should be, evasion of specific questions, and facts that fail verification.
- **Ask for specifics and check them.** Truthful accounts generally survive unexpected follow-up questions better than rehearsed stories; ask for sequence, sensory detail, and things that are checkable.
- **Verify with behavior and third parties** rather than judging by charm. See [dark-psychology.md](dark-psychology.md): high-charm, low-consistency is a flag.
- **Note incentives** and ask who benefits from the story.

See [tactics/social.md](tactics/social.md) for reading people more broadly.

## Counter-Intelligence: Protecting What You Know

Intelligence has a defensive side.

- **Operational security in daily life:** Don't reveal plans, finances, schedules, or vulnerabilities to people who don't need them. "Conceal your intentions" is Greene's Law 3 for a reason ([power-dynamics.md](power-dynamics.md)).
- **Assume what you post is permanent and searchable.** Review your own digital footprint as an adversary would.
- **Limit single points of compromise:** unique passwords, two-factor authentication, compartmentalized accounts. See [technology-leverage.md](technology-leverage.md).
- **Recognize elicitation.** Skilled questioners extract information through flattery, false confidences, and casual-seeming questions. Practice pausing before sharing and asking, "Why do they want to know?" See [counter-strategies.md](counter-strategies.md).
- **Verify identity through independent channels** before acting on unusual requests (social engineering defense).

## Common Analytic Traps

- **Confirmation bias:** seeking and weighting what supports your view.
- **Anchoring:** over-weighting the first number or story.
- **Mirror imaging:** assuming others think and want what you would.
- **Availability bias:** judging by what's vivid or recent.
- **Groupthink:** consensus replacing analysis.
- **Overconfidence from more information:** more data raises confidence faster than accuracy. Beyond a point, extra detail doesn't improve the call (a classic finding in judgment research).
- **Premature closure:** stopping at the first plausible story.
- **Seeing patterns in noise:** humans over-detect conspiracy and design.
- **Narrative fallacy:** preferring a coherent story to a messy truth.

Counter with the techniques above and with a record of your past calls ([decision-journal.md](decision-journal.md)).

## A Workflow for Any Important Question

1. **Write the decision and the question.** What do I need to know, by when?
2. **State your current belief and confidence.** Then try to disprove it.
3. **List competing hypotheses.**
4. **Collect from independent sources;** rate source and information separately.
5. **Look for diagnostic evidence,** and especially the evidence that could kill your favorite.
6. **Run a pre-mortem or red team** on the leading conclusion.
7. **State the conclusion with a probability** and what would change it.
8. **Decide and record.** Set a review date.
