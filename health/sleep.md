# Sleep

Sleep is the highest-leverage health intervention available. Every other protocol in this playbook — training, nutrition, cognition, emotional regulation — is downstream of sleep quality. Sacrifice it last, not first.

---

## Why It's the Priority

During sleep, the brain clears metabolic waste via the glymphatic system (the same waste that accumulates in Alzheimer's pathology). Growth hormone is released in its largest daily pulse. Memory consolidates. Emotional processing occurs. Immune function runs maintenance. Muscle protein synthesis peaks.

There is no biohack, supplement, or protocol that compensates for chronic sleep deprivation. Everything else depends on this.

---

## Circadian Biology

Understanding the mechanism makes the rules obvious rather than arbitrary.

**The two-process model of sleep:**

1. **Circadian rhythm** — a biological clock primarily governed by light exposure, running on approximately a 24-hour cycle. It signals the body when it is biologically day and night, regardless of behavior.

2. **Sleep pressure (adenosine)** — adenosine is a chemical byproduct of wakefulness that accumulates continuously while you're awake. The longer you've been awake, the higher the sleep pressure. Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors — it doesn't remove the adenosine, it just prevents you from sensing it. The adenosine is still accumulating.

**Sleep occurs at the intersection of these two processes:** when sleep pressure is high and the circadian rhythm signals night-time. This is why consistent wake time is the single most important sleep lever — it anchors the circadian rhythm, which determines when the system is ready to initiate sleep.

**Chronotype:** Morning types (larks) have circadian rhythms set slightly early. Evening types (owls) run slightly late. Chronotype is partly genetic. The prescription is not to fight your chronotype but to work with it — scheduling your high-cognitive-demand work during your natural peak, and not expecting 6am productivity from an 11pm circadian rhythm.

**Light as the primary zeitgeber (time-giver):**
- Morning bright light exposure (10–15 minutes outside within the first hour of waking, or 1000+ lux from a light therapy box) is the strongest signal for anchoring the circadian rhythm to the desired wake time
- Evening light of any wavelength suppresses melatonin — it is not only blue light but total lux that matters
- The practical rule: maximize bright light exposure in the morning; minimize all light (not just blue light) in the 2 hours before bed

## Sleep Architecture

Sleep is not uniform. It cycles through stages, each with distinct biological functions.

**The 90-minute sleep cycle:**

Sleep runs in approximately 90-minute cycles. A full night contains 4–6 cycles. The composition of each cycle changes across the night.

| Stage | Type | Function | What disrupts it |
|-------|------|----------|-----------------|
| **NREM 1** | Light | Transition; falling asleep | Any stimulus |
| **NREM 2** | Light | Motor learning; procedural memory | Fragmentation |
| **NREM 3** (Slow-Wave/Deep) | Deep | Physical restoration; growth hormone; memory consolidation; immune function | Alcohol (even moderate); inconsistent schedule |
| **REM** | Rapid Eye Movement | Emotional processing; creative association; learning consolidation | Alcohol; sleep deprivation |

**The first third of the night** is dominated by slow-wave (NREM 3) sleep. This is where physical restoration and growth hormone release occur. Missing the early hours of sleep costs disproportionately in physical recovery.

**The last third of the night** is dominated by REM sleep. This is where emotional processing and creative cognition occur. Cutting sleep short by even 90 minutes eliminates a disproportionate amount of REM, impairing mood, creativity, and learning consolidation.

**Alcohol and sleep architecture:** Alcohol sedates — it is not the same as sleep. Alcohol suppresses REM sleep significantly and fragments slow-wave sleep in the second half of the night. The "I sleep better with a drink" effect is the alcohol's sedative effect on sleep onset, not improvement in sleep quality. The recovery cost runs all night.

## Sleep Debt

Sleep debt accumulates with each night of insufficient sleep. Contrary to popular belief:

- **You cannot fully repay sleep debt on weekends.** The cognitive performance deficits from chronic restriction do not fully recover with two days of extended sleep. The damage is real.
- **Social jet lag** — the pattern of sleeping 6 hours Monday through Friday and 9 hours on weekends — is one of the most metabolically harmful sleep patterns, equivalent to regular transatlantic travel.
- **One bad night is not a crisis.** The recovery protocol after a single poor night: no alarm for the next night if possible, keep wake time only slightly later than normal (more than 90 minutes later and you disrupt the next night's sleep), avoid napping past 3pm (napping reduces sleep pressure for the night).

The goal is not to manage sleep debt — it is to prevent its accumulation through consistent structure.

---

## The Rules

1. **Consistent wake time, every day.** The most important rule. Anchor the circadian rhythm here; sleep onset will follow. Weekend "sleep-ins" should not exceed 60–90 minutes past weekday wake time.
2. **No screens 30–60 minutes before bed.** The issue is both the light (melatonin suppression) and the cognitive arousal of the content. This is most important on the hard nights when it matters most.
3. **Bedroom: cold (65–68°F / 18–20°C), dark, and quiet.** Temperature is mechanically linked to sleep onset — the body needs to drop core temperature by 1–2°F to initiate sleep. A cool room accelerates this process.
4. **No caffeine after 1–2pm.** Half-life is 5–7 hours. A coffee at 3pm is still at 50% concentration at 9pm.
5. **No alcohol within 3 hours of sleep.** It fragments sleep architecture even when it appears to help with sleep onset.

## The 60-Minute Wind-Down Protocol

Sleep is not a switch. It is a landing approach. The protocol creates the conditions for descent.

**60 minutes before bed:**
- Dim all overhead lights; use lamps at eye level or below
- Set thermostat to target sleep temperature
- No new cognitively demanding work — complete any open loops in writing

**30 minutes before bed:**
- Write tomorrow's top 3 priorities (offload the mental queue; the brain generates intrusive thoughts about unfinished tasks)
- No screens
- If still activated, try 4-7-8 breathing: inhale 4 counts, hold 7 counts, exhale 8 counts — this activates the parasympathetic system

**15 minutes before bed:**
- Magnesium glycinate (300–400mg) — magnesium is involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions; 68% of Americans are deficient; glycinate form is best absorbed and has the most sleep-relevant effect (muscle relaxation, GABA activation)
- Dim light only; candle-level lux is ideal
- Read fiction or something with no stakes — the narrative state is cognitively engaging but not arousing

**In bed:**
- Body scan relaxation (progressive muscle relaxation) or box breathing for acute anxiety
- If lying awake for 20+ minutes, get out of bed — do not associate the bed with wakefulness

## Optimization Lever Ranking

Most sleep optimization content focuses on the marginal levers. Here is the actual hierarchy by impact:

| Tier | Lever | Impact |
|------|-------|--------|
| **Critical** | Consistent wake time | Highest |
| **Critical** | Morning light exposure | High |
| **Critical** | Cool bedroom temperature | High |
| **High** | No alcohol near bedtime | High |
| **High** | 60-minute wind-down protocol | Medium-High |
| **Medium** | Magnesium glycinate | Medium |
| **Low** | Specific apps and tracking devices | Low |
| **Low** | Sleep supplements (except Mg) | Low-Medium |

Fix the critical tier before spending time or money on anything below it.

## Travel and Jet Lag Protocol

**Eastward travel** (advancing the clock) is harder than westward travel (delaying it). The circadian rhythm resists being moved earlier.

**Re-anchoring protocol:**
1. Upon arrival, force yourself onto local time immediately — do not nap on arrival if it's day at the destination
2. Get bright light exposure at the new wake time the first morning, even if exhausted
3. Use low-dose melatonin (0.5–1mg, not the standard 5–10mg) 1–2 hours before the new target bedtime for 2–3 days — this is a circadian signal, not a sedative; high doses don't work better
4. Avoid caffeine after 1pm local time from day one

**Eastward travel:** shift to local time as fast as possible; use morning light aggressively; accept 2–4 days of suboptimal sleep.

**Westward travel:** easier; push bedtime later; bright light in the evening helps delay the clock.
