# Nutrition

Food is the fuel and substrate for everything. The brain, muscle, hormones, immune function, gut microbiome, and mood all run on what you put in. Nutrition is not complicated — it is made complicated by the supplement industry, diet culture, and conflicting research that is mostly noise around a stable signal.

> **Evidence key.** `[E: strong]` — consistent RCTs or meta-analyses. `[E: moderate]` — good observational data or small/short trials. `[E: weak]` — mechanism, animal data, or expert opinion only. `[E: contested]` — serious evidence on both sides. Untagged = practice or preference, not an empirical claim.

---

## Principles

1. **Protein first.** `[E: strong]` Target 0.7–1g per pound of bodyweight daily. This is the single highest-leverage nutritional intervention for body composition, satiety, and metabolic health. Everything else is secondary.
2. **Eat food, mostly real, not too much.** Minimize ultra-processed food — not for moral reasons, but because it is engineered to defeat satiety signals and drive overconsumption.
3. **Don't outsource your defaults.** Know what you eat most days. Leave flexibility for everything else.
4. **Hunger is not an emergency.** Learn to distinguish genuine hunger from boredom, stress, or habit.

## Metabolic Health: The Master Variable

Insulin sensitivity is the single most important metabolic variable. When cells respond efficiently to insulin, glucose is used effectively for energy. When insulin sensitivity is impaired (insulin resistance), glucose accumulates in the blood, fat storage increases, inflammation rises, and the conditions for metabolic disease develop.

Most chronic disease in the developed world — Type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, certain cancers — shares impaired metabolic health as a root cause. Nutritional choices are the primary lever on this variable.

**What improves insulin sensitivity:**
- Resistance training (muscles are the largest consumer of glucose; building them improves clearance)
- Zone 2 cardio
- Minimizing ultra-processed carbohydrates and sugar — particularly liquid calories, which bypass satiety mechanisms
- High-fiber foods (slow glucose absorption)
- Adequate sleep (one night of poor sleep measurably impairs insulin sensitivity)

**The inflammatory pathway:** `[E: moderate]` Chronic low-grade inflammation is one common mechanism through which poor nutrition damages multiple systems — cardiovascular, neurological, immune, hormonal. The most consistently implicated factors: refined carbohydrates, ultra-processed food, and excess added sugar. Seed oils are `[E: contested]` — see the fat section. The most anti-inflammatory: fatty fish, olive oil, colorful vegetables, fermented foods, and fiber. The diet that reduces inflammation is not exotic. It is the diet closest to what food was before the 20th century.

## Protein: The Non-Negotiable

**The target:** 0.7–1g per pound of bodyweight (1.6–2.2g per kilogram). `[E: strong]` Gains in lean mass plateau around 1.6 g/kg on average (Morton et al., 2018 meta-analysis); the upper end is insurance. On the higher end if actively training; on the lower end if sedentary.

**Why it matters:**
- Muscle protein synthesis (building and maintaining muscle) requires adequate dietary protein — the body cannot manufacture it from other macronutrients
- Protein is the most satiating macronutrient — higher protein meals produce longer-lasting fullness
- Protein has the highest thermic effect of food — approximately 25–30% of the calories from protein are burned in digestion itself, compared to 5–10% for carbohydrates and 0–3% for fat

**Protein timing and distribution:** `[E: moderate]` Muscle protein synthesis is maximized by distributing protein intake across 3–4 meals of 30–50g each, rather than concentrating it in one large serving. The leucine threshold: each protein serving needs approximately 2–3g of leucine (the primary anabolic amino acid) to maximally trigger muscle protein synthesis. Whey protein and eggs are particularly leucine-dense; plant proteins generally need higher serving sizes to meet the threshold. Note: a 2023 trial (Trommelen et al.) found a single 100g dose still drove a large, prolonged anabolic response, so total daily protein matters more than precise distribution.

The post-workout anabolic window is real but wider than commonly believed — within 2 hours before or after training is sufficient; precision beyond this produces minimal additional benefit.

**Quality sources:** Eggs, meat, fish, poultry, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, legumes (incomplete protein, pair with grains for full amino acid profile). Protein powder (whey or casein for omnivores, pea protein for plant-based) as a convenient supplement when whole food sources are inconvenient.

## Carbohydrates: Timing and Quality

Carbohydrates are not the enemy. Their quality, quantity, and timing determine their effect.

**Quality:** The difference between a sweet potato and white bread is not just calories — it is fiber content, glycemic index, micronutrient density, and the degree of processing. Whole, minimally processed carbohydrates (vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fruit) provide fiber, vitamins, minerals, and polyphenols. Ultra-processed carbohydrates (crackers, cereals, bread from refined flour) deliver energy with minimal nutritional return and maximum satiety disruption.

**Quantity:** Total carbohydrate intake should be calibrated to activity level. Active people (training 5+ days/week) can use and benefit from more carbohydrate. Sedentary individuals have lower glycogen demands. Fat gain is driven by total energy surplus; direct conversion of carbohydrate to fat is a minor pathway in humans `[E: strong]`. There is no universal carbohydrate prescription — it scales with energy expenditure.

**Timing — the training window:** `[E: weak]` Carbohydrates consumed around training (before or after) are partitioned toward glycogen replenishment and away from fat storage. A carbohydrate-containing meal within 1–2 hours after resistance training is the most metabolically favorable timing, particularly for muscle growth and recovery.

**The food reward trap:** Ultra-processed carbohydrates (chips, cookies, crackers, cereals) are specifically engineered to maximize food reward without triggering satiety — the optimal ratio of fat, sugar, salt, and refined carbohydrate. They do not satisfy hunger; they temporarily suppress it while increasing desire for more. "Bet you can't eat just one" is not clever marketing. It is accurate physiology.

## Fat: Structure and Function

Dietary fat as a category is not the cause of cardiovascular disease. The low-fat era over-simplified the science, and sugar-industry funding shaped early research — but the core diet-heart studies were not retracted, and fat *type* still matters (see below). Fat is a structural component of cell membranes, the substrate for steroid hormones including testosterone and cortisol, the carrier for fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), and a primary energy source for many tissues.

**The omega-6 / omega-3 balance:** `[E: contested]` Pre-industrial humans consumed omega-6 to omega-3 fatty acids in approximately a 4:1 ratio. Modern Western diets run approximately 20:1. The imbalance is pro-inflammatory. The primary driver: ubiquitous refined seed oils (canola, corn, soybean, sunflower) in all processed food. Counter-evidence: controlled trials find linoleic acid (the main omega-6) does not raise inflammatory markers, and replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat lowers cardiovascular events (AHA 2017 advisory). The mainstream position is: raise omega-3, and don't fear omega-6 in whole-food amounts. The real problem with seed oils is that they ride along with ultra-processed food.

**Saturated fat:** `[E: contested]` The evidence is more nuanced than the 1980s guidelines suggested, but the direction holds: a Cochrane review (Hooper et al., 2020) found reducing saturated fat cut combined cardiovascular events by ~17%, with the benefit coming when it was replaced by polyunsaturated fat. Replacing saturated fat with refined carbohydrates (what actually happened during the low-fat era) increased cardiovascular disease rates. Context matters: saturated fat in whole food sources (beef, eggs, full-fat dairy) behaves differently than saturated fat in ultra-processed food. Red-line: `[E: strong]` trans fats are unambiguously harmful — avoid all hydrogenated oils.

**Practical fat strategy:** Prioritize olive oil `[E: strong]` (PREDIMED), fatty fish, avocado, eggs, nuts, and moderate amounts of quality animal fats. For high-heat cooking, avocado oil, refined olive oil, or ghee are stable choices. Coconut oil raises LDL more than other plant oils `[E: moderate]` — not a default.

## Time-Restricted Eating

**The circadian nutrition insight:** `[E: moderate]` The body's metabolic machinery is synchronized to the light-dark cycle. Metabolic enzymes, insulin sensitivity, and digestive efficiency all follow circadian rhythms — performing optimally in the morning and early afternoon, declining in the evening. Eating late at night is metabolically disadvantageous regardless of total calories.

**Time-restricted eating (TRE):** Confining food intake to a consistent 8–12 hour window, aligned with daylight hours. The evidence: Satchidananda Panda's research at the Salk Institute demonstrated that TRE in mice reversed obesity, diabetes, liver disease, and cardiovascular disease even without caloric restriction. Human results are weaker `[E: moderate]`: a 16:8 trial found no meaningful benefit over normal meal timing (Lowe et al., 2020), and with calories matched, TRE added nothing beyond calorie restriction itself (Liu et al., 2022, *NEJM*). Its main value is as a tool that makes eating less easier.

**Practical implementation:** 
- Eat within an 8–12 hour window (e.g., 8am–6pm or 9am–7pm)
- Stop eating 2–3 hours before sleep (see [sleep.md](sleep.md) — late eating disrupts sleep architecture)
- The fast begins when the last meal ends — 8–10 hours of overnight fasting is sufficient for most of the metabolic benefits

**Fasting protocols (more intensive):** 
- **16:8:** 16-hour fast, 8-hour eating window. The most accessible TRE format; most people can achieve this by delaying breakfast or advancing dinner
- **One-meal-a-day (OMAD):** Extreme compression; suitable for experienced practitioners; can impair protein distribution and muscle protein synthesis
- **5:2:** Eating normally 5 days/week; restricting to 500–600 calories 2 non-consecutive days. Produces metabolic benefits without daily restriction; manageable for most

**Who benefits most from TRE:** People with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, high triglycerides, or poor fasting glucose. Healthy, lean, active individuals see smaller benefits; muscle protein synthesis concerns become relevant at longer fasting durations.

## Daily Defaults

**Breakfast:** High protein, minimal sugar. Sets the satiety tone for the day. A breakfast dominated by refined carbohydrates and sugar produces a blood glucose spike followed by a crash and increased hunger within 2–3 hours. Eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, or a protein shake anchors the morning.

**Lunch:** Largest meal if possible — energy when it's most needed, digestion time before sleep.

**Dinner:** Lighter. `[E: weak]` Glucose tolerance is lower in the evening, and some trials show earlier-loaded eating helps weight loss modestly; the effect is small next to total intake.

**Snacks:** Only if genuinely hungry, not as habit. Hunger is not an emergency. If a meal 2–3 hours prior was adequate in protein and fiber, hunger between meals is usually signal to distinguish.

## Micronutrient Defaults

The following five nutrients are chronically deficient in modern populations and have evidence-based connections to performance, mood, and long-term health. Address them before optimizing anything else.

**Vitamin D:** `[E: moderate]` One of the most prevalent deficiencies in the developed world. Synthesized through sun exposure but most people — particularly in northern latitudes or desk-based jobs — don't get enough. Correcting deficiency matters for bone and immune health; in people who are already replete, large trials (VITAL) found no reduction in cancer or cardiovascular events. Supplement: 1,000–4,000 IU daily (4,000 IU is the standard upper limit without monitoring). The K2 pairing is `[E: weak]`. Test blood levels annually; 30–50 ng/mL is a defensible target (above 50 has no demonstrated benefit).

**Magnesium:** Involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions. About half of Americans eat less than the recommended intake `[E: moderate]` — low intake is not the same as clinical deficiency. Deficiency impairs sleep quality, muscle function, and stress response. The form-specific claims — glycinate for sleep, malate for energy, threonate for cognition — are `[E: weak]`. 300–400mg daily of glycinate is the practical default.

**Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA):** `[E: moderate]` The long-chain fatty acids found in fatty fish, not the ALA found in flaxseed (conversion from ALA to EPA/DHA is poor in humans). EPA and DHA are structural components of brain cell membranes and precursors to anti-inflammatory compounds. Most diets are severely omega-3 deficient relative to omega-6. Eating fatty fish 2x/week is the best-supported default; supplement trials for heart outcomes are mixed (VITAL null; REDUCE-IT positive with a prescription EPA). 1–2g combined EPA+DHA daily is a reasonable supplement dose if you don't eat fish. Refrigerate to prevent oxidation.

**Zinc:** Essential for testosterone production, immune function, and protein synthesis. `[E: weak]` Supplementing raises testosterone only if you are deficient; most omnivores who eat meat or shellfish are not. Found in meat and shellfish (oysters are the richest source). Supplementation: 15–30mg zinc as picolinate or citrate, taken with food to avoid nausea. Don't exceed 40mg without monitoring (high zinc impairs copper absorption).

**Creatine monohydrate:** `[E: strong]` Technically not a vitamin, but the most evidence-backed performance supplement in existence. Increases phosphocreatine stores in muscle, enhancing high-intensity output. `[E: moderate]` Increasingly studied for cognitive benefits — the brain uses phosphocreatine; supplementation improves working memory and processing speed, particularly under sleep deprivation or cognitive stress. 3–5g daily, any time. No loading phase required.

## The Gut-Brain Axis

The gut microbiome — the 100 trillion bacteria, fungi, and other organisms inhabiting the digestive system — communicates bidirectionally with the brain via the vagus nerve, immune signals, and metabolites. The existence of the axis is established; its effect on human mood and cognition is `[E: weak]` — most causal evidence is from animals. About 90% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut, but gut serotonin does not cross into the brain.

**Practical interventions:**
- **Eat 30 different plants per week.** `[E: moderate]` (observational, American Gut Project) The diversity of plant consumption is the strongest predictor of microbiome diversity. A varied microbiome is associated with better metabolic health, immune function, and mood. This sounds like a lot — it includes herbs, spices, legumes, nuts, seeds, vegetables, and fruits.
- **Fermented foods daily:** Yogurt (live cultures), kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, miso. `[E: moderate]` A small 2021 Stanford trial (n=36) found that a high-fermented-food diet increased microbiome diversity and decreased inflammatory markers better than a high-fiber diet alone.
- **Minimize antibiotic use where not medically necessary.** Antibiotics damage the microbiome indiscriminately. After necessary antibiotic courses, targeted probiotic supplementation and high-prebiotic food intake (garlic, onions, leeks, asparagus) support recovery.

## The Food Environment

You will eat what is in your home, regardless of intention. The kitchen environment determines default eating behavior more reliably than willpower.

**Design the food environment:**
- Don't buy food you don't want to eat. This is the highest-leverage nutritional intervention for most people — if it's not in the house, it takes active effort to consume it
- Visible, accessible healthy options: fruit on the counter, cut vegetables in the fridge at eye level, protein sources front and center
- Plates, not bags: portion distortion is real when eating directly from large containers
- Drink water first when you think you're hungry `[E: weak]`

See [reality-engineering.md](../reality-engineering.md) for the general framework; this is its application specifically to the kitchen.

## Alcohol: Clear Accounting

Alcohol is the most socially accepted metabolic disruptor. If you drink, know what it does:

- **Sleep architecture:** Even moderate alcohol consumption suppresses REM sleep, the most cognitively restorative sleep stage. "Better sleep after a drink" is sedation, not rest — sleep quality degrades even when subjective experience improves (see [sleep.md](sleep.md))
- **Muscle protein synthesis:** `[E: moderate]` Heavy drinking after training reduced MPS by roughly a quarter to a third over the following hours (Parr et al., 2014)
- **Liver priority:** The liver prioritizes alcohol metabolism above all other tasks — fat oxidation is halted during processing
- **Cognitive function:** Measurable impairment persists significantly longer than the subjective feeling of impairment
- **Testosterone:** Acutely suppressed for 12–24 hours after consumption; chronically suppressed with regular drinking

This is not prohibition. It is accounting. Make the trade consciously.

## Rules

- Track food for 2 weeks per year to recalibrate intuition. People's estimates of their caloric and protein intake are reliably inaccurate without periodic anchoring.
- Eat slowly enough to notice fullness — it lags behind actual intake `[E: moderate]`. Use this lag, don't fight it.
- Limit liquid calories: alcohol, juice, and sweetened coffee drinks are a reliable path to unintentional overconsumption. Liquids don't trigger the same satiety signaling as solid food.
- The 80/20 rule applied to nutrition: what you do consistently is more determinative than what you do occasionally. The diet on 90% of days is the diet; what you eat at parties and restaurants on the remaining 10% is irrelevant.
- Never eat standing in front of an open refrigerator. Sit down, use a plate, eat intentionally.
- Eat from a limited repertoire of default meals you can make without thinking, and save variety for occasions. Decision fatigue applied to food is real and causes deterioration to whatever is easy and available.
