# Health Intelligence

Your body is the only vehicle you get, and most people operate it with less data than they use to buy a phone. Health intelligence is the discipline of measuring the right things, interpreting them sensibly, and acting early. Prevention is cheap. Repair is expensive. Most serious conditions give years of warning to people who are looking.

**Read this first:** This is general education to make you a better-informed participant in your own care. It is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Reference ranges vary by lab, population, and guideline, and what's optimal depends on your history. Work with a qualified clinician, bring your data, and make decisions together. Never stop or start prescribed treatment based on a document like this.

---

## The Principle: Measure, Interpret, Act, Retest

1. **Measure** the markers that predict outcomes you care about.
2. **Interpret** against ranges *and* your own trend. A single value is a snapshot; a trend is a story.
3. **Act** on the biggest levers first: sleep, movement, nutrition, stress, substances. See [health/sleep.md](sleep.md), [health/fitness.md](fitness.md), [health/nutrition.md](nutrition.md).
4. **Retest** to see if it worked. Measurement without response is trivia.

The big determinants of lifespan and healthspan are the boring ones: cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle mass and strength, body composition, blood pressure, blood sugar regulation, lipid profile, not smoking, moderate-to-low alcohol, sleep, and social connection. See [longevity-protocols.md](../longevity-protocols.md). Intelligence means keeping those under watch.

## The Baseline: Annual Review With Your Clinician

At minimum, once a year (frequency and content depend on age, sex, family history, and risk):

- **Physical exam and history,** including family history of heart disease, cancer, diabetes, and early death. Family history changes screening.
- **Blood pressure** (see below).
- **Bloodwork** (panel below).
- **Age- and risk-appropriate screenings,** following current guidelines for your country and profile: for example colorectal cancer screening typically starting in the mid-forties, skin checks, and others depending on sex and history. Guidelines change; ask your clinician what applies to you.
- **Vaccinations,** dental care, eye exam, hearing as appropriate.
- **Mental health check.** Mood, anxiety, sleep, substance use. See [resilience.md](../resilience.md).

## Key Measurements

Ranges below are commonly cited reference points. Labs and guidelines differ. Use them for orientation and discuss your results with a clinician.

### Blood pressure
- Commonly classified (2017 ACC/AHA guideline): normal below 120/80 mmHg; elevated 120–129 systolic with diastolic below 80; hypertension stage 1 at 130–139 / 80–89; stage 2 at 140/90 or above.
- **Measure properly:** seated and rested for five minutes, arm supported at heart level, correct cuff size, several readings on different days. A home cuff gives a far better picture than occasional clinic readings, which can run high from anxiety.
- Sustained high blood pressure damages vessels, heart, brain, and kidneys silently for years. It is one of the highest-yield things to catch and treat.

### Blood sugar and insulin sensitivity
- **Fasting glucose:** commonly below 100 mg/dL normal; 100–125 prediabetes range; 126 or above in repeat testing suggests diabetes (ADA criteria).
- **HbA1c** (average over about three months): below 5.7% normal; 5.7–6.4% prediabetes range; 6.5% or above diabetes range.
- **Fasting insulin** is sometimes added to detect insulin resistance earlier; interpretation varies, so go through it with a clinician.
- Trend upward over years matters even inside "normal." Fitness, muscle, sleep, and a diet built on whole foods are the main levers.

### Lipids and cardiovascular risk
- **LDL cholesterol, HDL, triglycerides, total cholesterol:** the standard lipid panel.
- **ApoB** (the number of atherogenic particles) is increasingly regarded as a better risk marker than LDL-C alone, especially with high triglycerides, insulin resistance, or metabolic syndrome. Lower is generally better for risk; targets depend on your overall risk, so decide with a clinician.
- **Lipoprotein(a), Lp(a):** largely genetic, rarely tested, and a recognized independent risk factor. Many experts suggest measuring it at least once in a lifetime because it changes risk assessment and doesn't change much with lifestyle.
- **Coronary artery calcium (CAC) scan:** imaging that detects calcified plaque; sometimes used in middle age to refine risk when the decision to treat is uncertain. Ask a clinician whether it applies to you.
- **hsCRP (inflammation):** commonly, below 1 mg/L lower risk, 1–3 average, above 3 higher; acute illness distorts it, so don't test while sick.

### Other routine blood markers
- **Complete blood count and metabolic panel:** red and white cells, liver and kidney function, electrolytes.
- **Ferritin and iron studies:** low ferritin can cause fatigue; high can signal inflammation or iron overload.
- **Vitamin D (25-OH):** commonly, 30 ng/mL or above is considered sufficient by some bodies; others use 20. The Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline withdrew its 30 ng/mL threshold and advises against routine testing in healthy adults. Don't megadose without testing.
- **Thyroid (TSH, with free T4 if abnormal):** common cause of fatigue, weight, and mood changes.
- **Testosterone (men, with symptoms):** total and free, morning and fasting sample, ideally repeated. Low levels often reflect sleep loss, excess body fat, alcohol, or illness. Treat causes first and involve a clinician before any therapy. See [masculinity.md](../masculinity.md) and [sexuality.md](../sexuality.md).
- **B12 and folate,** particularly if you eat little animal food.
- **Liver enzymes (ALT/AST):** early signal of fatty liver, which is common and reversible.

### Body composition
- **Waist circumference** (and waist-to-height ratio) predicts metabolic risk better than weight alone. A waist under half your height is a common rule of thumb.
- **Muscle mass and grip strength:** strong predictors of healthy aging and survival. Grip strength is a cheap proxy test.
- Weight on a scale misses what matters: how much is muscle, how much is visceral fat.

### Fitness as a vital sign
- **VO2 max / cardiorespiratory fitness** is among the strongest predictors of mortality in large cohort studies, with the largest gains in risk going from the lowest fitness group to a moderate level. Estimated by wearables; measured in a lab test. See [longevity-protocols.md](../longevity-protocols.md).
- **Resting heart rate and heart rate recovery** give useful trends.
- Strength benchmarks: bodyweight movements, loaded carries, and getting up from the floor without hands.

### Wearables
Sleep duration, resting heart rate, and HRV (heart rate variability) from a good device are useful as **trends against your own baseline,** not absolutes and not to compare with other people. A sustained rise in resting heart rate or fall in HRV can flag illness, overtraining, stress, or poor sleep. Don't let tracking turn into anxiety. If a number makes you worse, stop looking at it daily.

## Injury Prevention

Injuries do more to derail long-term training than any other factor. Most are preventable with unglamorous habits.

**Principles:**
1. **Progress load gradually.** Muscle adapts faster than tendons, ligaments, and bone. Rapid jumps in volume or intensity are a main cause of overuse injury. A commonly cited "about 10% a week" is a rough guard, not a validated law: the underlying idea is to increase in steps your tissues can adapt to, and to take planned lighter weeks. See periodization in [health/fitness.md](fitness.md).
2. **Warm up specifically.** Raise temperature, then rehearse the movement with lighter loads before heavy work.
3. **Sleep.** Short sleep is associated with higher injury rates in athletes, including a study of adolescent athletes in which those sleeping under eight hours had substantially more injuries. Recovery is where adaptation occurs.
4. **Strength is protective.** Stronger muscles and tendons tolerate load. Strength training reduces sports injuries in controlled trials.
5. **Train through the range** and build control at end ranges, rather than only stretching passively.
6. **Technique and movement quality** under fatigue. Stop sets when form degrades.
7. **Manage total stress,** not only training load: poor sleep, work stress, and under-eating raise injury risk.
8. **Address asymmetries and old injuries** rather than training around them forever.

**Joint-by-joint thinking (Gray Cook and Mike Boyle):** A useful heuristic that some joints primarily need mobility (ankles, hips, thoracic spine) and others primarily need stability (knees, lumbar spine, scapula). When a mobility joint is stiff, the neighboring stability joint often compensates and gets irritated. For example, stiff hips and ankles can contribute to knee or low-back stress. It's a rule of thumb for investigation, not a diagnosis.

**Tendons:** Slow to adapt, slow to heal. Progressive loading, including slow heavy eccentric and isometric work, is a standard part of tendinopathy rehab. Complete rest often weakens the tendon. Get the problem assessed.

**Pain triage:**
- Muscle soreness (dull, diffuse, both sides, peaks at 24–48 hours, eases with movement) is normal.
- Sharp, localized, one-sided, joint-related, worsening, or night pain, numbness or tingling, swelling, or loss of function are not. Reduce load, don't push through, and get assessed by a clinician or physical therapist if it persists beyond a few days or is severe.
- Seek urgent care for suspected fractures, severe swelling, inability to bear weight, chest pain, or neurological symptoms.

**Mobility baseline:** Daily 10 minutes covering hips, thoracic spine, shoulders, and ankles; a deep squat hold; hanging from a bar; walking daily. Consistency beats intensity.

## Daily Behaviors That Move the Needle Most

These dominate almost everything above:

1. **Sleep** 7–9 hours at consistent times.
2. **Zone 2 cardio** several hours a week plus some higher-intensity work.
3. **Resistance training** 2–4 times a week, with progressive overload.
4. **Daily walking,** a high step count and breaking up long sitting.
5. **Whole-food, protein-adequate diet;** limit ultra-processed food, sugary drinks, and excess alcohol.
6. **Don't smoke or vape.** Smoking cessation is the single biggest intervention for those who smoke.
7. **Manage stress,** social connection, and purpose. Loneliness is a measurable health risk. See [community.md](../community.md), [friendship.md](../friendship.md).
8. **Sun, light, and time outdoors,** and sunscreen/skin checks for skin cancer.
9. **Dental and eye care.**

## Becoming an Informed Patient

- **Bring a one-page summary** to appointments: concerns, symptoms with dates, medications and supplements, family history, and your questions. Time is short.
- **Ask:** What are the options, including doing nothing? What are the benefits and risks? What are the numbers (absolute, not just relative risk)? What happens if I wait? What would you do in my position?
- **Understand absolute vs. relative risk.** "Doubles your risk" from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000 is different from 1 in 10 to 2 in 10. See [mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md](../mental-models/probabilistic-thinking.md).
- **Get a second opinion** for major diagnoses or procedures.
- **Keep your own records:** lab results, imaging reports, medications, vaccination history. You are the one constant across providers.
- **Be careful with consumer testing and supplements:** many direct-to-consumer panels generate incidental, unactionable findings; supplement quality is uneven; and "optimal" range claims often outrun the evidence. Pursue tests that could change a decision. Apply the source discipline in [intelligence-tradecraft.md](../intelligence-tradecraft.md) and [epistemic-self-defense.md](../epistemic-self-defense.md).
- **Never ignore persistent symptoms:** unexplained weight loss, blood in stool or urine, new lumps, persistent cough, chest pain, sudden vision or neurological changes, or a mole that changes. Early action is the whole advantage.
