# Deception and Active Measures

Deception is as old as war, and influence operations are as old as politics. What changed is cost and speed: a message that once needed a printing press, a radio network or an intelligence service can now be produced and distributed by almost anyone, to millions, in minutes.

This file is defensive. It explains how deception, propaganda and disinformation work, drawing on history, academic research and public-agency sources, so you can recognize them and reason well about what reaches you. It describes mechanisms. It is not a manual for running them, and it takes no side in current political fights. Every state, movement and company in this file has used these methods or been targeted by them.

The core claim: you cannot out-run a flood of falsehood with more fact-checking. You defend by controlling your own inputs, slowing down at the right moments, and knowing the patterns.

---

## Vocabulary: Get the Distinctions Right

Sloppy words produce sloppy thinking. Keep these apart:

- **Misinformation:** false or misleading content, shared without intent to deceive. Your uncle sharing a fake quote he believes is misinformation.
- **Disinformation:** false or misleading content created or spread deliberately to deceive. Intent is the difference, and intent is often hard to prove from outside.
- **Propaganda:** systematic communication designed to shape attitudes and behavior for the sender's ends. It can use true facts, selective facts or lies. Overt propaganda names its source; "black" propaganda hides or fakes it.
- **Influence operation:** a coordinated effort to shape the views or actions of a target audience. It can be overt (public diplomacy, advertising, lobbying) or covert (hidden sponsor, fake identities).
- **Covert vs. overt:** the question is whether the audience knows who is speaking and why. Concealed origin is the red flag, not persuasion itself. Persuasion is a normal part of public life.
- **Active measures (aktivnye meropriyatiya):** the Soviet term for covert and overt influence work: forgeries, front organizations, planted stories, agents of influence. Western agencies adopted the term for the Soviet toolkit.
- **Cheap fake:** real media edited cheaply (slowed, cropped, mislabeled, re-captioned). **Deepfake:** media synthesized or altered by machine learning.

Most harm comes from the boring end: real images with false captions, and true facts with false context.

## A Documented Case: The AIDS Story

In the 1980s, a story circulated worldwide that the US had created the AIDS virus as a weapon. Scholarship, including a 2009 article by Thomas Boghardt in *Studies in Intelligence* and later work by Douglas Selvage and Christopher Nehring using East German Stasi files, traces its promotion to a Soviet bloc campaign. The East German biophysicist Jakob Segal produced a pamphlet in 1986. The story then moved through a Soviet-linked newspaper in India and on into media in many countries, including mainstream Western outlets.

Lessons that generalize:

- **A real grievance or fear is the best carrier.** The story landed because it attached to existing distrust.
- **Laundering.** A claim passes through several outlets until the original source is invisible. By the time you see it, it appears to have come from "everywhere."
- **A credentialed messenger helps.** Scholars disagree on how much Segal knew about his role. The point is that the sponsor stayed hidden.
- **The claim outlived the operation.** It still circulates decades later, long after being rebutted. Operation names vary by source (the Stasi file name and the commonly used label differ), which is itself a reminder to check labels.

Parallel examples across states and eras: British propaganda in World War I, Nazi propaganda under Goebbels, US Cold War programs, Chinese state influence work, and modern Russian efforts. Commercial equivalents are real too: the tobacco industry's decades of manufactured doubt about smoking science is well documented in internal industry papers.

## Military Deception Doctrine

The study of deception as a craft is largely the work of Barton Whaley, who compiled a large historical database of surprise and deception and wrote *Stratagem: Deception and Surprise in War*. Recurring findings:

**1. Deception works by exploiting what the target already expects.** A commonly cited rule, often called Magruder's Principle in US military deception literature, says it is easier to get an adversary to keep a belief he already holds than to make him change it. The idea appears in the Whaley-era deception research and in US Army doctrine, but the exact origin of the name is unclear, so treat it as a convention.

**2. Deception serves surprise.** It is aimed at an action: make the target do something, or fail to do something, not merely believe something.

**3. Deception needs a channel the target trusts.** Intelligence the target collected itself, or got by apparent accident, is believed far more than anything handed over.

**4. Deception needs feedback.** The deceiver must see whether the story is taking.

**Operation Fortitude (1944).** The Allies built a fictional army group, aimed at the Pas de Calais, to hold German reserves away from Normandy. It combined dummy equipment, false radio traffic, and double agents controlled through the British Double Cross system. It succeeded because it fit what German planners already expected: the shortest crossing was the obvious one. For Operation Mincemeat and Midway, see [warfare.md](warfare.md).

**Maskirovka.** The Soviet term for a broad doctrine of concealment, deception and misdirection, applied at operational and strategic levels. Soviet planning for the 1944 summer offensive in Belarus (Operation Bagration) is widely described as using deception to suggest the main blow would fall elsewhere. Details in popular accounts vary, so go to historians for specifics.

**Reflexive control.** A Russian theoretical concept, associated with the work of Vladimir Lefebvre and later Russian military writers, about feeding an adversary information that leads him to choose what you want. Western analysts debate how much it explains actual practice. Related caution: the "Gerasimov Doctrine" is a Western label for a 2013 article by Russia's chief of the general staff. The analyst who coined the phrase, Mark Galeotti, later disowned it, saying it was not a doctrine. Be wary of any single neat theory that explains an opponent's behavior.

**The defender's lesson.** If the deceiver exploits your expectations, your defense is to name your expectations. Ask what you are assuming, and what evidence would look like if you were wrong. This is the logic of Analysis of Competing Hypotheses in [intelligence-tradecraft.md](intelligence-tradecraft.md).

## The Modern Information Environment

The mechanics, stripped to what matters for defense:

**Amplification.** Platforms rank by engagement. Content that provokes outrage, fear or tribal feeling travels farther, regardless of origin or accuracy. A small coordinated group, or a few influential accounts, can start a cascade that real people then carry.

**Manufactured consensus.** Humans use "everyone seems to think this" as evidence. Coordinated or fake accounts, bought engagement, and astroturfing (campaigns that look grassroots but are organized and funded from elsewhere) fake the signal. Real people can also be mobilized in coordinated ways. Large numbers prove attention, not truth.

**The firehose.** RAND's label (Paul and Matthews, 2016) for a high-volume, multichannel, repetitive style of propaganda that does not care about consistency or truth. Its aim is often fatigue and cynicism: the audience concludes nothing can be known. Do not take that bait.

**Flooding the zone.** A related popular phrase for burying a story under a high volume of competing claims and noise. The defense is selection: choose a few high-quality sources and ignore the volume.

**The liar's dividend.** Legal scholars Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron named the effect: as fakes become possible, real evidence can be dismissed as fake. Deepfakes cause harm twice: by fooling people, and by giving cover to anyone caught on real tape.

**Identity and emotion hooks.** Content built to say "your group is under attack" or "they despise you" bypasses evaluation. If a post makes you feel certain and angry in under five seconds, you have been targeted by design, whether by a state, a marketer or an engagement-seeker.

**Cheap fakes outperform deepfakes.** The most common deception is a real clip with the wrong date, place or caption. Check context first.

## Why It Works: The Human Side

Deception is not powerful because you are stupid. It is powerful because it uses normal mental shortcuts. See [cognitive-biases.md](cognitive-biases.md).

- **Illusory truth effect:** repetition raises perceived truth. Hasher, Goldstein and Toppino (1977) first showed that repeated statements are rated truer. Later work suggests it can occur even when people know better. You do not need to believe a claim to be moved by hearing it fifty times.
- **Confirmation bias:** claims that fit your view get waved through; claims that don't get scrutinized. Deceivers target your side's wish.
- **Source confusion:** you remember the claim, forget where it came from, and the memory loses its "unreliable source" tag.
- **Social proof and tribal identity:** beliefs are membership badges. Doubting one costs social standing. See [epistemic-self-defense.md](epistemic-self-defense.md).
- **Affect heuristic:** strong feeling reads as strong evidence.

## What Helps and What Doesn't

Be honest about the evidence. Nothing here is a cure.

**What the research supports (with modest, real effects):**
- **Prebunking / inoculation.** McGuire (1961) proposed that exposing people to a weakened form of a persuasion attack builds resistance. Van der Linden and colleagues have tested it against misinformation techniques (emotional language, false dichotomies, fake experts, scapegoating) with games and short videos. Effects are real but modest, and they fade without reinforcement.
- **Lateral reading.** Stanford researchers (Wineburg and colleagues) found that professional fact-checkers left a site to see what others said about it, and did better than historians and students who read deeply on the page. See [intelligence-tradecraft.md](intelligence-tradecraft.md).
- **Accuracy prompts.** Pennycook, Rand and colleagues found that simply nudging people to think about accuracy before sharing improved the quality of what they shared. Effects are small and some replications show smaller ones. Treat it as a cheap habit, not a shield.
- **Friction before sharing.** Waiting, or reading before reposting, reduces spread. It costs nothing.
- **Source diversification.** Several sources with different incentives and different failure modes beat one trusted channel.

**What works less well:**
- **Fact-checks alone.** A lie is cheap and fast; the correction is slow and reaches a smaller audience. The fact-check still matters for the record, but it will not win the race.
- **Debunking by repetition.** Restating the myth in order to refute it can reinforce familiarity. Lead with the truth and mark the false claim clearly.
- **The "backfire effect."** The fear that corrections make beliefs stronger was popular, but later work (Wood and Porter, 2019) found it rare. Corrections usually help a little. Don't use backfire as an excuse to stop correcting people.
- **Deepfake detection by eye.** See below.

## The Personal Defense Protocol

Use this when a claim is surprising, emotional, or demands action.

1. **Pause on strong emotion.** Anger, fear, vindication and disgust are the alert. Do nothing for a few minutes. If it matters, wait 24 hours.
2. **Name the claim.** What exactly is being asserted? Separate fact, inference and opinion.
3. **Check the source and its incentives.** Who is speaking, who pays them, what do they gain if you believe this? An unfamiliar site gets lateral reading before anything else.
4. **Find the original.** Trace to the document, video, dataset, transcript or full quote. Be suspicious of screenshots, cropped clips and "sources say."
5. **Check independent corroboration.** Independent means not derived from each other. Ten posts citing one claim are one claim.
6. **Check date, place and context.** Is the image from this event?
7. **Ask who benefits.** Not as proof of falsehood (true things also benefit people) but as a prompt to look harder.
8. **Identify what's missing.** What would you expect to see if this were true that you do not see? What is the strongest alternative explanation?
9. **Set your confidence.** Use a rough probability, not yes or no. Say "unverified" when it is.
10. **Delay or decline sharing.** If you cannot verify, do not amplify. Silence is a valid move.

## Images, Video and Audio

- **Provenance first.** Where did this first appear, who posted it, can you find the earliest version? Content with a traceable chain of custody beats content with none.
- **Reverse image search.** Use more than one engine. Old photos of different events are routinely recycled.
- **Context mismatch.** Weather, signage, language, clothing, shadows, vehicle types and license plates can contradict a caption.
- **Look for the full clip.** Edited clips remove what came before and after.
- **Find corroborating footage.** Real events usually generate multiple angles and independent reporting.
- **Limits of "looks fake."** Visual artifacts (odd hands, unnatural blinking, lip-sync) are unreliable and the tells change as the tools improve. Automated detectors are also unreliable and vary in accuracy, particularly on new generators or compressed uploads. Absence of visible artifacts proves nothing, and neither does a detector's verdict.
- **Provenance standards.** Industry efforts such as content-credential metadata (the C2PA standard) aim to record how media was made. Useful where present, but absence of credentials does not mean something is fake, and metadata can be stripped.
- **For high-stakes decisions,** verify through an independent channel. If you get a voice message from a relative asking for money, call them back on a number you already have. See [intelligence-tradecraft.md](intelligence-tradecraft.md).

## Deception in Everyday Life

The same patterns operate at human scale:

- **Con artists.** Scams build trust, create urgency, isolate the victim from advisors, and lean on authority or a once-in-a-lifetime offer. The defense is time and a second opinion. See [dark-psychology.md](dark-psychology.md).
- **Sales manipulation.** Artificial scarcity, fake deadlines, anchoring, and social proof. See [sales.md](sales.md).
- **Workplace spin.** Selective metrics, euphemism, credit-claiming, and narrative control. Ask for the underlying numbers and the baseline.
- **Negotiation bluffs.** A claimed alternative, a claimed deadline, a claimed limit. Test them quietly, and know your own alternative first. See [negotiation.md](negotiation.md).
- **Relationship deception.** Inconsistency over time is more telling than any "tell." Lie detection from body language is near chance (about 54% in Bond and DePaulo's meta-analysis). Check facts and track patterns.

## The Ethics of Your Own Misdirection

You will sometimes need to conceal. Not every concealment is deception, and not every deception is dishonorable. But the default should be honesty, for a practical reason: trust is a long-game asset, and it is expensive to rebuild.

**Legitimate concealment:**
- Privacy about your finances, health, plans, and family.
- Confidentiality you have promised.
- A negotiating position you simply do not disclose.
- Safety: withholding information from someone who means harm.

**Illegitimate deception:**
- Creating false beliefs that people then rely on to their cost.
- Fraud, fake identities used to mislead, and manufactured consensus.
- Lies to people who trust you and have a right to the truth.

**Tests:**
1. Would I be comfortable if the other person learned exactly what I did and why?
2. Does this harm someone who relied on me?
3. Could I sustain it, or does it require a growing web of further lies?
4. Does it make me the kind of person I respect?

Withholding is almost always available. Lying is rarely required. See [ethics.md](ethics.md) and [warrior-codes.md](warrior-codes.md).

## The Information Diet

Your inputs are your attack surface.

1. **Cut the feed.** Remove or limit algorithmic feeds. Prefer sources you choose over sources that choose for you.
2. **Prefer primary sources.** Read the filing, study, transcript or statement, at least for what matters.
3. **Schedule news.** One or two fixed windows a day. First reports are often wrong, and 24 to 72 hours later the picture is better. See [attention.md](attention.md).
4. **Mix incentives.** Use sources with different owners, funding and viewpoints, plus at least one that corrects its errors in public.
5. **Track your own errors.** Note claims you believed and later learned were false. Which source, what emotion, which bias?
6. **Reduce outrage intake** from every direction, including your own side.

## One-Page Checklist

- [ ] Am I having a strong emotional reaction? Pause.
- [ ] What exactly is claimed? Fact, inference or opinion?
- [ ] Who is the source, and what do they gain?
- [ ] Have I seen the original, not a screenshot or a summary?
- [ ] Is there independent corroboration (not derived from the same source)?
- [ ] Are the date, place and context right?
- [ ] What is missing? What is the best alternative explanation?
- [ ] Does it flatter my side or confirm what I already believed? Raise the bar.
- [ ] How confident am I, honestly, as a rough percentage?
- [ ] If unverified, can I simply not share it?

## Weekly Practice

1. **Pick one claim** you encountered this week that you believed or almost shared. Run the protocol on it after the fact.
2. **Trace one story** to its origin. Count how many "sources" were really one.
3. **Read one source** from outside your usual set, and write its strongest point.
4. **Log one error:** something you believed that was wrong, and how it reached you.
5. **Audit your feeds:** remove one source that mostly produces outrage.
6. **Review in writing,** and record the result in your [decision-journal.md](decision-journal.md).
